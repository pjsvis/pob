# Bibliography Playbook

## Purpose

Defines the bibliography discipline for all published tracks (book chapters, standalone essays, blog posts): the narrativised entry, the reference cap, the two-register structure for the book, and the research-vs-published distinction. Ensures the bibliography is a narrative instrument and an anti-yap gate, not a credentials display.

## When to use

- Before any editorial pass that touches references (read alongside `style-playbook.md`).
- When drafting a narrativised bibliography for an essay or chapter.
- When assembling the back-of-book expanded bibliography.
- When deciding whether a source earns its place in published text.

## The principle

The bibliography is a narrative instrument, not a credentials display. A bare citation list lets you drop a reference without stating what it does in the argument — the academic equivalent of the yap: voluminous, impressive, free of editorial commitment. Forcing each entry to explain *why it is here and what it contributes* makes the reference earn its place in the prose, the way the voice gate makes a paragraph earn its place. If you can't write the narrative sentence, the source isn't doing work — it's decoration, and it gets cut.

This is the Shannon natural-unit argument applied to citations: a citation you can investigate is a discrete, auditable unit; a citation wall is the image-with-no-solved-natural-unit case — present, impressive, not checkable.

## The narrativised entry

Every published reference carries a narrative sentence. The format (from `self-contained-essay-playbook.md`):

> Author (Year). *Title*. Publisher. One or more sentences explaining why this source is cited and what it contributes.

The narrative sentence is the anti-yap gate. If you cannot write it, the source is cut. No bare citations in published text.

## The cap

Few references, each investigable, is the point — and it inverts the usual instinct to pile references for authority.

- **Book chapter:** 5–8 narrativised references in the text.
- **Blog essay:** 2–4 narrativised references.
- **The research scratchpad is exempt.** Research is yap-scale by design; the cap applies at the selection stage, when research becomes published text. The voice pass is what cuts research to signal.

A bibliography of five entries a reader could find and read in an afternoon is stronger than thirty bare citations that say "trust the edifice." The narrativised style makes the cap enforceable: a bloated bibliography reveals itself immediately when every entry has to justify itself in prose.

## The two-register structure (book only)

The book carries two bibliographies, for two readers.

### 1. In-text / foundational bibliography

The few, narrativised, investigable references — the sources the argument genuinely uses. Each entry explains what the source contributes to the argument. This is the bibliography the narrative reader meets.

### 2. Back-of-book expanded bibliography

For the researcher who wants the wider field. The narrative here is different: **why each source is included or excluded from the text.** This is the editorial audit trail — the selection made visible and auditable, not just a longer list.

The back-of-book register is NOT the yap pile the in-text discipline prevents. The inclusion/exclusion narrative keeps it honest: every entry still carries a sentence, but the sentence says "this is in the field but not in the text, because…" or "this is in the text; here is the wider context it sits in." The narrative discipline applies to both registers; the content differs.

This split serves the applecart principle: the narrative reader's body stays in the room because the text isn't cluttered, and the researcher gets what they need at the back. Two readers, two registers, one voice.

## The research-vs-published distinction

Research priors (the scratchpad, the assets) can and should hold more references — the point of research is to find the field, and the field may be wide. The narrativisation and the cap apply at the selection stage, when research becomes published text.

The yap is research; the voice is authorship; never let the yap author — and the bibliography is voice, not yap. (See `blog/the-yap-and-the-voice.md`.)

## The voice pass

The narrativised entries are prose, and prose is voice. The bibliography passes through the same single-voice gate as the rest of the text, against the staved-finger benchmark. A bibliography entry in a different register from the surrounding text is a seam the reader hears.

## Checklist (before publication)

- [ ] Every in-text reference has a narrative sentence explaining its contribution. No bare citations.
- [ ] The reference count is within the cap (5–8 chapter, 2–4 essay), or every excess entry has justified its place.
- [ ] Each reference is investigable by a reader in an afternoon (findable, readable).
- [ ] The back-of-book expanded bibliography (book only) carries inclusion/exclusion narrative, not just a longer list.
- [ ] The bibliography passes the single-voice gate — the entries read in the same register as the text.
- [ ] The research scratchpad is not published as the bibliography; selection has happened.
