# The Myth of Transdermal Magnesium: A Comprehensive Toxicological and Pharmacokinetic Evaluation of Cutaneous Permeability and Systemic Bioavailability
## 1. Introduction: The Clinical and Commercial Duality of Magnesium
Magnesium, a divalent cation essential to mammalian physiology, occupies a paradoxical position in modern medicine. Biologically, it is a critical cofactor in over 300 enzymatic reactions, governing processes as fundamental as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) metabolism, DNA and RNA synthesis, neuromuscular transmission, and the regulation of vascular tone.[1, 2] Clinically, magnesium deficiency—often termed "the invisible deficiency"—is implicated in a spectrum of pathologies ranging from hypertension and type 2 diabetes to migraine, depression, and chronic fatigue.[2, 3] Yet, despite its established importance, the therapeutic delivery of magnesium remains a subject of intense debate, bifurcated into two distinct domains: the evidence-based realm of oral and intravenous administration, and the rapidly expanding, commercially driven sector of transdermal application.


The central proposition of the transdermal industry is seductive in its simplicity: that the skin, the body's largest organ, can serve as a highly efficient portal for magnesium delivery. Proponents argue that applying magnesium salts—typically magnesium chloride ($MgCl_2$) in the form of "oils" (brines), gels, or lotions, and magnesium sulfate ($MgSO_4$) in baths—bypasses the gastrointestinal tract, thereby circumventing the rate-limiting and often unpleasant side effects of oral ingestion, such as osmotic diarrhea.[2, 4, 5] Marketing narratives frequently claim "100% absorption," rapid cellular saturation, and the ability to "detoxify" the body through reverse osmosis.[1, 6]


However, these claims collide with the fundamental principles of dermatological science. The human integument is not a passive sponge designed to absorb environmental solutes; it is a sophisticated, evolutionarily refined barrier engineered to prevent the loss of internal fluids and the ingress of xenobiotics. The stratum corneum, the skin's outermost layer, presents a formidable hydrophobic shield to hydrophilic ions. Consequently, the proposition that transdermal magnesium absorption is effective for systemic repletion requires a rigorous evaluation of physicochemical laws, skin barrier physiology, and the quality of available clinical evidence.


This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the scientific literature regarding cutaneous magnesium permeability. By dissecting the physicochemical properties of the magnesium ion, analyzing the structural biology of the epidermal barrier, and critically appraising the disparate results of clinical trials—from unpublished pilot studies to rigorous randomized controlled trials—we aim to confirm or refute the efficacy of transdermal magnesium therapy. Furthermore, we will disentangle the genuine local dermatological benefits of magnesium balneotherapy from the unsupported claims of systemic bioavailability, offering a nuanced understanding of mechanisms such as follicular transport and the confounding sedative effects of bromides in Dead Sea salts.


## 2. Physicochemical Barriers to Percutaneous Ion Transport
To evaluate the plausibility of transdermal magnesium absorption, one must first examine the thermodynamic and kinetic hurdles imposed by the nature of the magnesium ion itself and the environment it must traverse.


### 2.1 The Physicochemical Paradox of the Magnesium Ion
The primary determinant of a molecule's ability to passively diffuse through the stratum corneum is its partition coefficient ($log P$), which describes its relative solubility in lipids versus water. The stratum corneum's intercellular matrix is a lipid-rich environment composed of ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids.[1] For a substance to permeate this layer via the intercellular route—the dominant pathway for most transdermal drugs—it must possess a degree of lipophilicity.


Magnesium, however, is an inorganic cation ($Mg^{2+}$). In its dehydrated state, it has a small ionic radius of approximately 72 picometers (pm) or 0.72 Ångstroms ($\AA$). Proponents of transdermal therapy often cite this small size as evidence of its penetrability. This argument, however, ignores the aqueous reality of biological systems. Magnesium has a high charge density, which creates a powerful electrostatic attraction to water molecules. In solution, magnesium ions do not exist in isolation; they are surrounded by a tightly bound primary and secondary hydration shell.


Research utilizing molecular modeling and experimental data indicates that the radius of the *hydrated* magnesium ion expands dramatically to approximately 400 pm (4–5 $\AA$), an increase of roughly 400 to 500 times its dehydrated volume.[1, 7] This hydration shell is energetically expensive to strip away. To pass through the lipid bilayer of the stratum corneum, the ion would theoretically need to shed its water molecules, a process requiring significant desolvation energy. The "born energy" required to move a charged ion from a high-dielectric medium (water) to a low-dielectric medium (lipid) is prohibitively high.[1]


Consequently, Fick's First Law of Diffusion ($J = \frac{D \cdot K \cdot \Delta C}{h}$) predicts negligible flux ($J$) for magnesium ions across intact skin. The partition coefficient ($K$) between the aqueous vehicle (the bath or spray) and the lipid membrane is virtually zero. While the concentration gradient ($\Delta C$) may be high—particularly with supersaturated "oils" or Dead Sea brines—the resistance of the barrier ($h$ and $K$) effectively nullifies the driving force of diffusion for the hydrated ion.


### 2.2 The Stratum Corneum: The Brick-and-Mortar Fortress
The anatomical structure of the skin further reinforces this physicochemical barrier. The stratum corneum is often described by the "brick and mortar" model. The "bricks" are corneocytes—terminally differentiated keratinocytes packed with cross-linked keratin filaments and enclosed in a cornified envelope. These cells are essentially proteinaceous blocks that offer high mechanical resistance but limited permeability.[1]


The "mortar" is the intercellular lipid lamellae. Unlike cell membranes, which are fluid phospholipid bilayers, the lipids of the stratum corneum are organized into highly ordered, crystalline gel-phase structures. This rigidity further restricts the diffusion of molecules, particularly those that are large or charged.[8] The tortuous path created by the overlapping corneocytes increases the effective diffusion path length significantly, requiring molecules to travel far distances to traverse a relatively thin tissue layer.


Given that the magnesium ion is both charged (hydrophilic) and, in its hydrated state, bulky, it is inherently incompatible with the lipid mortar and unable to pass through the protein bricks. This leaves the system dependent on alternative, non-corneal routes of entry, specifically the "shunt pathways" of skin appendages.


### 2.3 The Follicular Shunt Hypothesis
Recognizing the impermeability of the stratum corneum to ions, researchers have investigated whether skin appendages—hair follicles and sweat glands—could serve as "shunts" or shortcuts for magnesium absorption. This hypothesis posits that the epithelium lining the hair follicle is more permeable than the stratum corneum and extends deeper into the dermis, closer to the vascular bed.[2, 7]


Rigorous doctoral research from the University of Queensland (UQ) has provided the most detailed investigation into this mechanism. Using specific fluorescent magnesium indicators (Mag-fura-2) and multiphoton microscopy, researchers demonstrated that magnesium ions *can* permeate the skin, but this permeation is spatially confined. When hair follicles were experimentally blocked using a novel cyanoacrylate plugging method, magnesium permeation was significantly attenuated, confirming that the follicles are the primary route of entry for topically applied magnesium.[7, 9]


However, the physiological reality limits the systemic impact of this pathway. Hair follicles and sweat glands occupy only 0.1% to 1% of the total skin surface area.[1] While the flux per unit area *within* the follicle may be higher than the stratum corneum, the total available surface area is minute. Consequently, while follicular transport allows for *some* magnesium to enter the skin—potentially explaining local dermatological effects—the total mass transfer is likely insufficient to raise systemic serum levels significantly or correct a total body deficit. The "shunt" is simply too narrow to serve as a major arterial road for systemic nutrition.


## 3. Critical Appraisal of Evidence Supporting Transdermal Efficacy
The claim that transdermal magnesium is effective is sustained largely by a few frequently cited studies, many of which suffer from methodological limitations, lack of peer review, or industry sponsorship. A rigorous review requires dissecting these foundational texts to separate reproducible science from marketing literature.


### 3.1 The Waring Study (Unpublished): The Origin of the Myth
Perhaps the most cited document in the transdermal magnesium industry is an unpublished report by Dr. R.H. Waring from the University of Birmingham.[10] In this open-label trial, 19 subjects bathed in magnesium sulfate (Epsom salts) solutions for 12 minutes daily over 7 days.


The report claims that serum magnesium levels rose from a mean of 104.68 ± 20.76 ppm to 114.08 ± 25.83 ppm after the first bath, and to 140.98 ± 17.00 ppm after 7 days.[10] It also noted rises in urinary magnesium excretion, suggesting systemic absorption and subsequent renal clearance.


**Critical Analysis:**


- **Methodological Flaws:** The study lacks a control group bathing in tap water or sodium chloride. Magnesium levels exhibit circadian rhythmicity and are heavily influenced by dietary intake, which was not rigorously controlled. Without a placebo group, attributing the rise solely to the bath is scientifically invalid.
- **Statistical Ambiguity:** The standard deviations reported are large (± 25.83 ppm), indicating massive inter-individual variability.
- **Publication Status:** The fact that this study remains unpublished in a peer-reviewed journal decades later suggests it did not meet the rigorous standards required for scientific validation. It exists primarily as a PDF hosted on commercial websites, diminishing its evidentiary weight.

### 3.2 The Watkins and Josling Pilot Study
Another pillar of the transdermal argument is a pilot study by Watkins and Josling (2010), often cited by "magnesium oil" manufacturers.[4, 11] This study involved 9 patients applying transdermal magnesium spray and foot soaks for 12 weeks.


**Critical Analysis:**


- **Biomarker Validity:** The study measured "cellular magnesium" using hair mineral analysis. Hair analysis is widely regarded by toxicologists and clinical chemists as unreliable for assessing current nutritional status due to external contamination (from shampoos, dyes, and indeed, the magnesium spray itself) and the slow growth rate of hair, which reflects past rather than present status.[12]
- **Sample Size:** With only 9 participants and no control group, the claim of a "59.5% increase" in cellular magnesium is statistically meaningless and likely represents external contamination of the hair shaft rather than systemic uptake.

### 3.3 The Kass et al. (2017) Study: A Mixed Picture
A more rigorous attempt was made by Kass et al. in a single-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study involving 25 participants using a magnesium cream (56 mg/day) for two weeks.[12]


**Findings:**


- **Serum Levels:** The study reported a "clinically relevant" increase in serum magnesium (0.82 to 0.89 mmol/L) in the treatment group. However, this increase was *not* statistically significant for the group as a whole ($p=0.29$). Significance was only achieved in a *post-hoc* subgroup analysis of non-athletes ($p=0.02$).[12]
- **Urinary Excretion:** Crucially, there was no statistically significant increase in urinary magnesium excretion ($p=0.48$). Since the kidneys are the primary regulators of magnesium balance and excrete excess absorbed magnesium, the lack of urinary response strongly suggests that no significant systemic absorption occurred.
- **Interpretation:** The authors' conclusion of efficacy relies on a subgroup analysis, a practice often criticized as "data dredging." The disparity between serum and urine results undermines the hypothesis of significant systemic uptake.

### 3.4 The Shealy Study and "Intracellular" Testing
Dr. Norman Shealy has also promoted transdermal magnesium, citing a study where participants used magnesium oil and foot soaks for 4 weeks. He claimed marked improvements in "intracellular" magnesium levels using a diagnostic test called ExaTest (sublingual epithelial cell scraping).[13]


**Critical Analysis:**


- **Diagnostic Validity:** The ExaTest is not a standard clinical diagnostic tool. While it claims to measure intracellular electrolytes, the lack of widespread validation and the potential for contamination from oral magnesium residues (if the subject ingested anything) or simply the variability of epithelial turnover makes it a contentious biomarker. Like the hair analysis, this method is peripheral to standard serum and urine pharmacokinetics used in evidenced-based medicine.

## 4. Evidence Refuting Systemic Efficacy
In contrast to the pilot studies described above, larger and more robust investigations have consistently failed to demonstrate meaningful systemic effects of transdermal magnesium.


### 4.1 The Gröber Systematic Review
A systematic review published in *Nutrients* by Gröber et al. (2017) provides a definitive critical appraisal. After evaluating the available literature, the authors concluded that the propagation of transdermal magnesium is "scientifically unsupported".[1] They emphasized the physiological impossibility of significant ionic transport across the stratum corneum and characterized the transdermal movement as a "myth." They advised that oral supplementation remains the only evidence-based method for correcting deficiency.


### 4.2 Exercise Recovery and Performance Studies
The sports medicine community has rigorously tested transdermal magnesium, driven by the hypothesis that topical application could deliver magnesium directly to muscle tissues to reduce cramping and soreness.


- **The Gel Study (2025):** A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involving 35 active participants found **no effect** of topical magnesium gel on muscle soreness, peak torque, or blood markers of muscle damage (creatine kinase, IL-6) following downhill running.[14] Crucially, plasma magnesium levels did not change, indicating the magnesium neither entered the blood nor the muscle.
- **The Eccentric Exercise Study:** A similar double-blind crossover study on 19 men found that transdermal magnesium chloride did not reduce perceived soreness or improve force recovery.[15]

**Implication:** If the "magnesium oil" could penetrate the skin and "saturate the tissues" as marketing claims suggest, these physiological markers of muscle trauma and recovery should have shown improvement. The null results strongly imply that the magnesium remains on the surface of the skin, failing to reach the underlying muscle tissue.


### 4.3 The "Magnesium Oil" Misnomer and Irritation
The product marketed as "magnesium oil" is, in reality, a supersaturated solution of magnesium chloride brine. It feels oily due to its high viscosity and hygroscopic (water-attracting) nature, not because it contains lipids.[4, 16]


Users often report a stinging or itching sensation, which marketers frequently frame as a diagnostic sign of "magnesium deficiency" or a "detox reaction".[17] Toxicologically, this is incorrect. The sensation is a classic irritant contact dermatitis caused by the high osmotic pressure of the salt solution and its naturally low pH. The hypertonic brine dehydrates the epidermal cells and triggers nociceptors (pain receptors). It is a sign of barrier disruption, not absorption or deficiency.


## 5. The Magnesium Bath: Sulfate vs. Chloride and the Dead Sea Anomaly
To fully understand the landscape of transdermal magnesium, one must distinguish between the different salts used in balneotherapy (bathing therapy), as their chemical behaviors and associated claims differ significantly.


### 5.1 Magnesium Sulfate (Epsom Salts)
Magnesium sulfate ($MgSO_4 \cdot 7H_2O$) is the traditional choice for bath salts.


- **Solubility and Chemistry:** While soluble, the sulfate ion ($SO_4^{2-}$) is large and divalent, adding another layer of difficulty to transdermal transport.
- **The "Detox" Myth:** A pervasive claim is that Epsom salt baths work via "reverse osmosis," drawing toxins out of the body while allowing magnesium in.[6] This is biologically implausible. The skin is not a semi-permeable dialysis membrane. Osmosis moves water, not complex toxin molecules. While hypertonic baths might draw a small amount of water from the epidermis (trans-epidermal water loss), they do not selectively extract heavy metals or metabolic waste.
- **Therapeutic Mechanism:** The perceived benefits of Epsom salt baths—muscle relaxation and pain relief—are likely attributable to the **thermal effects** of the water (vasodilation, increased blood flow, reduced muscle spindle firing) and the **hydrostatic pressure** of immersion, rather than systemic magnesium uptake.[18]

### 5.2 Magnesium Chloride and Dead Sea Salts
Dead Sea salts differ fundamentally from Epsom salts. They are primarily magnesium chloride ($MgCl_2$, ~30–35%), with significant concentrations of potassium chloride ($KCl$, ~25%) and, crucially, bromides ($Br^-$).[19, 20]


- **Solubility:** Magnesium chloride is significantly more soluble and hygroscopic than sulfate, potentially increasing its residence time on the skin and its interaction with the stratum corneum.[21]
- **Systemic Absorption Claims:** Despite the higher solubility, studies comparing Dead Sea baths to tap water have typically found minimal or no change in serum magnesium levels in healthy controls or psoriasis patients.[22] One study noted a decrease in serum manganese and a variable response in lithium, but magnesium levels remained stable, reinforcing the barrier's efficacy against this ion.

### 5.3 The Confounding Role of Bromides
A critical, often overlooked factor in the "relaxation" effect of Dead Sea baths is the presence of bromides. The concentration of bromide in the Dead Sea is approximately 5,000 mg/L, roughly 50 times higher than standard ocean water.[23, 24]


- **Pharmacology:** Bromide salts (e.g., potassium bromide) were historically used as sedatives and anticonvulsants before the advent of modern barbiturates. They act by substituting for chloride ions in neuronal ion channels, hyperpolarizing neurons and depressing central nervous system activity.[25, 26]
- **Transdermal Potential:** Unlike magnesium, there is evidence that bromides can be absorbed systemically through the skin or via inhalation of aerosols during bathing. Cases of "bromism" (bromide toxicity), characterized by confusion, ataxia, and skin eruptions (bromoderma), have been documented in individuals overusing Dead Sea salts or products.[23, 27]
- **Insight:** The profound relaxation, improved sleep, and stress reduction reported by users of Dead Sea salts may be a pharmacological effect of **bromide absorption**, compounded by thermal relaxation, rather than any increase in serum magnesium. The "magnesium sleep" effect is likely a "bromide sedation" effect.

## 6. Dermatological Applications: The Reality of Local Effects
While the systemic absorption hypothesis crumbles under scrutiny, the dermatological benefits of magnesium salts are supported by credible evidence. The confusion arises when *local* skin benefits are conflated with *systemic* nutritional correction.


### 6.1 Barrier Repair and Filaggrin Expression
The UQ research that identified the follicular pathway also provided a mechanism for local efficacy. The application of magnesium chloride was shown to upregulate the expression of **filaggrin** (FLG) in the epidermis.[8, 28]


- **Filaggrin's Role:** Filaggrin is a structural protein essential for the formation of the skin barrier. It breaks down into natural moisturizing factors (NMFs) that keep the skin hydrated and maintains the acidic pH required for antimicrobial defense.
- **Clinical Relevance:** In conditions like atopic dermatitis (eczema) and psoriasis, filaggrin expression is often downregulated. By stimulating FLG production, topical magnesium improves skin hydration, reduces roughness, and repairs the barrier phenotype.[8, 29] This is a *local* effect within the epidermis, requiring penetration only into the viable epidermis, not the systemic circulation.

### 6.2 Anti-Inflammatory Mechanisms
Balneotherapy with magnesium-rich water has been shown to reduce inflammation in psoriatic plaques. The mechanism appears to involve the inhibition of Langerhans cells, the skin's resident immune sentinels. High extracellular magnesium concentrations inhibit the antigen-presenting capacity of these cells, dampening the T-cell mediated inflammatory response characteristic of psoriasis.[30, 31] Again, this is a surface-mediated immunomodulatory effect that does not necessitate systemic absorption.


## 7. Comparative Pharmacokinetics: Oral vs. Transdermal
To understand why the medical consensus remains with oral supplementation, one must compare the specialized machinery of the gut with the passive resistance of the skin.


### 7.1 The Specialized Gut
The gastrointestinal tract is evolutionarily designed to manage magnesium homeostasis. It employs specialized active transport proteins, specifically **TRPM6** and **TRPM7** (Transient Receptor Potential Melastatin), located in the brush border of enterocytes.[1] These transporters actively pump magnesium ions against concentration gradients, allowing for regulated absorption.


- **Bioavailability:** Oral bioavailability ranges from 20% to 50%, depending on the body's status. If magnesium stores are low, the gut upregulates TRPM6 to increase absorption. If stores are high, absorption decreases.
- **The "Laxative" Mechanism:** The gastrointestinal side effects of magnesium (diarrhea) are osmotic. Unabsorbed magnesium ions in the colon attract water. Specifically, for every millimole of magnesium remaining in the gut, approximately 7.3 grams of water is retained in the stool.[5] While unpleasant, this mechanism highlights the gut's limited uptake capacity; it does not validate the skin as a superior alternative.

### 7.2 The Passive Skin
In contrast, the skin lacks TRPM6/7 transporters in the stratum corneum.[1] Transport relies entirely on passive diffusion through a lipid medium that is hostile to the hydrated ion. Without active transporters, there is no mechanism to "pull" magnesium into the system or regulate its uptake based on physiological need. The "saturation" claimed by proponents is physically impossible without a transport mechanism to overcome the lipid barrier's resistance.


## 8. Safety, Toxicity, and the Danger of Substitution
### 8.1 The Risk of Untreated Deficiency
The most significant danger of the transdermal magnesium trend is not toxicity, but therapeutic failure. Patients with genuine hypomagnesemia—caused by diuretic use, proton pump inhibitors, alcoholism, or chronic renal disease—require measurable, reliable repletion.[3, 32] Relying on magnesium sprays or baths, which provide unpredictable and likely negligible systemic delivery, places these patients at risk of continued deficiency complications, such as cardiac arrhythmias (Torsades de pointes), neuromuscular hyperexcitability, and seizures.[33]


### 8.2 Toxicity Risks
While absorption is generally too low to cause systemic toxicity in healthy adults, risks exist in specific populations:


- **Renal Compromise:** In patients with severe renal failure, the kidneys cannot excrete even small amounts of absorbed magnesium. While transdermal absorption is low, repeated application on compromised skin (e.g., ulcers, extensive psoriasis) could theoretically contribute to hypermagnesemia in this vulnerable group.[5]
- **Bromism:** As noted, the use of Dead Sea salts carries the specific risk of bromide accumulation. Patients presenting with confusion or unexplained skin rashes who frequently use these products should be screened for elevated serum bromide levels.[23]

## 9. Synthesis and Conclusion
The proposition that transdermal magnesium absorption is an effective method for elevating systemic magnesium levels is **not supported by the weight of scientific evidence**.


The "standard claim" that magnesium baths increase bodily magnesium levels without GI side effects is a fallacy built upon:


1. **Misinterpretation of Pilot Data:** Relying on unpublished or methodologically flawed studies (Waring, Watkins) while ignoring robust RCTs and systematic reviews (Gröber, Kass).
2. **Physicochemical Ignorance:** Overlooking the massive hydration shell of the magnesium ion and the lipophilic nature of the skin barrier.
3. **Conflation of Effects:** Confusing local dermatological benefits (barrier repair via filaggrin) and thermal relaxation (vasodilation) with systemic nutritional repletion.
4. **The Bromide Confounder:** Attributing the sedative effects of Dead Sea salts to magnesium rather than the potent central nervous system depressant, bromide, which is present in high concentrations.

**Final Verdict:**


- **Systemic Efficacy:** **Ineffective.** The skin permeability to magnesium ions is negligible. Transdermal application should not be used as a substitute for oral or intravenous therapy in the treatment of magnesium deficiency.
- **Local Efficacy:** **Valid.** Magnesium-rich topical applications have demonstrable benefits for skin barrier function and inflammation in conditions like psoriasis and eczema.
- **Bath Benefits:** **Real but Misattributed.** The relaxation from "magnesium baths" is likely a combination of thermal therapy, placebo response, and (in the case of Dead Sea salts) bromide absorption, rather than systemic magnesium uptake.

Clinicians should counsel patients that while magnesium baths are a safe and pleasant adjunctive therapy for stress and skin conditions, they are not a viable route for nutritional supplementation. The gastrointestinal tract, despite its side effects, remains the only evolutionarily adapted and scientifically validated route for systemic magnesium repletion.
