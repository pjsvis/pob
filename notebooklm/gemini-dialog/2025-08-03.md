# A Fraying Fabric: The Outlook for Social Conditions in the United Kingdom Amidst a Polycrisis
### Executive Summary
The United Kingdom stands at a critical juncture, facing a medium-term outlook for its social conditions that is profoundly challenging and fraught with risk. This report presents a comprehensive assessment of this outlook, arguing that a convergence of severe external pressures and acute domestic vulnerabilities is placing the nation's social fabric under unprecedented strain. The analysis indicates that without a significant reorientation of public policy and a more benign international environment, the UK is on a trajectory toward a "lost decade" for living standards, which threatens to deepen poverty, accelerate the degradation of public services, and fray the bonds of social cohesion.


The global context is one of radical uncertainty. The post-Cold War era of hyper-globalisation has given way to a period of intense geoeconomic competition, trade fragmentation, and policy-driven decoupling. The war in Ukraine has catalysed a permanent shift in European security, reversing the post-Cold War "peace dividend" and forcing a long-term reallocation of fiscal resources from social welfare to defence. These geopolitical shocks are unfolding against a macroeconomic backdrop of subdued global growth, persistent inflationary pressures, and a higher interest rate environment, collectively constraining the UK's economic prospects and policy options.


These external headwinds are colliding with a set of deep-seated domestic fault lines. The UK's public finances, as assessed by the Office for Budget Responsibility, are in a "relatively vulnerable position," with high public debt and severely eroded fiscal buffers limiting the state's capacity to respond to future shocks. Simultaneously, high levels of household debt, particularly among lower-income groups, create a significant vulnerability that can amplify the effects of any economic downturn, risking a sharp contraction in spending and a wave of defaults. The Bank of England's "severe but plausible" stress test scenario illustrates the potential for these vulnerabilities to translate into a deep recession, with significant declines in asset prices and surges in unemployment.


The social consequences of this economic precarity are already becoming evident in forward-looking projections. Analysis from the Resolution Foundation points to a bleak outlook for household incomes, with the poorest half of the population facing stagnant or falling real incomes over the next five years. This could make the 2020s the first decade in the modern era to see zero growth in disposable incomes. Complementing this, research by the Joseph Rowntree Foundation reveals a social crisis of deepening poverty, with an alarming rise not only in the headline poverty rate but in "very deep poverty" and destitution, where millions are unable to afford to meet their most basic physical needs.


This deterioration in household well-being is mirrored by a crisis in public services. The National Health Service, a bellwether for the wider public sector, faces a staggering financial deficit, forcing service cuts and rationing that directly impact patient care and undermine public confidence. This is the culmination of a decade of underinvestment and the erosion of preventative services, leaving the system brittle and unable to cope with rising demand and new shocks.


The cumulative weight of these economic and social pressures poses a significant risk to the UK's political stability. Historical precedents, both in the UK and internationally, demonstrate a clear link between severe economic crises, social unrest, and political radicalisation. In a climate of already high political polarisation and declining trust in institutions, a prolonged period of hardship could trigger a dangerous feedback loop, where policy failure erodes legitimacy, and a delegitimised political system becomes incapable of effective action.


This report concludes by outlining three potential scenarios for the medium term—a central case of "Muddling Through" with continued social degradation, a pessimistic "Stagflationary Shock" leading to a severe social crisis, and an optimistic "Resilient Recovery." Navigating this storm will require a proactive and strategic policy agenda focused on rebuilding resilience across the economy and society. This includes a credible plan for fiscal stabilisation that does not deepen social hardship, urgent reform of the social security system to eliminate destitution, a long-term strategy for the renewal of public services, and a concerted effort to bolster social cohesion and democratic health. The choices made in the immediate future will determine whether the UK's social fabric can withstand the immense pressures of this new era.


## Section 1: The Global Context: An Era of Radical Uncertainty
The social conditions within the United Kingdom over the medium term will be inescapably shaped by a global environment that is more volatile, fragmented, and competitive than at any time in recent decades. The UK's National Security Strategy 2025 correctly identifies the emergence of a "new era... characterised by radical uncertainty," in which the international order is being reshaped by great power competition, authoritarian aggression, and the erosion of established norms.[1] This section details the three primary vectors of this global polycrisis—geoeconomic fragmentation, persistent security and commodity shocks, and a macroeconomic drag—which collectively create a formidable set of external constraints and pressures on the UK's economy and society.


### 1.1 The New Geoeconomics: Fragmentation and Reconfiguration
The foundational assumption of an ever-integrating global economy, which underpinned Western economic strategy for three decades, is no longer valid. The world is undergoing a "policy-driven reversal of global economic integration," a process the International Monetary Fund (IMF) terms "geo-economic fragmentation".[2] This is not a cyclical downturn in trade but a structural reconfiguration of global supply chains and investment flows, driven by strategic competition and a prioritisation of national security and resilience over pure economic efficiency.


Analysis by the McKinsey Global Institute, using 2024 data, confirms that this reconfiguration is persistent and accelerating. The United States has continued to reduce its trade dependency on China, redirecting flows towards economies such as Mexico and Vietnam, while European nations have decisively decoupled from the Russian economy.[3] The most significant quantifiable shift is a 7% fall in the average "geopolitical distance" of trade between 2017 and 2024, a period encompassing the intensification of US-China trade tensions and Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. This metric confirms that economies at opposite ends of the geopolitical spectrum are actively trading less with one another.[3] This fragmentation carries substantial economic costs. The World Economic Forum, in partnership with Oliver Wyman, estimates that a significant disintegration of the global financial system into distinct blocs could result in one-year economic output losses ranging from 0.5% to as much as 5% of global GDP, depending on the severity of the scenario.[4, 5]


For the United Kingdom, an open, services-oriented economy operating outside the world's three major economic blocs (the United States, the European Union, and China), this new landscape presents a profound strategic challenge. The UK's own goods trade intensity fell by 3% between 2017 and 2023, driven by sluggish export growth, indicating an early vulnerability to these trends.[3] In a world of rising protectionism and competing regulatory regimes, the UK risks being caught in the crossfire of trade disputes, facing restricted market access and heightened costs for critical imports.[6, 7, 8] This dynamic creates a unique "triple exposure" for the nation. First, unlike EU member states, it must navigate the new geoeconomic landscape alone, lacking the scale to set regulatory standards or the leverage of a large single market. Second, as a leading European security power, it is at the forefront of the continent's response to Russian aggression, bearing the full fiscal and economic consequences. Third, the global nature of the City of London makes the UK economy exceptionally sensitive to the international financial cycles and monetary policy spillovers that characterise this volatile era, a core risk identified in the Bank of England's stress testing framework.[9] This convergence of exposures means the UK is less insulated from the global polycrisis than larger, more self-contained economies or integrated blocs.


### 1.2 Persistent Shocks: Conflict, Energy, and Food Security
The 2022 invasion of Ukraine was not a fleeting crisis but a catalysing event that has triggered long-term structural changes in European security, energy, and food systems, with direct and enduring consequences for UK social conditions.


The most significant of these is the wholesale re-prioritisation of public finances towards defence. European military expenditure is surging, rising by 17% in 2024 alone, the steepest year-on-year increase since the end of the Cold War.[10] This is not a temporary spike; NATO members have endorsed new national spending benchmarks of up to 5% of GDP by 2035, a target that would more than double the previous 2% guideline.[11, 12] This represents a fundamental reversal of the post-Cold War "peace dividend"—the fiscal space created by reduced military spending from the 1990s onward, which enabled the expansion of social investment and the welfare state. This new era of rearmament creates an unavoidable and long-term trade-off. As analysis by the New Economics Foundation highlights, the scale of investment required to meet green and social goals, such as climate mitigation and healthcare, is comparable to the proposed increases in defence spending. In an environment of already strained public finances, "choosing weapons over addressing climate breakdown and social fragility" becomes a stark political reality.[13] This structural shift will crowd out social investment for a generation, locking in underfunding for critical public services and making the crises detailed later in this report systemic rather than cyclical.


In energy markets, Europe has successfully engineered an historic decoupling from Russian natural gas, a necessary step to enhance long-term security. However, this has come at a significant medium-term cost. The IMF concludes that while the war has accelerated Europe's green transition and will lead to lower emissions by 2030, it has also resulted in higher domestic energy prices and required vast investment in alternative supplies and infrastructure, such as Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) terminals.[14, 15] These elevated energy costs feed directly into household budgets and business expenses in the UK, acting as a persistent drag on living standards and competitiveness.


The war's impact on global food security has been equally profound, causing what the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) describes as the "greatest military-related increase in global food insecurity in at least a century".[16] Ukraine's agricultural sector has been devastated, with an estimated $40.2 billion in losses and damages by early 2023, and its exports of wheat and corn are predicted to remain significantly below pre-war levels.[16] While global commodity prices have fallen from their peaks in 2022, this masks a continuing crisis. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) warns that millions will remain chronically undernourished in 2030 because of the war's effects.[16] Furthermore, local food price inflation remains stubbornly high in many low- and middle-income countries, and the cost of humanitarian assistance has skyrocketed.[16, 17] For the UK, this translates into higher prices for imported food and contributes to domestic food price inflation, a pressure that disproportionately harms the poorest households for whom food constitutes a larger share of expenditure.


### 1.3 The Macroeconomic Drag: A World of Slow Growth and High Uncertainty
The geopolitical and security shocks are unfolding within a global macroeconomic environment that is itself a major headwind. The world economy appears to be settling into a prolonged period of subdued growth, persistent (though moderating) inflation, and elevated policy uncertainty, creating a challenging backdrop for the UK's domestic recovery.


Major international forecasting bodies have consistently revised down their projections for global growth. The World Bank's June 2025 *Global Economic Prospects* report, for instance, cut its forecast for 2025 to just 2.3%, citing heightened trade tensions and policy uncertainty as the primary drivers.[18, 19] Similarly, the OECD's June 2025 outlook projects global growth slowing to 2.9% in both 2025 and 2026, warning that "substantial barriers to trade, tighter financial conditions, diminishing confidence and heightened policy uncertainty" are having adverse impacts.[20, 21] The IMF's July 2025 *World Economic Outlook* projects a tenuous global growth rate of 3.0% for 2025, emphasizing that downside risks from potentially higher tariffs and geopolitical tensions remain significant.[22, 23]


This pervasive uncertainty is a critical factor in itself. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) noted in its 2025 annual report that while a slowdown had yet to fully materialise in hard data, "elevated measures of uncertainty and weakening consumer and business confidence indicators clearly signal a deterioration of economic activity ahead".[24] This climate of unpredictability, particularly around future trade policy, makes it harder for businesses to plan and invest, curbing growth prospects.[21, 25]


This global slowdown directly affects the UK through multiple channels. Weaker global demand constrains the UK's export-oriented sectors, while volatile financial markets and cautious investor sentiment can stifle investment. Furthermore, the global fight against inflation, led by the US Federal Reserve, creates significant cross-border monetary policy spillovers. A more hawkish stance in the US can transmit to the UK through tighter global financial conditions, influencing domestic interest rates, capital flows, and the cost of borrowing for the UK government, businesses, and households.[26, 27] This external monetary environment further constrains the Bank of England's room for manoeuvre and complicates the management of the domestic economy.


## Section 2: Britain's Economic Fault Lines
The global headwinds described in the previous section are not impacting a resilient economy. Instead, they are colliding with a British economy characterised by deep-seated structural weaknesses and acute vulnerabilities. A decade of stagnant productivity growth, compounded by the shocks of Brexit and the COVID-19 pandemic, has left the UK with minimal buffers in both its public and private sector balance sheets. This section analyses three critical domestic fault lines: a precarious fiscal position that limits the state's capacity to act as a shock absorber; a high level of household debt that serves as a potent amplifier of recessionary forces; and the official assessment of how these vulnerabilities could combine to produce a severe economic downturn.


### 2.1 The Vulnerable State: A Precarious Fiscal Position
The United Kingdom's public finances have been severely weakened by the cumulative impact of the 2008 financial crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the 2022 energy crisis, leaving the government with dangerously little fiscal space to confront the next shock. The Office for Budget Responsibility's (OBR) July 2025 *Fiscal risks and sustainability* report provides a stark assessment, describing the UK's public finances as being in a "relatively vulnerable position".[28]


At the end of 2024, the UK's government deficit stood at 5.7% of GDP, a figure approximately 4 percentage points higher than the average for advanced economies. This places the UK with the third-highest deficit among 28 advanced European economies.[28] Underlying public debt is now at its highest level since the early 1960s and is projected to continue rising over the medium term. This deterioration is not solely the result of external shocks; it also reflects a consistent failure to consolidate the public finances during periods of recovery. The OBR notes that despite a stated goal of reducing public debt as a share of GDP featuring in eight of the last nine fiscal frameworks, underlying debt has actually risen by 60% of GDP over the past 20 years.[28] This has been driven by a pattern of governments reversing planned tax rises and, more significantly, abandoning planned spending reductions in the face of political pressure.


The consequences of this fiscal erosion are profound. The UK's capacity to respond to future crises has been substantially diminished. Arresting the rise in debt has become significantly more challenging due to a combination of slower economic growth and the sharp rise in global interest rates, which has pushed the UK's 10-year bond yields to be among the highest in the advanced world.[28] A critical structural imbalance now exists: even with the tax-to-GDP ratio rising to its highest level since 1950, government borrowing is still 3% of GDP above the level that would be required to durably stabilise the debt.[28] This lack of fiscal headroom is the single greatest constraint on the government's ability to mitigate the social consequences of a future economic downturn. It severely limits the scope for large-scale household support packages, investment in public services, or counter-cyclical fiscal stimulus, making the social outcomes of any crisis more severe than they otherwise would be.


### 2.2 The Household Debt Overhang: An Amplifier of Recession
Parallel to the vulnerability in public finances is an equally significant weakness in the private sector: the high level of household debt. While not at the extreme peak seen before the 2008 financial crisis, when the debt-to-income ratio reached 156%, household balance sheets remain stretched and are poised to act as a powerful amplifier in any future recession.[29, 30]


The House of Commons Library briefing on this topic states unequivocally that in the medium term, "high debt in the economy can make a recession deeper and longer".[29, 30] The mechanism is a dangerous feedback loop. During and after a recession, households with high debt levels are forced to cut back on their spending more aggressively than other households to service their debts in the face of falling incomes or job insecurity. This sharp contraction in consumption deepens the recession, leading to further job losses and income declines. Simultaneously, these households are more likely to default on their loans, resulting in significant losses for lenders, which can trigger a credit crunch that further strangles the economy.[29, 30]


Recent trends suggest this vulnerability is becoming more acute. The cost of living crisis has forced a growing number of households to rely on credit to pay for essentials. Debt advice charities like Stepchange and Citizens Advice reported a record level of clients in January 2025.[30] An Office for National Statistics (ONS) survey in April 2025 found that of the 72% of adults who saw their cost of living increase, 14% reported using more credit than usual as a result.[29] This indicates that debt is no longer primarily being used to finance large purchases but is increasingly a tool for basic survival for a significant minority of the population. This dynamic of "twin deficits of resilience"—a fiscally constrained state and financially stretched households—is a departure from previous crises. In 2008, the state had the fiscal capacity to absorb the shock from the private sector. During the pandemic, household balance sheets were, on average, in a stronger position. The current conjuncture combines the weaknesses of both, suggesting that neither the public nor the private sector can effectively act as a shock absorber, pointing towards a uniquely damaging and prolonged social crisis in the event of a new shock.


### 2.3 Anatomy of a Downturn: The Bank of England's Stress Test Scenario
To understand the potential severity of a future crisis, it is not necessary to speculate. The Bank of England's 2025 Bank Capital Stress Test (BCST) provides a "severe but plausible" blueprint for how the global shocks and domestic vulnerabilities outlined in this report could converge to create a deep and damaging UK recession.[9] While not a forecast, the BCST is a crucial analytical tool that reveals the official assessment of the UK's systemic risks.


The narrative of the 2025 stress test scenario is a direct reflection of the contemporary threat landscape. It posits a "severe negative global aggregate supply shock," which is exacerbated by heightened geopolitical tensions, leading to sharp increases in commodity prices and a material disruption in global supply chains through trade fragmentation.[9] This triggers a sharp rise in global inflation, forcing central banks to raise interest rates significantly. The result is deep, simultaneous recessions across the UK and global economies, accompanied by large falls in asset prices.[9]


While the precise quantitative projections for the 2025 test are designed for bank capital adequacy purposes, the methodology aligns with what Oxford Economics describes as a "99th percentile tail-risk scenario".[31] Modelling based on this framework provides a granular view of the potential social dislocation. Such a scenario would involve an outsized start-to-trough fall in UK house prices of 28%, a surge in the unemployment rate, and a sharp drop in households' real income. This would trigger a wave of personal and corporate insolvencies, with rates projected to exceed those recorded during the 2008 Global Financial Crisis.[31]


This scenario also highlights the asymmetric vulnerability of the UK economy. London's high concentration of financial and related professional services—accounting for over 20% of its output—makes it uniquely exposed to a crisis of this nature.[32] A severe financial shock would deliver a devastating blow to the capital's economy and, by extension, the entire country. The financial services industry is the UK's largest taxpayer, contributing an estimated £110.2 billion in the year to March 2023.[33] A crisis in the sector would therefore blow a catastrophic hole in the UK's already fragile public finances, forcing a secondary wave of severe austerity or tax rises that would disproportionately harm public service-dependent populations in other regions. The BCST thus provides a sobering, quantified glimpse of the potential magnitude of the social crisis that could unfold if the UK's economic fault lines are subjected to the severe global pressures of this new era.

## Section 3: The Social Ledger: Worsening Outcomes for the British People
The macroeconomic and fiscal vulnerabilities detailed in the preceding section are not abstract risks; they are translating into a tangible and alarming deterioration in the social conditions experienced by a large portion of the British population. This section connects the economic analysis to its human consequences, demonstrating that the UK is already on a trajectory of deepening social distress. The outlook for living standards is bleak, poverty is becoming more widespread and severe, and the public services that form the social safety net are at a breaking point. Any new economic shock would therefore not be falling on a healthy society, but one already weakened by years of stagnation and inequality.


### 3.1 The End of Progress? Living Standards in a Lost Decade
For much of the post-war period, rising living standards were a core component of the UK's social contract. That era of progress appears to be over. The medium-term outlook for household incomes is exceptionally weak, with the 2020s on track to be the first decade in the modern era to witness zero growth in typical disposable incomes, a historic stagnation that represents a profound policy failure.[35, 40]


According to the Resolution Foundation's *Living Standards Outlook 2025*, which synthesises OBR and Bank of England forecasts, the central projection is for typical disposable incomes (after housing costs) to grow by a mere 1%, or £300 in real terms, over the five years between 2024-25 and 2029-30.[40] This follows a "lost half-decade" since 2019-20, where the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and the cost of living crisis have wiped out any progress, despite a temporary income recovery in 2024-25 driven by tax cuts.[41, 42] When viewed over the entire decade, the projection is for zero growth in median incomes, a stark contrast to the 18% growth recorded in the 2000s and the 9% growth in the 2010s.[41]


Crucially, this bleak aggregate picture masks a sharp and growing inequality in outcomes. The poorest half of the non-pensioner population is on course to fare even worse, with their average real incomes projected to *fall* by 1% over the second half of the decade.[40] In contrast, the incomes of the richest half are set to rise by 1%, and typical pensioner incomes are projected to grow by a healthy 5%.[40, 42] Housing tenure is a key determinant of these divergent fortunes. Outright owners are set to see the fastest income growth, while mortgagors face an average income fall of 1% as they continue to refinance from low fixed-rate deals onto much higher interest rates.[40] This stagnation and divergence in living standards creates a fertile ground for social discontent and places immense pressure on family finances, particularly for those with children and high housing costs.


### 3.2 Poverty in the 21st Century: Scale, Depth, and Destitution
The consequence of stagnant incomes and a fraying social safety net is a poverty crisis that is not only widespread but is becoming deeper and more severe. The nature of poverty in the UK has changed; it is increasingly characterised by an inability to afford the most basic essentials for a dignified life. This represents a social emergency that is unfolding even before the impact of a new major recession.


The Joseph Rowntree Foundation's (JRF) authoritative *UK Poverty 2025* report finds that in 2022/23, more than one in five people in the UK (21%), totalling 14.3 million, were in poverty. This includes 4.3 million children—approximately three in every ten—and 8.1 million working-age adults.[43] These figures have returned to their high pre-pandemic levels after temporary COVID-related support was withdrawn.


More alarming than the headline number is the worsening depth of this poverty. In 2022/23, 6 million people were in "very deep poverty," meaning their household income was more than 40% below the official poverty line. For these households, the average income was 57% below the poverty line, a gap that has increased by almost two-thirds over the past 25 years, indicating that the poorest are falling further and further behind.[43] This trend culminates in the most extreme form of material hardship: destitution. In 2022, approximately 3.8 million people, including one million children, experienced destitution, defined as being unable to afford to meet their most basic physical needs to stay warm, dry, clean, and fed. This figure has more than doubled since 2017, signalling a catastrophic failure of the social safety net for a growing part of the population.[43]


This crisis is structurally embedded and unlikely to be solved by economic growth alone. JRF's modelling shows that even in a "strong economy" scenario with higher employment, the number of people in poverty is projected to remain stubbornly high at around 14.6 million in 2029.[44] This reveals a critical decoupling, where the benefits of economic activity are no longer reaching the lowest-income households. The reasons are systemic: the erosion of the value of social security benefits, policies like the two-child limit, and the high cost of essential goods like housing and energy have created a situation where a rising economic tide no longer lifts the boats at the bottom of the harbour. For these households, the anchor of high costs and an inadequate safety net is holding them down, regardless of headline GDP growth.


### 3.3 The Fraying Safety Net: Public Services at Breaking Point
The third pillar of social well-being, alongside household incomes and poverty, is the provision of high-quality public services. Here too, the outlook is one of acute crisis. The National Health Service (NHS), the UK's most cherished public institution, serves as a powerful case study for a wider malaise affecting public services, which are caught in a vice between rising demand, the legacy of underinvestment, and a new, severe funding crunch that is forcing explicit cuts to provision.


Despite a £22.6 billion funding increase announced in the 2024 Autumn Budget, the financial position of the NHS remains dire. The King's Fund reports that the first iteration of financial plans for the 2025/26 financial year showed a "very significant financial deficit" of £6.6 billion across NHS systems.[45, 46] This pressure is driven by a confluence of factors: high inflation for non-pay costs like energy and medicines, rising staff costs following industrial action, and an increase in patient acuity, with sicker patients requiring more complex and expensive care.[46]


This financial distress is having a direct and negative impact on patient care. To balance their budgets, NHS leaders are being forced to make "tough choices," including freezing recruitment, reducing services such as patient transport and community paediatrics, and holding down investment in public health and mental health services.[45, 46] These are no longer efficiency savings; they are explicit reductions in the scope and quality of care. This situation is the direct result of what can be termed the "social long COVID" of 2010s austerity. The deep cuts to public spending in that decade stripped away preventative services, starved the system of capital investment (leading to a current maintenance backlog of £13.8 billion), and eroded workforce resilience.[46, 47, 48] This created a brittle system that was unable to withstand the shocks of the 2020s and which now requires far greater emergency investment to simply stand still.


The public is acutely aware of this degradation. Public satisfaction with the NHS has fallen to its lowest level since the British Social Attitudes survey began over 40 years ago.[45] There is a pervasive fear among the public that the service "will not be there for me when I need it".[49] The crisis in the NHS is therefore not just a fiscal problem; it is a crisis of the social contract, symptomatic of a wider decline in the state's ability to provide the security and support that citizens expect.

## Section 4: Fractures in the Polity: Risks to Social and Political Stability
The severe economic and social strains detailed in the preceding sections are unlikely to remain contained within the economic sphere. A prolonged period of stagnant living standards, deepening poverty, and degrading public services poses a significant risk to the UK's social cohesion and political stability. This section assesses these second-order consequences, exploring the historical relationship between economic hardship and social unrest in the UK, the documented link between financial crises and political radicalisation, and the likely impact of a downturn on crime and security. The analysis suggests that a major economic shock could trigger a dangerous feedback loop, where economic crisis and political instability become mutually reinforcing.


### 4.1 Echoes of the Past: Economic Crisis and Social Unrest
British history offers a clear and cautionary lesson: periods of acute economic distress are frequently accompanied by widespread social and political unrest. The mechanisms are straightforward—economic hardship erodes the legitimacy of the existing order and provides fertile ground for movements demanding radical change.


In the years immediately following the Napoleonic Wars, the economic slump of 1819, which brought widespread wage cuts and unemployment, directly prompted a wave of mass protest demanding parliamentary reform. This period of turmoil saw the rise of radical movements aiming to rally such overwhelming numbers that the government would be forced to concede, culminating in serious unrest and armed rebellions in several parts of the country by 1820.[50]


A more contemporary example can be found in the 2011 England riots. While the immediate trigger was the police shooting of Mark Duggan in North London, the disorder spread rapidly against a backdrop of the post-2008 recession, high youth unemployment, and the initial wave of the coalition government's austerity programme, which included significant cuts to local services such as youth centres.[51, 52] Subsequent research, including interviews with those who participated, revealed that while motivations were mixed, many cited economic exclusion, government cuts, lack of jobs, and anger at the scrapping of educational support as key factors.[52, 53] Official analysis confirmed the link to deprivation, with 64% of young defendants coming from the poorest fifth of areas in the country.[52] These historical precedents demonstrate that while a specific event may act as a catalyst, underlying economic grievances are often the combustible material for widespread social disorder.


### 4.2 The Politics of Hardship: Polarisation and Extremism
Beyond spontaneous unrest, there is compelling international evidence that severe economic crises, particularly those with a financial origin, systematically alter the political landscape, fostering polarisation and increasing support for extremist ideologies. The UK, with its current vulnerabilities, is not immune to this dynamic.


A landmark historical study covering 20 advanced economies over 140 years found a powerful and consistent pattern: in the five years following a systemic financial crisis, political parties on the far-right increase their vote share by an average of 30% relative to their pre-crisis level.[54] The study concludes that "voters seem to be systematically lured by the political rhetoric of the far right, with its frequently nationalistic or xenophobic tendencies" in the aftermath of a crash. The mechanism is a breakdown in conventional politics; crises tend to lead to parliamentary fractionalisation and shrinking government majorities, making decisive governance more difficult and creating a political vacuum that extremist and populist movements are quick to exploit.[54]


The contemporary UK exhibits many of the preconditions for such a political shift. The government's own National Security Strategy acknowledges that "extremist ideologies are on the rise" in the current "era of radical uncertainty".[1] Recent research highlights a "boiling pot" of societal threats in the UK, fuelled by the cost of living crisis, concerns over immigration, and global events, which are contributing to the mainstreaming of extremist narratives.[55] This is occurring in a context of already high political polarisation—the highest in the developed world outside the United States between 2017-2022—and a severe decline in public trust. In 2024, 45% of people in the UK reported that they "almost never trust the government to put the nation's interests first," up from 23% in 2020.[55]


This convergence of factors could create a "crisis of legitimacy" feedback loop. An economic shock, exacerbated by the state's fiscally constrained inability to respond effectively, could lead to a collapse in public trust. This would delegitimise the political system, fuelling the polarisation and extremist politics identified in the research. A more fragmented and radicalised political environment would, in turn, be less capable of implementing the coherent, long-term policies needed to resolve the underlying economic crisis, thus creating a downward spiral of economic failure and political dysfunction.


### 4.3 Crime, Insecurity, and Recession
The relationship between economic recessions and crime rates is complex and often misunderstood. While a simple link between unemployment and acquisitive crime is not always borne out in the data, a severe and prolonged downturn is likely to lead to increases in specific types of crime, creating a tangible impact on public feelings of safety and security.[56]


Academic research from the UK and US indicates that the overall link between recessions and crime can be weak or even counter-intuitive. A downturn may paradoxically reduce opportunities for some property crimes, as people spend less on high-value goods and spend more time at home, leaving property less unguarded.[57, 58] However, this aggregate picture masks significant shifts within crime categories. There is a strong and direct correlation between economic downturns and a rise in fraud and cybercrime. As financial pressures mount on individuals and businesses, a minority turn to fraudulent means to address their difficulties, a trend that was observed in the 1980, 1990, and 2008 recessions.[59]


The most disturbing and socially damaging link, however, is between youth unemployment and long-term criminality. Research has identified a powerful "crime scars" effect: young people who leave school and attempt to enter the labour market during a recession are significantly more likely to engage in crime not just in the short term, but throughout their lives.[60] Early experiences of unemployment and exclusion can increase an individual's stock of "criminal capital" while reducing their accumulation of human capital in the legal labour market, increasing the probability that they become a career criminal. This effect, combined with the documented negative impact of recessions on the mental health of the young, including increased risk of depression and suicide [61], suggests a severe economic crisis would inflict a deep and lasting "generational scar." It would create a cohort of young people with not only lower lifetime earnings but also higher rates of criminality and chronic mental health issues. This represents a profound loss of human potential and a massive future liability for public services, including the justice system, the NHS, and the welfare state, fundamentally worsening the UK's long-term social and economic trajectory.


## Section 5: Navigating the Storm: An Integrated Outlook and Policy Pathways
The convergence of global pressures and domestic frailties places the United Kingdom's social conditions on a precarious path. The preceding analysis has detailed the scale of the challenges: a stagnant economy, deepening poverty, fracturing public services, and rising risks to social cohesion. This concluding section synthesises these findings into a set of plausible scenarios for the medium term and outlines a proactive policy agenda designed not merely to manage the next crisis, but to build the fundamental economic and social resilience required to navigate this new era of uncertainty.


### 5.1 Three Futures for Britain: Scenarios for the Medium Term
The medium-term outlook is not predetermined. It will be shaped by the interplay of external shocks and domestic policy choices. Three broad scenarios capture the range of potential futures.


- **Central Scenario (Muddling Through):** This scenario is based on the consensus forecasts of major economic institutions.[35, 36] It is characterised by a continuation of the recent trend of anaemic GDP growth, hovering around 0.7-1.0% per year. Inflation moderates but remains a persistent concern, while unemployment drifts slowly upwards to a peak of around 5.2%.[35, 36] In this future, there is no single catastrophic shock, but a slow, grinding deterioration of social conditions. The bleak projections outlined in Section 3 become reality: real incomes for the poorest half of the population continue to stagnate or fall, "very deep poverty" and destitution remain at crisis levels, and public services like the NHS are forced into a state of permanent crisis management, with degrading quality and access.[40, 43, 45] Social cohesion continues to fray under the strain of inequality and disappointment, but widespread, organised unrest is largely avoided. This is a future of managed decline, a "lost decade" for social progress.


- **Pessimistic Scenario (Stagflationary Shock):** This scenario is triggered by the materialisation of the "severe but plausible" risks identified by the Bank of England.[9] A major geopolitical event—such as an escalation of the war in Ukraine or a new conflict—causes a sharp spike in global energy and food prices. This supply shock hits an already fragile global economy, tipping it into a deep recession. For the UK, this translates into a severe stagflationary crisis: GDP contracts sharply, unemployment surges towards levels not seen since the 1980s, and a renewed burst of inflation erodes real incomes and forces the Bank of England to keep interest rates high. This economic collapse would catalyse a severe social crisis. The high levels of household debt would trigger a wave of mortgage defaults and personal insolvencies, leading to a housing market crash.[29, 31] The number of people in destitution would rise rapidly.[43] The confluence of acute economic pain and a fiscally paralysed state would create a high risk of major social unrest, echoing the events of 2011 but on a larger scale, and could fuel a significant surge in political radicalisation and support for extremist movements.[51, 54]


- **Optimistic Scenario (Resilient Recovery):** This scenario is less probable but still possible. It requires a fortunate combination of external developments and highly effective domestic policy. Globally, a de-escalation of trade tensions and geopolitical conflicts leads to a fall in energy prices and a restoration of business confidence, boosting global growth.[19, 35] Domestically, a new government implements a successful and credible long-term economic strategy that unlocks business investment and boosts the UK's chronically weak productivity growth. In this future, GDP growth accelerates towards a healthier 2% or more, creating jobs and driving real wage growth across the income distribution. The stronger economy provides the government with the fiscal space to begin reinvesting in public services and strengthening the social safety net. This would start to reverse the negative social trends, reducing poverty and improving the quality of public services. While the deep-seated structural challenges of regional inequality and social division would remain, this scenario offers a path away from crisis and towards a renewal of the social contract.


### 5.2 A New Social Contract: A Policy Agenda for Resilience
Given the gravity of the risks outlined in this report, a reactive, crisis-management approach to governance is wholly insufficient. The UK requires a proactive, long-term strategy aimed at fundamentally rebuilding its economic and social resilience. This necessitates a new social contract, underpinned by a policy agenda with four key pillars.


- **Economic Stabilisation and Renewal:** The immediate priority must be to establish macroeconomic stability. This requires a credible medium-term fiscal plan to put the public finances on a sustainable path, thereby reassuring markets and reducing the UK's borrowing costs. However, this consolidation must be carefully designed and back-loaded to avoid tipping a fragile economy into a deeper recession in the short term. This must be accompanied by a modern industrial strategy that moves beyond the sterile debate between austerity and unfunded spending. It should focus on boosting investment and productivity by tackling long-standing structural weaknesses in areas such as planning, skills, and infrastructure, and by identifying and supporting sectors where the UK can build a competitive advantage in the new geoeconomic landscape.


- **Social Security Reform to End Destitution:** The social safety net is the primary defence for households against economic shocks. Its current state is inadequate, as evidenced by the alarming rise in destitution. An urgent, root-and-branch reform is required with the explicit policy goal of eliminating the most extreme forms of poverty. This agenda must include, as a minimum: uprating benefits in line with the true cost of living; abolishing policies such as the two-child limit and the overall benefit cap, which have been identified as key drivers of deep poverty; and reforming Local Housing Allowances to ensure they reflect the actual cost of renting in different parts of the country.[40, 41, 43] This is not just a moral imperative but an economic one; a society scarred by destitution cannot be a productive or stable one.


- **Public Service Renewal:** A functional welfare state requires high-quality public services. The current model of annual, unpredictable funding and short-term crisis management is unsustainable. A new, long-term settlement is needed, particularly for the NHS and social care, that provides multi-year funding certainty to enable effective planning and investment.[45] This stable funding must be tied to a radical programme of reform, as advocated by institutions like The King's Fund, focused on shifting from a reactive "sickness service" to a proactive system based on prevention, moving care out of hospitals and into the community, and embracing digital transformation to improve efficiency and patient outcomes.[49, 62]


- **Bolstering Social Cohesion and Democratic Health:** The risks of political fragmentation and extremism are real and must be addressed directly. This requires a cross-government strategy to rebuild democratic resilience, as called for in the review by Dame Sara Khan.[55] Such a strategy would involve investing in local civic institutions, developing a robust national approach to tackling disinformation and conspiratorial narratives, and ensuring that all major policy decisions are stress-tested for their potential impact on social and regional inequalities. The goal must be to foster a politics of common purpose that can withstand the inevitable strains of this challenging new era, reinforcing the social fabric rather than allowing it to fray further.

The world is reconfiguring into distinct, competing economic blocs. A US-aligned bloc is deepening its internal trade and security ties, centered on North America, Europe, and key Indo-Pacific partners. Counterbalancing this is a strengthening, albeit asymmetrical, Sino-Russian axis, which serves as an economic lifeline for Moscow and a strategic partnership for Beijing. Navigating the space between these blocs is a set of crucial "connector" economies—including India, Brazil, and the nations of ASEAN—whose alignment is now a primary object of great power competition.


Global value chains are being rewired. The pursuit of "friend-shoring" and supply chain securitization is not leading to a simple "de-globalization" but rather a complex "re-globalization." Supply chains are lengthening and becoming more intricate as they are re-routed through these connector economies to create geopolitical buffers, introducing new dependencies and risks.


In Europe, a historic twin transition is underway. The simultaneous push to achieve energy independence through an accelerated green transition and to rearm on a scale not seen since the Cold War is placing immense strain on the continent's fiscal capacity, industrial base, and labor markets. The choices made to manage the collision of these two priorities will define Europe's economic and strategic trajectory for a generation.


Finally, the international monetary system is entering a period of flux. While the US dollar's dominance remains secure in the medium term due to the absence of a viable alternative, its long-term foundations of trust and predictability are being steadily eroded by internal US policy choices, including fiscal pressures and the unilateral use of economic tools. This is accelerating a slow but inexorable shift toward a more multipolar currency landscape.


For corporate strategists, investment managers, and government policymakers, the coming decade will demand a paradigm shift. Success will no longer be determined by optimizing for cost and efficiency within a single global system, but by navigating the frictions, risks, and opportunities of a fractured and contested world. This report provides the analytical framework necessary to understand this new geo-economic order and to formulate the resilient strategies required to thrive within it.


## Section 1: The 2025 Global Economic Landscape: Tenuous Resilience Amid Deepening Fissures
As of mid-2025, the global economy presents a paradoxical picture of tenuous resilience set against a backdrop of persistent uncertainty and deepening structural fissures. Headline growth forecasts from major international institutions, while appearing stable, diverge on the magnitude and drivers of economic activity, masking the profound impact of geopolitical shifts. The dominant theme is not one of robust recovery but of a world economy being reshaped by policy-driven fragmentation, with divergent paths emerging for major economic blocs and a clear tilt of risks to the downside.


### Baseline Forecasts - A Contradictory Picture
The primary international financial institutions offer differing assessments of the near-term outlook, with the divergence itself revealing the underlying tensions in the global system. The International Monetary Fund (IMF), in its July 2025 World Economic Outlook (WEO), projects a modest global growth rate of 3.0% for 2025, with a slight increase to 3.1% in 2026.[1, 2] This represents an upward revision from its April 2025 forecast, a seemingly optimistic signal. However, the drivers of this revision are telling: the IMF attributes the upgrade to short-term, distortionary factors such as "front-loading" of trade by businesses rushing to stockpile goods in anticipation of higher tariffs, lower-than-announced effective US tariff rates, improved financial conditions often linked to a weaker US dollar, and fiscal expansion in some major economies.[1, 3, 4]


In stark contrast, the World Bank's June 2025 Global Economic Prospects report paints a more somber picture. It projects that global growth will slow significantly to just 2.3% in 2025, a substantial downgrade from previous forecasts.[5, 6] The Bank explicitly links this pessimism to "a sharp rise in trade barriers and heightened policy uncertainty," which has prompted it to cut growth forecasts for nearly 70% of all economies worldwide.[5, 7] The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) echoes this caution, downgrading its global growth forecast to 2.9% for both 2025 and 2026. Its analysis similarly identifies rising trade barriers, tighter financial conditions, and mounting policy uncertainty as the primary drags on the global economy.[8, 9, 10] The Peterson Institute for International Economics (PIIE) offers an even more bearish outlook, projecting global growth at a mere 2.7% in 2025 and directly attributing the deteriorating forecast to major policy shifts in the United States, particularly the introduction of new, wide-ranging tariffs.[11, 12]


This divergence in forecasts is not a simple disagreement over data but rather a difference in temporal focus and interpretation. The IMF’s seemingly positive revision is driven by economic actors reacting to the very fragmentation and uncertainty that the World Bank, OECD, and PIIE identify as the core long-term problem. The "front-loading" of imports is a one-off phenomenon that borrows growth from the future, creating a temporary, artificial bump in 2025 activity. This short-term "sugar rush" of activity is, in fact, a leading indicator of the structural decay in the global trading system. The resilience is therefore illusory, masking the underlying weakness that will become more apparent once these temporary distortions wane. Decision-makers should thus treat the short-term growth figures with extreme caution, recognizing them as a harbinger of future weakness and volatility.

### Securitizing Supply Chains: "Friend-Shoring" and "Near-Shoring"
Complementing the tariff strategy is a comprehensive industrial policy aimed at securitizing critical supply chains. This is a "whole-of-government" effort designed to shift the logic of global production away from a singular focus on short-term cost efficiency and toward a multi-faceted calculus that prioritizes resilience, security, and alignment with US values.[22, 23] The policy seeks to proactively strengthen domestic manufacturing, particularly in strategic sectors like semiconductors, large-capacity batteries, and critical minerals, using incentives from legislation like the CHIPS and Science Act.[22, 23]


For supply chains that cannot be fully re-shored, the strategy is twofold. "Friend-shoring" involves building "trusted networks" by moving production to allies and partners who share US strategic interests and values, including standards on labor and the environment.[23] "Near-shoring" focuses on shifting supply chains closer to home, primarily to regional partners like Mexico and other countries in Central America, to reduce logistical vulnerabilities and transit times.[20, 22] While the rhetoric emphasizes trust and partnership, the practical effect, as documented in detailed trade flow analysis, is a significant reconfiguration of global trade based on geopolitical alignment.[24]


### Global Spillovers and Economic Consequences
The impact of this American recalibration is cascading across the global economy. US neighbors, Canada and Mexico, have been "hit hard" by the new trade actions, facing immense pressure to renegotiate the terms of their economic relationship with their largest partner.[11] The economic conflict with China has intensified dramatically, leading to a sharp decline in bilateral trade flows and forcing a strategic decoupling in key technology sectors.[21]


This policy-driven shock is forcing a rapid and costly adaptation from businesses worldwide. Companies are scrambling to protect their margins and supply lines by diversifying their supplier base away from China and toward new manufacturing hubs in Vietnam, India, and Malaysia; consolidating freight to manage shipping costs; and exploring nearshoring options.[20] This is leading to a quantifiable "re-routing" of global trade, a central finding of recent economic analysis.[13]


The spillovers are not confined to trade. They are creating a perfect storm for many emerging markets (EMs). The combination of inflationary US tariffs, a hawkish Federal Reserve response, and a consequently strong US dollar is severely tightening global financial conditions.[14, 16] This increases the burden of dollar-denominated debt, raises borrowing costs, and heightens the risk of financial crises in vulnerable nations.[17] The impact is heterogeneous, with EMs that have weaker fundamentals, less flexible exchange rates, and more open capital accounts facing the greatest peril.[16, 17, 25] Crucially, these shocks are transmitted not only through direct bilateral links with the US but also indirectly through a complex web of global trade and financial linkages, meaning no corner of the world economy is truly insulated.[17, 18]


The stated goal of "friend-shoring" is to enhance supply chain resilience by reducing dependency on a single geopolitical rival, namely China. However, a deeper analysis of trade data reveals a more complex and potentially perilous reality. The policy is not fostering a broadly diversified and resilient global production base but is instead creating new, highly concentrated dependencies on a small number of intermediary countries. The US has, in fact, significantly reduced its direct imports of manufactured goods from China.[24] This displaced trade has been redirected primarily to two key areas: Mexico, which surpassed China to become the largest US goods supplier in 2023, and the nations of ASEAN, with Vietnam being a standout beneficiary.[24] This appears, on the surface, to be a successful diversification.


However, a third-order effect is simultaneously occurring. While the US diversifies its final import sources, these new intermediary hubs—particularly in ASEAN—are seeing a marked *increase* in their own import concentration, driven by deepening ties with China for industrial inputs and components.[24] This reveals the true nature of the shift: the US is not truly "decoupling" from the vast Chinese industrial ecosystem. Instead, it is lengthening the supply chain, adding a geopolitical buffer by routing it through "friendly" third countries. This strategy may reduce direct bilateral political risk but at the cost of creating a more complex, opaque, and potentially less efficient global value chain that is now critically dependent on the political stability, infrastructure capacity, and policy choices of these intermediary nations. This creates new chokepoints and vulnerabilities, arguably exchanging one form of dependency for another.


Furthermore, the new US tariff policy is proving to be a double-edged sword for the long-term status of the US dollar. In the immediate term, the policy is inflationary. The tariffs raise the cost of imported goods, contributing to the "above target" US inflation noted by the IMF.[3] This, in turn, forces the Federal Reserve to maintain a hawkish monetary policy with higher interest rates to manage domestic price pressures, which supports the dollar's value through favorable interest rate differentials.[12] However, this short-term strength comes at the expense of the dollar's long-term structural foundations. The unilateral and often unpredictable nature of the tariff policy—what some have termed "policy whiplash"—erodes the trust and stability that are paramount for a global reserve currency.[26] This is compounded by the fact that the tariffs, by slowing growth and disrupting trade, could worsen the US fiscal position over the long run. The core threat to the dollar's hegemony has long been identified not as an external challenger but as internal US dysfunction, particularly unsustainable fiscal policy.[27] The current trade strategy creates a dangerous feedback loop: it strengthens the dollar in the short term for the "wrong" reasons (fighting policy-induced inflation) while simultaneously weakening the very attributes of reliable governance and fiscal stability that underpin its global role. This increases the risk profile of holding the dollar, potentially prompting central banks and major investors to accelerate their long-term diversification strategies, not because a superior alternative has suddenly emerged, but because the issuer of the world's reserve currency is itself becoming a primary source of global uncertainty.


## Section 3: Europe's Strategic Realignment: Energy, Defense, and the Search for Autonomy
The war in Ukraine has served as a historical catalyst, forcing a profound and simultaneous transformation in European strategic thinking. Across the continent, governments are undertaking two monumental tasks: re-engineering their energy systems to break free from Russian dependency and embarking on the most significant military buildup since the end of the Cold War. This dual transition, driven by the imperatives of security and sovereignty, is reshaping Europe's economic landscape, creating immense challenges, and setting the continent on a new, uncertain trajectory toward strategic autonomy.


### 3.1. The Energy Pivot: From Dependency to Diversification and Decarbonization
The weaponization of energy by Russia in the wake of its full-scale invasion of Ukraine triggered a severe energy crisis in Europe. The continent, which had relied on Russia for approximately 40% of its natural gas supplies, was confronted with unprecedented supply disruptions and extreme price volatility, threatening economic stability and social cohesion.[28, 29, 30]


The response has been a rapid and decisive pivot away from Russian energy. This strategic decoupling involves two parallel tracks. The first is diversification of supply. European nations have moved swiftly to substitute Russian fossil fuels with imports from a range of other suppliers, including Norway, the United States (particularly for Liquefied Natural Gas), Qatar, and Algeria.[30] The second, and more structurally significant, track is the dramatic acceleration of the continent's green transition. What was once primarily a climate policy imperative is now viewed as a core component of national and energy security.


Governments across the EU now recognize that accelerating the shift to renewable energy offers the "best longer term opportunity to regain competitiveness while bolstering energy security and keeping energy costs low".[31] This has translated into a surge of investment. Global clean energy investment is projected to reach $2.2 trillion in 2025, a figure that is double the investment in fossil fuels, with Europe being a key driver of this trend.[32] By 2025, renewables are forecast to generate 35% of the world's electricity, with the EU accounting for 15% of the additional renewable generation capacity coming online.[33] This forced energy transition is expected to have lasting positive environmental effects. An IMF analysis projects that by 2030, the cumulative impact of the war-induced energy shift will reduce the EU's overall greenhouse gas emissions by approximately 5% compared to pre-war trendlines, making the bloc's climate targets "slightly less costly" to achieve.[30]


### 3.2. The Return of Geopolitics: Rearmament and its Economic Consequences
Concurrent with its energy pivot, Europe is undergoing a military reawakening. The war in Ukraine shattered the post-Cold War "peace dividend" and spurred an unprecedented surge in defense spending. Global military expenditure witnessed its steepest year-on-year increase in 2024 since the end of the Cold War, with Europe serving as the primary engine of this growth.[34] Aggregate defense spending in the EU rose from a low of 1.1% of GDP in 2014 to 1.3% in 2023, and is estimated to have finally reached the long-standing NATO target of 2% of GDP in 2024.[35] Countries on NATO's eastern flank, such as Poland, have led the way, with spending reaching 4.2% of GDP.[34]


This surge is set to accelerate. In a landmark shift, NATO members have endorsed a new, far more ambitious spending benchmark: a commitment to invest 5% of GDP annually in a combination of "core defence requirements as well as defence- and security-related spending" by the year 2035.[36, 37] Reaching this target would represent a monumental fiscal effort, requiring EU NATO members to increase their annual defense-related budgets by an estimated €613 billion.[38]


The economic consequences of this rearmament are a subject of intense debate. Proponents point to a potential positive growth multiplier. The European Commission, for instance, has simulated that an additional 1.5% of GDP in defense spending could lift the EU's overall GDP by 0.5% above its baseline trajectory by 2028.[35] Research from the Kiel Institute suggests the economic benefits could be even greater, with potential GDP growth of 0.9% to 1.5%, provided the spending is strategically targeted at high-tech, domestically produced armaments that generate significant R&D spillovers into the wider economy.[39]


However, this optimistic view is countered by significant concerns about the "guns versus butter" trade-off. Critics warn that this massive reallocation of public funds toward the military will inevitably crowd out critical investments in other essential areas. The estimated annual investment gap to meet the EU's existing green and social goals—including climate mitigation, healthcare, and housing—is between €375 billion and €526 billion, a sum of the same order of magnitude as the proposed increases in defense spending.[38] This creates a stark political choice between funding "weapons over addressing climate breakdown and social fragility".[38] Furthermore, the rapid spending surge carries substantial risks of procurement inefficiencies, price gouging, and fiscal strain, especially for member states already grappling with high levels of public debt.[36]

A deeper analysis reveals that Europe's twin transitions—the green energy revolution and the military rearmament—are on a direct collision course for the continent's finite resources. The simultaneous pursuit of these two capital-intensive, technologically demanding goals creates an unsustainable competition for fiscal space, skilled labor, industrial capacity, and critical raw materials. The green transition requires trillions of euros in investment for renewable energy generation, grid modernization, and energy storage solutions.[32] The military transition demands hundreds of billions of euros in new annual spending.[38] Both sectors draw heavily on similar industrial inputs, such as advanced materials, semiconductors, and high-precision manufacturing, and compete for the same limited pool of engineers, data scientists, and specialized technicians. Analysts have explicitly highlighted this inherent trade-off, noting that the financial resources required for green and social priorities are of the same scale as the proposed defense increases, and they warn of the danger of tying up "labour, industrial supply chains and technical capacity that are already stretched".[38]


This resource collision means that Europe cannot achieve both transitions at their currently planned speed and scale without a fundamental overhaul of its economic and political model. This reality will likely force a difficult choice among three potential paths over the coming decade: first, one transition could be implicitly or explicitly prioritized over the other, with security imperatives potentially slowing the pace of climate action; second, both could be pursued simultaneously but inefficiently, leading to significant bottlenecks, persistent inflation, and a failure to meet key targets in either domain; or third, the crisis could catalyze a new, more integrated European approach, involving coordinated industrial policy and common debt issuance to manage the resource clash, thereby deepening European political and economic integration.


Furthermore, there is a central irony in Europe's quest for strategic autonomy. While the long-term objective of increased defense spending is to build a more sovereign and capable European security posture, the immediate, short-term effect is likely to be an increase in its reliance on the United States. The European defense industrial base, having atrophied during the decades of the peace dividend, lacks the capacity to absorb the sudden and massive surge in demand for military hardware.[36] This "mismatch between rising demand and supply" is a widely recognized risk that will almost certainly push European nations to procure "off-the-shelf" equipment from the world's dominant arms supplier: the US defense industry.[36] Currently, around 80% of European defense procurement originates from outside the EU, primarily from the United States.[39] Therefore, the initial phase of Europe's rearmament (roughly 2025-2030) will paradoxically deepen its security integration with, and increase its financial flows to, the United States. A truly independent European defense identity and industrial base will take a decade or more to construct. In the medium term, this increased reliance could become a significant point of friction if US and European strategic priorities were to diverge, complicating the very search for autonomy that prompted the spending in the first place.


## Section 4: The Geometry of a Fragmented World: Reconfiguring Trade and the Rise of Economic Blocs
The combined shocks emanating from North America and Eastern Europe are not merely disrupting the global economy; they are fundamentally reconfiguring its structure. The long-dominant paradigm of hyper-globalization, driven by the relentless pursuit of efficiency, is giving way to a new era of geo-economic fragmentation. This shift is characterized by a "policy-driven reversal of global economic integration," where strategic competition and the securitization of supply chains have supplanted liberalized trade as the organizing principles of the international system.[15] This process is not a simple "de-globalization" but a more complex "re-globalization," where trade and investment flows are being rewired along geopolitical lines, giving rise to distinct and competing economic blocs.


### The End of Hyper-Globalization
There is a broad consensus among leading economic observers that the ambition to construct an ever more deeply integrated world economy has been abandoned.[15] Trade growth has slowed precipitously, and the logic of maximizing supply chain efficiency has been superseded by the imperative of ensuring resilience.[15] This backlash is fueled by a confluence of factors: strategic competition between great powers, fears of technology theft through investment flows, and concerns about the resilience of supply chains for vital goods.[15] The result is a measurable shift in the geometry of global trade. Analysis by the McKinsey Global Institute shows a sharp reduction in the "geopolitical distance" of trade, meaning countries are increasingly trading with partners that are more geopolitically aligned, as measured by factors like UN voting patterns.[24] The potential economic cost of this fragmentation is substantial, with the World Economic Forum and NERA estimating that global GDP could be reduced by 0.5% to as much as 5% in more extreme scenarios of disintegration.[40]


### Mapping the New Blocs
As the old, integrated system fractures, a new, more fragmented map of the world economy is emerging, organized around at least three distinct poles.


First, a **U.S.-Aligned Bloc** is consolidating. The United States is actively reducing its trade with geopolitical rivals like China and redirecting it toward allies and partners.[24] This includes deepening economic ties within North America, with Mexico now serving as the largest single source of goods imports to the US.[24] It also involves strengthening partnerships with key Indo-Pacific economies, particularly the nations of ASEAN, and a renewed transatlantic economic relationship with Europe, which has significantly increased its trade with the US, especially for energy, following its strategic decoupling from Russia.[24]


Second, a formidable, if highly asymmetrical, **Sino-Russian Axis** has been cemented by the war in Ukraine. This partnership functions as an economic "lifeline" for Russia and a strategic asset for China.[41, 42] The relationship is indispensable for Moscow, which now relies on China as its primary market for energy exports and its main source of the industrial goods, technology, and consumer products needed to withstand Western sanctions and sustain its economy.[42, 43] For Beijing, the partnership is beneficial but not essential; it provides a secure and discounted source of vital commodities and aligns with its broader ideological goal of challenging the Western-led international order.[42, 43] This economic integration is deepening rapidly, with bilateral trade surging to record highs post-2022 and an increasing share of that trade being settled in yuan and rubles to bypass the US dollar and mitigate the impact of sanctions.[41, 43]


Third, a diverse group of **"Connector" Economies** is rising in importance, not as a coherent bloc, but as the crucial nodes and bridges navigating the space between the two primary poles. Nations like India, Brazil, Vietnam, and Indonesia are actively strengthening trade relationships across the geopolitical spectrum.[24] India, for instance, is simultaneously expanding its energy imports from Russia while maintaining robust and growing trade ties with the United States and Europe.[24] The ASEAN region has become particularly pivotal, functioning as an essential intermediary in global value chains. It increasingly imports industrial inputs and components from China, processes them, and then exports the finished goods to the United States and its allies, effectively serving as a conduit between the two largest, and increasingly estranged, economic powers.[24]

The prevailing narrative of "de-globalization"—a simple retreat from international economic engagement—is a misleading oversimplification of the current reality. A more accurate description is "re-globalization," a process where the world economy is actively reorganizing into parallel, yet still interconnected, systems. Global trade volumes are not collapsing; they are being re-routed.[13] In fact, the average *geographic* distance of trade has continued its slow but steady increase over the past decade, a direct refutation of the idea that widespread nearshoring is taking hold on a global scale.[24] What has changed dramatically is the *geopolitical* distance of trade. The United States, Europe, and China are all trading less with their most geopolitically distant partners and more with those inside their respective spheres of alignment.[24] While the US deepens its economic ties with allies in Europe and the Indo-Pacific, China's trade has pivoted decisively toward developing economies in the "Global South," which now account for the majority of its total trade, and toward its strategic partner, Russia.[24] This bifurcation implies that businesses, investors, and governments must now learn to operate in a world with two increasingly distinct sets of standards, two competing technology ecosystems (for payments, data, and telecommunications), and two spheres of political and economic influence. The greatest challenges and opportunities will lie at the interface between these two parallel systems, where friction, competition, and the potential for miscalculation will be highest.


In this new geo-economic landscape, the strategic importance of the "connector" economies has grown exponentially. Middle powers like India, Brazil, Vietnam, and Indonesia are no longer peripheral actors in a unipolar system but have become the indispensable bridges—and potential chokepoints—between the two major blocs. Their role is the new strategic prize in the great power competition. McKinsey's analysis, for example, explicitly highlights ASEAN's emerging function as an "intermediate step" in the reconfigured US-China value chain, processing Chinese inputs for American consumers.[24] Similarly, India's carefully managed strategy of multi-alignment, engaging economically with all sides, positions it as a pivotal swing state in the global order.[24] Both the US "friend-shoring" policy and China's "Belt and Road Initiative" can be understood as competing grand strategies designed to secure the economic and political alignment of these critical connector nations. As these countries become more integral to the functioning of both blocs' supply chains, their internal political stability, infrastructure capacity, and macroeconomic policies are transformed from domestic concerns into sources of global systemic risk. A political crisis, infrastructure failure, or economic downturn in a key connector country could now trigger cascading disruptions across both of the world's major economic systems. This new reality demands a fundamental shift in risk analysis, focusing intensely on the internal dynamics of these middle powers. For corporations, the next generation of supply chain resilience will require not just diversifying *to* these connector economies, but diversifying *among* them to mitigate the risk of a single point of failure in this new, fragmented world.


## Section 5: The Future of the Global Monetary Order: Cracks in the Dollar's Hegemony?
The profound shifts in trade and geopolitics are inevitably raising fundamental questions about the future of the international monetary system and the long-standing hegemony of the US dollar. The analysis as of mid-2025 suggests a two-speed reality. In the medium term, the dollar's dominance remains secure, primarily due to the lack of any viable, large-scale alternative. However, over the longer term, the very policies being pursued by the United States are steadily eroding the foundations of trust and stability upon which the dollar's "exorbitant privilege" rests, accelerating a slow but structural shift toward a more fragmented and multipolar currency landscape.


### The Dollar's Enduring Dominance (The "TINA" Argument)
Despite widespread discussion of "de-dollarization," the US dollar remains the world's undisputed primary reserve currency. It accounts for approximately 57% of all international foreign-exchange reserves held by central banks, a share that, while down from a decade ago, still vastly exceeds that of any competitor.[44] The euro follows at a distant second with around 21%, while the Chinese renminbi's role, though growing, remains in the low single digits.[44, 45]


This dominance is underpinned by the "TINA" principle: There Is No Alternative. No other currency or capital market can match the unique combination of attributes offered by the US dollar and its associated financial system. The depth, breadth, and liquidity of US capital markets, particularly the market for US Treasury bonds, are unparalleled, providing the only truly global safe harbor for international capital in times of stress.[46] Furthermore, the dollar's role extends far beyond reserves. It is the primary medium of exchange for global trade and the dominant unit of account for invoicing international transactions. Even when trade occurs between two other countries, the transaction is often priced and settled in dollars, a reality that grants the United States significant economic leverage and extraterritorial influence through its ability to control access to the dollar-based financial system.[26]


### The Long-Term Threats: Internal Dysfunction, Not External Challengers
Paradoxically, the most credible and potent threats to the dollar's long-term dominance are not emerging from external challengers like the euro or the renminbi, but from within the United States itself.[27] Expert analysis, notably the views of economist Kenneth Rogoff, points to internal US policy choices and political dysfunction as the primary corrosive forces.[27]


The central concern is the trajectory of US fiscal policy. A political system described as being "addicted to public debt" is pursuing a path of unsustainable fiscal deficits, which could, over the next decade, lead to a crisis of confidence in the US government's ability to service its obligations.[27] Such a scenario would have devastating consequences for the dollar's brand, potentially triggering a combination of higher interest rates, persistent inflation, and severe financial instability.[27]


This fiscal risk is compounded by the "policy whiplash" and the increasing "weaponization" of the dollar. The erratic and unilateral use of tariffs, coupled with the aggressive use of financial sanctions, fundamentally undermines the attributes of predictability and political neutrality that are essential for a global reserve currency.[26, 45] These actions incentivize other countries, particularly geopolitical rivals, to actively seek out and build alternatives to the dollar system to reduce their vulnerability. This is manifesting in a slow but steady diversification of central bank reserves away from the dollar and into other assets, such as gold or a broader basket of currencies.[45] Adding to this uncertainty is speculation around a potential "Mar-a-Lago Accord"—a hypothetical, politically driven strategy to deliberately weaken the dollar to boost US export competitiveness—which further erodes its credibility as a stable store of value.[46]


### The Slow Path to a Multipolar Currency System
The erosion of the dollar's status is not expected to lead to its replacement by a new single hegemon. Instead, the consensus view is that the world is on a slow and gradual path toward a more "multipolar reserve currency environment".[26] In this future landscape, the dollar would likely remain the most important currency but would function as a "first among a triumvirate," sharing influence with a more prominent euro and a regionalized Chinese renminbi.[27]


This diversification is already underway. The BRICS nations (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa) are actively pursuing strategies to reduce their reliance on the dollar for bilateral trade and investment.[26] The "yuan-ization" of the burgeoning Russia-China trade, where transactions are increasingly settled in their national currencies, is a concrete example of this trend toward building bilateral financial channels that bypass the dollar system entirely.[41] In Asia, there is an expectation that regional central banks will continue to gradually shift reserves out of the dollar and into a more diversified basket that includes other major currencies and those of key regional trading partners.[44]


A critical shift in the dollar's behavior is signaling this changing reality. The "Dollar Smile" theory has long been a useful framework for understanding the currency's movements, positing that the dollar tends to strengthen in two distinct scenarios: when the US economy is outperforming the rest of the world (the right side of the smile), and during periods of major global risk and uncertainty, when capital flees to the safety of US assets (the left side of the smile).[46] However, analysis of recent market behavior in 2025 indicates that this pattern is breaking down. The dollar has begun to exhibit uncharacteristic weakness even during episodes of global risk aversion.[46] The reason for this is fundamental: the traditional "safe haven" effect relies on the premise that the United States is a source of stability in a turbulent world. Yet, increasingly, the source of global economic uncertainty *is* US policy itself—specifically, the unpredictability of its trade and fiscal policies.[11, 46] Consequently, capital is no longer automatically flowing to the US as a default safe harbor. Instead, investors are beginning to look toward other regions that may offer either better growth prospects or greater policy stability.[46] Financial analysts now believe the dollar smile "curve has likely flattened," meaning it now requires far more significant shifts in either global optimism or risk aversion to drive meaningful dollar appreciation.[46] This change has profound implications. If the dollar's role as the ultimate risk-off asset becomes less reliable, global asset allocators and central bank reserve managers will be forced to fundamentally rethink their hedging and diversification strategies, which will, in turn, accelerate the slow, structural move away from a unipolar reliance on US assets.


Furthermore, the narrative of de-dollarization is often misconstrued as a direct contest where the Chinese renminbi must "replace" the dollar. This framing misses the more subtle and strategic objective of China and its partners. Their goal is not necessarily to achieve global monetary hegemony but rather to construct a viable, parallel financial architecture that can operate independently of the US-controlled system, thereby reducing their vulnerability to American sanctions and financial pressure. The increasing settlement of Russia-China trade in their own currencies is a prime example of this strategy in action; it is not designed to be a new global standard but a bilateral system that is "good enough" to insulate their essential economic relationship from external interference.[41] The development of alternative payment messaging systems (like China's Cross-Border Interbank Payment System, CIPS) and the exploration of central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) are further building blocks of this parallel infrastructure. These systems do not need to match the depth, liquidity, or efficiency of the dollar-based network to be strategically effective. They merely need to be functional enough to allow for critical trade and financial flows to continue in the event that a country or group of countries is cut off from the primary global system. The world is therefore heading toward a two-track financial system: one, the deep, liquid, and globally integrated dollar-based system; the other, a less efficient but sanctions-resilient, yuan-centric network. Over the next decade, countries and multinational corporations will increasingly face the complex choice of which system to prioritize, or bear the significant costs and operational complexities of attempting to operate within both.


## Section 6: Compounding Crises: Persistent Vulnerabilities in the Global System
The primary geo-economic shocks of US protectionism and the war in Ukraine are not occurring in a vacuum. They are cascading through the international system, interacting with and severely exacerbating pre-existing global vulnerabilities. This dynamic is creating a series of compounding crises, most acutely in the realms of global food security and the debt sustainability of the developing world. These issues are no longer just humanitarian or developmental concerns; they have become central arenas for geopolitical competition and major sources of systemic risk.


### The Enduring Global Food Security Crisis
Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine unleashed what has been described as the "greatest military-related increase in global food insecurity in at least a century".[47] Ukraine, a breadbasket for the world, has seen its agricultural sector systematically targeted and degraded. The war has inflicted billions of dollars in damage to farms, processing facilities, and export infrastructure, while rendering vast tracts of farmland unusable due to landmines and the destruction of critical irrigation systems like the Kakhovka Dam.[47] As a result, Ukraine's exports of key commodities like wheat and corn are projected to remain significantly below pre-war levels for the foreseeable future.[47]


While global commodity food prices have receded from the dramatic peaks seen in 2022, this masks a persistent crisis at the local level. In many low- and lower-middle-income countries, domestic food price inflation remains dangerously high, with rates exceeding 15% and, in some cases, even 30%.[47, 48] This is driven by currency depreciations against the strong US dollar, high energy and transport costs, and disruptions to local supply chains. Consequently, the number of people facing hunger globally, while having declined slightly from its pandemic-era peak, remains stubbornly above pre-pandemic levels, with hunger continuing to rise in most subregions of Africa and Western Asia.[48]


This crisis is being actively exploited. Russia has adopted a dual strategy of simultaneously degrading Ukraine's export capacity while leveraging its own record agricultural output as a tool of foreign policy. It is strategically using its own food exports to "replace Ukrainian grain" on world markets, often offering subsidized or free shipments to court influence and build dependencies in the Global South.[47]


### Debt Distress in the Developing World
For many emerging market and developing economies (EMDEs), the current global environment represents a perfect storm. The World Bank has warned that these nations face a "very nasty combination" of lower prices for their commodity exports and increasingly volatile global markets.[6] This squeeze on export revenues is occurring just as their expenditures are soaring.


The confluence of factors analyzed in previous sections—slowing global growth, rising protectionism in key export markets, and tighter global financial conditions driven by US monetary policy—is severely straining public finances across the developing world.[14, 16, 17] A persistently strong US dollar increases the real burden of servicing the enormous stock of dollar-denominated debt that many of these countries hold. The World Bank notes that the fiscal space needed for critical investments in development and poverty reduction is being rapidly consumed by rising debt service costs, a problem compounded by declining levels of international donor support.[5]


The convergence of these pressures is creating a trap that threatens to reverse years of economic progress. Food security is no longer merely a humanitarian issue but has been transformed into a central arena for great power competition. Russia's strategy of simultaneously destroying Ukrainian agricultural supply and deploying its own subsidized grain as a tool of coercive diplomacy is a template for a new geopolitics of food. By strategically targeting Ukraine's agricultural economy, Russia not only harms its adversary but also creates vacuums in the global food market that it can then fill, thereby gaining leverage over food-importing nations in Africa, the Middle East, and beyond.[47] This transforms food from a simple commodity into a strategic asset, much like how Russia has historically used its energy resources. This new reality demands that the United States and its allies fundamentally rethink their approach. Food aid and agricultural development can no longer be viewed as policies separate from their broader geopolitical strategy. Effectively countering this new form of coercive influence will require a more proactive and integrated "food diplomacy." This must include robust efforts to shore up Ukraine's agricultural production and export capacity, providing financial and logistical support to vulnerable food-importing nations to help them resist Russian leverage, and working to ensure the overall stability and transparency of global food markets.


Simultaneously, the grim prospect of a "lost decade" for development is becoming an increasingly plausible reality for a significant portion of the globe. The convergence of multiple crises—slowing global growth, the fragmentation of trade, crushing debt service costs, and persistent food and energy price volatility—is creating a formidable development trap for many low- and middle-income countries. The World Bank has issued a stark warning that, on current trends, it could take the developing world (excluding China) "about two decades to recoup the economic losses of the 2020s," with per capita income growth falling far below the average seen in the first two decades of the 21st century.[6, 7] The traditional engine of development—export-led growth—is being choked off by the rise of protectionism and the slowdown in global trade.[15] At the same time, the fiscal capacity required to invest in domestic priorities like education, healthcare, infrastructure, and climate adaptation is being systematically eroded by ever-higher payments on foreign-held debt.[5] This confluence of negative forces significantly raises the risk of a wave of sovereign debt crises across the developing world in the latter half of the 2020s. Such crises would not be contained events; they would likely trigger significant political instability, fuel new migration pressures, and lead to a further retreat from global cooperation, creating a vicious cycle of fragmentation, poverty, and crisis that would have repercussions for the entire international system.


## Section 7: Strategic Outlook and Recommendations for 2026-2035
The global economic order is undergoing a fundamental transformation, driven by the intersecting forces of geopolitical competition, technological change, and the securitization of economic life. The period from 2026 to 2035 will not be a return to the pre-2020 status quo, but the consolidation of a new, more fragmented, and contested environment. Navigating this new landscape will require a paradigm shift in strategy for corporations, investors, and governments alike.


### The Shape of the World Economy to 2035
Synthesizing the analysis of this report, the world economy over the next decade is projected to be defined by the following key structural features:


- **A Structurally Slower, More Inflationary, and More Volatile Global Economy:** The efficiency losses from trade fragmentation, coupled with the costs of the green and military transitions and persistent policy uncertainty, will weigh on potential growth. Divergent national policies will lead to more volatile and de-synchronized business cycles and persistent, domestically-driven inflationary pressures in certain blocs.


- **A Bipolar or Tripolar System of Competing Economic Blocs:** The world will be organized around a US-aligned bloc and a Sino-Russian axis. These blocs will not be hermetically sealed but will feature distinct technological standards, financial systems, and regulatory approaches. A vast "contested middle" of connector economies will navigate the space between them, acting as both bridges and potential flashpoints.


- **A Shift in Corporate Strategy from "Just-in-Time" to "Just-in-Case":** The dominant corporate logic will shift from maximizing short-term cost efficiency to ensuring long-term resilience and security. This will involve building redundancy, diversifying supply chains beyond single-country concentrations, and accepting higher baseline costs as a necessary insurance premium against disruption.


- **A Financial System with a Still-Dominant but Weakening Dollar:** The US dollar will remain the primary global reserve and transaction currency through the next decade due to the lack of a scalable alternative. However, its long-term position will be steadily eroded by US policy choices and the rise of a parallel, yuan-centric financial network designed for sanctions resilience. This will lead to a more multipolar and less stable currency environment.


### Recommendations for Corporate Strategy

- **Elevate Geopolitical Risk to a Core Competency:** Geopolitical analysis can no longer be a niche function firewalled within a government affairs department. It must be embedded into the core processes of corporate strategy, capital allocation, and risk management. C-suites and boards must develop the capability to understand and model the impact of geopolitical shifts on their markets, supply chains, and competitive landscape.


- **Implement Radical Supply Chain Diversification:** The first wave of supply chain diversification moved production from China to single alternative locations like Vietnam or Mexico. The next wave must be more sophisticated. Strategy must move beyond single-country diversification to a multi-regional, multi-tier model that accounts for the risk of disruption *within* the connector economies themselves. This involves mapping dependencies not just on Tier 1 suppliers, but on Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers as well, and building regional production hubs for regional markets.


- **Scenario Plan for a Fragmented World:** Businesses must move beyond a single baseline forecast and develop robust strategies that can withstand multiple plausible futures. This involves stress-testing business models, balance sheets, and market-entry strategies against distinct scenarios of fragmentation—for instance, a "moderate fragmentation" scenario with persistent tariffs and a "severe fragmentation" scenario involving bifurcated technology standards and capital controls.


### Recommendations for Investment Strategy

- **Re-evaluate and Redefine Sovereign Risk:** Traditional models of sovereign risk, heavily weighted toward fiscal metrics and credit ratings, are no longer sufficient. Investment frameworks must be updated to incorporate new, critical variables, including a country's geopolitical alignment, its position within emerging trade blocs, its vulnerability to supply chain shocks, and its exposure to coercive economic measures.


- **Adopt Active Currency Hedging in a Multipolar World:** The "flattening of the dollar smile" signals that the dollar's role as a reliable safe-haven asset is becoming less certain. Asset managers can no longer assume the dollar will automatically appreciate during global risk-off events, particularly if the US is the source of the risk. This necessitates a more active approach to managing currency risk and a strategic, long-term diversification of currency exposure as other blocs, particularly in Asia, gain traction.


- **Identify the "Winners" of Fragmentation:** Geo-economic fragmentation will create clear winners and losers. Capital should be strategically allocated to companies, sectors, and countries that are well-positioned to benefit from the new geometry of trade. This includes logistics and manufacturing firms in key connector economies, producers of critical commodities and technologies essential for both the green and military transitions, and companies that specialize in providing resilience and security solutions.


### Policy Considerations for Governments

- **Invest Strategically in "Connectors":** Western governments must move beyond rhetoric and develop a coherent, long-term strategy of economic partnership with the key connector economies of the "contested middle." This should involve not just trade agreements but also substantial public and private investment in infrastructure, human capital, and institutional capacity to build resilient supply networks and offer a compelling alternative to the models proposed by strategic rivals.


- **Manage the Costs of Fragmentation through Renewed Multilateralism:** While the old model of globalization is over, a complete descent into anarchic protectionism would be economically catastrophic for all. Governments should foster multilateral dialogue aimed at establishing new "rules of the road" for the 21st-century economy. This could include agreements on managing subsidies, coordinating on export controls for sensitive technologies, and creating frameworks for resolving trade disputes in a world without a fully functioning WTO appellate body. The goal is not to reverse fragmentation but to manage its costs and prevent a race to the bottom.


- **Prioritize Domestic Resilience and Competitiveness:** In an era of strategic competition, long-term security and prosperity will be determined by domestic strength. Governments must pursue policies that enhance economic competitiveness, boost productivity growth, and manage the significant fiscal burdens of the new security environment. This includes investing in education and R&D, streamlining regulation to foster innovation, and developing credible, long-term plans to ensure fiscal sustainability.
