Yes, I'm familiar with the concept of persistent lights in the context of maps and photographs.  It sounds like you're describing the phenomenon where bright lights, especially at night, can appear as streaks or blooms in images.  This is a common issue, and it relates to a few different factors:


- **Overexposure:**  Bright lights can overwhelm the camera sensor, leading to overexposure in that area.  Instead of a sharp point of light, the excess energy spreads out, creating a larger, blurred, or streaked effect.


- **Lens Flare:**  Light entering the camera lens can reflect off internal elements, creating artifacts like streaks, starbursts, or ghosting.  This is particularly noticeable with bright, point sources of light against a dark background.


- **Long Exposure:** When taking long exposures at night, moving lights (like car headlights) will create streaks across the image as they move during the exposure time.  This is often used intentionally for artistic effect.


- **Atmospheric Conditions:**  Haze, fog, or other atmospheric particles can scatter light, making it appear more diffuse and contributing to the blooming effect.


- **Sensor Bloom:**  In some digital cameras, very bright light can cause the sensor to "bloom," where the charge from the overexposed pixels spills over into neighboring pixels.  This creates a larger, softer glow around the light source.


So, in maps or photographs, these persistent lights can manifest as:


- **Streaks of light along roads:**  This is often due to car headlights during long exposures.
- **Blooms or halos around streetlights or other bright lights:**  This is usually a combination of overexposure, lens flare, and potentially atmospheric effects.
- **Starburst patterns around bright light sources:**  This is a common type of lens flare.
