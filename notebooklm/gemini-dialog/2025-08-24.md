# An Unconventional Guide to Lanzarote: 10 Days of Art, Adventure, and Otherworldly Landscapes for Sandra & Robert
## An Introduction to the Island of Fire and Art
Lanzarote is an island of profound and beautiful contradictions. To arrive here is to step onto a canvas where nature, in its most raw and violent form, has been met with the most sensitive and harmonious of human touches. It is a place that defies the easy categorisation of a sun-soaked Canary Island getaway. Instead, it offers something far more elemental and memorable: a journey through a landscape that feels, at times, more lunar than terrestrial, a scorched geography born from fire and sculpted by wind, which has in turn inspired a legacy of art that is inseparable from the very rock it stands on.[1, 2]


The island as Sandra and Robert will experience it was forged in a cataclysm. Between 1730 and 1736, a series of devastating volcanic eruptions convulsed the land, burying entire villages under a sea of lava and ash and creating the hauntingly beautiful, Mars-like terrain of what are now known as the *Montañas del Fuego*, or Fire Mountains.[3, 4, 5] This is the island’s primal story, a history written in basalt and black sand. For centuries, this dramatic geology was simply a fact of life, a challenging environment to be endured. But in the 20th century, one man saw it not as a challenge, but as a masterpiece waiting to be framed.


That man was César Manrique. To understand modern Lanzarote is to understand his vision.[6] An artist, architect, and environmentalist born on the island, Manrique returned in the 1960s after a successful career in America to find his homeland on the cusp of a tourism boom.[4] He saw both the economic promise and the existential threat this posed. Fearing that Lanzarote would succumb to the soulless, high-rise development that had scarred other beautiful coastlines, he became the island's passionate guardian angel.[6] He lobbied tirelessly for planning regulations that would preserve the island’s traditional aesthetic—low-slung, whitewashed buildings with pops of green or blue woodwork—and successfully prevented the construction of a single high-rise hotel outside of the capital, Arrecife.[4]


This act of preservation, however, was only half of his genius. Manrique understood that to protect the island, he needed to give it an alternative, sustainable economy. His solution was a paradox of visionary brilliance: he would turn the island’s most unique natural features into the very tourist attractions that would fund its preservation. He didn't build *on* the landscape; he built *within* it. He took collapsed lava tubes, volcanic caves, and abandoned quarries and transformed them into breathtaking centres of art, culture, and tourism, each one a dialogue between his artistic intervention and the overwhelming power of nature.[3, 6] When Sandra and Robert visit Jameos del Agua or the Mirador del Río, they are not just seeing a tourist site; they are participating in Manrique’s grand, island-wide project of conservation through curation. Their journey will be a pilgrimage through this living gallery, a testament to the idea that humanity’s greatest creations are those that celebrate, rather than conquer, the natural world.


## The Manrique Legacy: A Pilgrimage Through Art and Nature
To follow the trail of César Manrique is to see Lanzarote through its most loving and imaginative eyes. His major works, the official Art, Culture, and Tourism Centres (CACT), are more than just destinations; they are immersive experiences designed to awaken the senses and foster a deep connection with the island's volcanic soul.[1]


### Jameos del Agua - The Enchanted Volcanic Grotto
Imagine descending a winding stone staircase into the earth, the bright Canary sun giving way to the cool, echoing quiet of a vast cavern. This is the entrance to Jameos del Agua, arguably Manrique’s most breathtaking creation.[3] A "jameo" is a local word for the opening left by the collapsed roof of a volcanic tunnel. Here, Manrique took a section of the six-kilometre-long lava tube created by the eruption of the Monte Corona volcano and transformed it into a subterranean paradise.[3, 7]


The first space, Jameo Chico, holds the site's most precious secret: a natural underground salt lake, its water crystal clear, which is home to a unique and endemic species of blind albino crab, *Munidopsis polymorpha*.[1, 8, 9] These tiny, pearlescent creatures are a symbol of the island’s fragility and uniqueness. The atmosphere here is one of hushed reverence; visitors speak in whispers, mesmerised by the sight of these delicate lifeforms that have evolved in total darkness.


Emerging from this grotto, the path leads into the Jameo Grande, a vast open-air cavern that delivers a moment of pure architectural awe. Here, Manrique created a stunning, pristine white swimming pool, its turquoise water a shocking and beautiful contrast against the rugged black basalt of the cave walls.[3] Surrounded by lush palm trees and hanging ferns, it feels like an oasis at the centre of the earth. Further on, another section of the lava tube has been converted into an acoustically perfect auditorium, a 550-seat concert hall that hosts musical events in one of the most unique settings imaginable.[9, 10, 11]


**A Wacky & Fun Experience:** For a truly magical evening, Sandra and Robert should book the "Jameos Nights" experience. On select evenings (typically Tuesdays and Saturdays), the centre reopens for a special event that combines a gourmet dinner in the cave restaurant with a live folk music concert in the auditorium.[12, 13, 14] Dining and listening to music within a beautifully illuminated volcanic tube is an unforgettable, deeply romantic experience.


**Practical Details:**


- **Location:** Carretera Arrieta-Órzola, in the north of the island.
- **Opening Hours:** Daily from 10:00 to 18:30 (last entry around 17:15). Jameos Noche events run from 18:30 until midnight.[14, 15]
- **Tickets:** Standard adult admission is approximately €15. A combined ticket with the nearby Casa de los Volcanes museum is around €21.[9, 15] Tickets for Jameos Noche are separate. It is highly recommended to book all tickets online in advance to secure a place and avoid queues.[16]
- **Note:** The ground is uneven volcanic stone, so comfortable shoes are a must. The pool is purely ornamental; swimming is not permitted.[16, 17]

### Cueva de los Verdes - A Descent into History's Refuge
Part of the very same lava tube system as Jameos del Agua, the Cueva de los Verdes offers a completely different, more primal experience.[3, 18] Where Jameos is a curated artistic space, Cueva de los Verdes is a journey into the raw, geological heart of the tunnel and the island's dramatic history. For centuries, this labyrinth of underground passages served as a vital hiding place for the local population during attacks by pirates and slave traders from the North African coast.[7, 11, 19]


The visit is by guided tour only, which lasts approximately 50 minutes and covers one kilometre of the tunnel.[18, 20] A guide leads the group through a series of interconnected caverns and galleries, some stacked three levels high.[21] The lighting, expertly designed by Manrique’s collaborator Jesús Soto, illuminates the incredible textures and colours of the cave walls.[18] The rich reddish and ochre tones are not paint, but the natural result of iron oxidation and salt deposits reflecting the light, creating a visual spectacle that feels both ancient and alive.[21] The tour passes through named chambers like the "Throat of Death" and the "Hall of the Aesthetes," as well as a natural auditorium where occasional concerts are held, renowned for its spectacular acoustics.[3, 19, 20]


**A Wacky & Fun Experience:** The cave holds a famous secret—a stunning optical illusion that plays with perception and is a delight to witness. To preserve the surprise for Sandra and Robert, it will not be revealed here, but it is a clever and memorable finale to the tour that makes a visit absolutely essential.


**Practical Details:**


- **Location:** Near Jameos del Agua in the north.
- **Opening Hours:** Daily from 10:00 to 16:00 (last tour).[22]
- **Tickets:** Adult admission is approximately €15-€16.[21, 22] Crucially, tickets must be purchased online in advance, as they are not sold at the entrance, and access is limited.[23, 24] There is no skip-the-line option, so arriving early is advised to join one of the first tours and avoid queues.[19]
- **Note:** This tour is not suitable for individuals with reduced mobility or severe claustrophobia, as it involves narrow passages and steep, uneven steps.[24, 25]

### Timanfaya & El Diablo - Dining on a Volcano
No trip to Lanzarote is complete without witnessing the awe-inspiring landscape of Timanfaya National Park. This is the epicentre of the 18th-century eruptions, a 51-square-kilometre protected area of dormant volcanoes and vast, rippling lava fields that is so otherworldly it has been used by NASA to train astronauts.[1, 2, 5]


Because the landscape is so fragile and protected, visitors cannot wander freely.[4] Access to the heart of the park, the *Montañas del Fuego*, is via a mandatory 40-minute bus tour (the *Ruta de los Volcanes*), which is included in the entrance fee.[3, 26] The bus navigates a narrow road that winds dramatically through craters and over lava flows, offering spectacular views. An audio commentary, often set to dramatic classical music, explains the geological history of the eruptions.[11, 26] For the best views, it is recommended to get a seat on the right-hand side of the bus.[26]


At the tour's hub, Islote de Hilario, park rangers perform fascinating geothermal demonstrations that reveal the intense heat still simmering just beneath the surface. Dry brush thrown into a pit bursts into flames instantly, and water poured into a borehole erupts seconds later as a powerful jet of steam, a thrilling reminder of the active volcano below.[4, 5, 27]


**A Wacky & Fun Experience:** Here, atop the Islote de Hilario, sits Manrique's contribution: the El Diablo Restaurant. This circular, panoramic building is an architectural marvel, but its true claim to fame is its kitchen.[6, 10] Food is cooked on a giant cast-iron grill placed over a six-metre-deep volcanic pit, using the natural geothermal heat that rises from the earth at temperatures of around 300°C.[28] Enjoying a meal of chicken or steak cooked by a volcano, while gazing out at the fiery red craters, is a uniquely Lanzarote experience that perfectly embodies Manrique’s philosophy of harnessing nature's power for human enjoyment.[1, 29]


**Practical Details:**


- **Location:** Southwest of the island, between Yaiza and Tinajo.
- **Opening Hours:** Daily from approximately 9:30 to 17:00 (last bus tour around 16:00).[30, 31]
- **Tickets:** Adult admission is around €8-€22 depending on the source, and includes the bus tour.[4, 32] Long queues for cars are common, so arriving early (before 10:00) or during lunchtime is strongly advised.[4, 26]
- **Camel Rides:** The popular 25-minute camel rides take place at the Echadero de los Camellos, which is located on the main road just *before* the park's main entrance. This is a separate attraction and fee.[26, 30, 33]

### Mirador del Río & Jardín de Cactus
Two of Manrique's other masterpieces offer stunning perspectives on the island's diverse landscapes.


**Mirador del Río:** Perched 475 metres high atop the dramatic Risco de Famara cliffs in the far north, this is one of the most spectacular viewpoints in the Canary Islands.[5] Manrique ingeniously integrated the building into the cliff face, making it almost invisible from the outside.[5] Inside, his signature style of curved white walls and large, panoramic windows—"the eyes of the building"—frames an unbelievable view of the Chinijo Archipelago, including the serene, golden-sanded island of La Graciosa directly below.[3, 7, 34] There is a café inside, making it the perfect spot for a coffee with a world-class view.[11]


**Jardín de Cactus (Cactus Garden):** This was Manrique’s final major work on the island, a testament to his ability to transform a derelict space into a place of beauty.[6] He took an old *rofero*, a quarry used for excavating volcanic gravel, and reshaped it into a stunning, amphitheatre-like botanical garden dedicated entirely to cacti.[5, 35] The garden is home to over 4,500 specimens from more than 450 different species, sourced from across the globe.[1, 5] Visitors can wander through the terraced levels, admiring the incredible diversity of shapes and sizes. A beautifully restored traditional windmill stands sentinel over the garden, offering a fantastic vantage point for photos.[5]


**Practical Details:**


- **Locations:** Mirador del Río is in the far north; Jardín de Cactus is near Guatiza on the northeast coast.
- **Opening Hours:** Both are typically open daily from 10:00 to 18:00.[4]
- **Tickets:** Mirador del Río admission is approximately €6.50; Jardín de Cactus is around €8-€8.50.[36, 37] Multi-attraction tickets (*bonos*) can be purchased at the first CACT centre visited, offering significant savings if planning to see three or more sites.[4, 35]

### Submerging into Art - The Museo Atlántico
For a truly unforgettable and surreal adventure, Sandra and Robert can dive into Europe's first and only underwater museum.[7] Located in a sheltered bay off the coast of Playa Blanca, the Museo Atlántico is an extraordinary art installation 12 to 15 metres below the surface of the Atlantic.[7, 38] Conceived by British artist Jason deCaires Taylor, the museum consists of over 300 life-sized sculptures resting on the sandy seabed.[7, 39]


This is not a static gallery. The sculptures, made from pH-neutral concrete, were designed to evolve over time, becoming an artificial reef that attracts and supports local marine life.[39, 40] Since its installation, marine biomass in the area has increased by over 200%, and the site is now frequented by angel sharks, barracudas, octopus, and butterfly rays.[39, 40] The experience of swimming amongst these silent, moss-covered figures is eerie, beautiful, and deeply thought-provoking. The artworks address contemporary themes such as the refugee crisis ("The Raft of Lampedusa") and our relationship with technology ("The Selfie Takers"), creating a powerful dialogue between art, nature, and humanity.[38, 41, 42]


**How to Experience It:** The best part is that no prior diving experience is necessary. Numerous local dive centres offer a "try dive" or "baptism" package specifically for the museum. This typically includes a morning session in a pool to learn the basics of scuba equipment and safety, followed by a boat trip and a fully guided dive at the museum in the afternoon.[38, 39, 43] It is a safe and accessible way for complete beginners to experience this unique wonder. For certified divers, it is a straightforward guided boat dive.


**Practical Details:**


- **Location:** Off the coast of Marina Rubicón, Playa Blanca.
- **Operators:** Reputable dive centres include Manta Diving, Safari Diving, and Rubicon Diving.[38, 39, 40]
- **Cost:** A guided dive for certified divers costs around €99.[40] A "try dive" package for beginners costs approximately €150.[43]
- **Booking:** It is essential to book in advance directly with a dive centre.

### Vines of Ash - Wine Tasting in La Geria
One of Lanzarote’s most bizarre and beautiful landscapes is the wine region of La Geria.[7] At first glance, it looks like a black desert dotted with thousands of crescent-shaped stone walls. This is not a natural formation but a remarkable feat of agricultural ingenuity developed over centuries to cultivate grapes in one of the world's most unlikely terroirs.[11, 18]


After the 18th-century eruptions covered the land in a thick layer of black volcanic gravel (*picón*), farmers discovered that this gravel was excellent at absorbing and retaining the night-time humidity. To protect the vines from the constant, drying trade winds, they dug shallow hollows (*hoyos*) into the gravel for each individual vine and built a small, semi-circular stone wall (*zoco*) around it.[8, 11] The result is a stunning, almost patterned landscape and, more importantly, some exceptional and award-winning wines, most notably from the crisp, aromatic Malvasía Volcánica grape.[12] A tour of this region is a fascinating lesson in human resilience and a delight for the palate.


**Where to Go:**


- **Bodegas El Grifo:** Established in 1775, this is one of the oldest wineries in the Canary Islands. It features a fascinating museum and offers an excellent audio-guided tour for €15, which includes a tasting.[10, 12]
- **Bodega La Geria:** One of the most visited wineries, offering guided tours of the vineyard and winery followed by a tasting of three wines for €15. Tours in English are at 14:00, and advance booking online is recommended.[12, 44]
- **Bodega Stratvs:** A more modern winery offering guided tours that showcase the winemaking process, culminating in a tasting of their wines paired with locally renowned Finca de Uga cheeses for €25.[12, 45]
- **Wine Tours Lanzarote:** For a more comprehensive experience, a dedicated tour operator like this can guide visitors to multiple boutique wineries for a curated tasting journey.[46]

### Hollywood in a Volcano - The Lagomar Museum
Tucked away in the quiet village of Nazaret is one of Lanzarote’s most fantastical properties: Lagomar.[7] Often described as a villain's lair straight out of a James Bond film, this labyrinthine house is built directly into the cliffs of an old volcanic quarry, with natural caves and tunnels serving as rooms and passageways.[5, 11] The design, originally conceived by César Manrique and executed by Jesús Soto, is a masterpiece of organic architecture, flowing seamlessly into the rocky environment.[5, 35]


What makes Lagomar truly legendary, however, is its story. In the 1970s, the actor Omar Sharif was filming on the island and fell so in love with the property that he purchased it on the spot. As the legend goes, the very same day, the developer who sold it to him, knowing Sharif was an avid card player, challenged him to a high-stakes game of bridge and won the house back. Sharif, it is said, never returned to the island.[6, 7] Today, visitors can explore this incredible property, with its hidden terraces, oasis-like pool, and secret corners, and soak in the atmosphere of nature, architecture, and a touch of Hollywood tragedy.[47]

### Sunset and Seafood in El Golfo
For a truly magical evening, a trip to the tiny fishing village of El Golfo on the southwest coast is essential. The experience should be timed to coincide with sunset.[10] The first stop is the viewpoint for the **Charco de los Clicos**, also known as the Green Lagoon.[7] A short, slightly uphill walk from the free car park leads to a stunning vista of the surreal, emerald-green lagoon, a partially submerged volcanic crater whose water gets its vivid colour from a specific type of algae.[10, 58] The contrast between the green water, the black volcanic sand beach, and the deep blue of the Atlantic is an unforgettable sight.[10, 59] Swimming in the protected lagoon is prohibited, but the view is spectacular.[7, 60]


After admiring the lagoon, the perfect next step is to head into the village itself. ### "Amazing Grace" Penned by Former Slave Ship Captain
**Yes, the iconic hymn "Amazing Grace" was written by John Newton, an Englishman who captained slave ships in the 18th century.** The song's powerful message of redemption and divine mercy is deeply intertwined with Newton's own tumultuous life and eventual conversion from a participant in the brutal slave trade to a fervent abolitionist.


Born in London in 1725, John Newton's early life was marked by a rebellious nature and a career at sea. His involvement in the transatlantic slave trade saw him rise to the rank of captain, transporting enslaved Africans to the Americas under horrific conditions.


A near-death experience during a violent storm at sea in 1748 proved to be a turning point in Newton's life, sparking a gradual spiritual awakening. However, his complete renunciation of the slave trade was not immediate. He continued to captain slave voyages for several years after his initial conversion experience.


It was only later in his life, after becoming an Anglican clergyman, that Newton fully confronted the evils of his past. He became a prominent voice in the abolitionist movement, writing pamphlets that detailed the cruelty of the slave trade and collaborating with figures like William Wilberforce to advocate for its end.


Newton penned the lyrics for "Amazing Grace" around 1772, and it was first published in 1779 as part of the "Olney Hymns." The hymn's famous lines, "Amazing grace! How sweet the sound, That saved a wretch like me! I once was lost, but now am found; Was blind, but now I see," are widely interpreted as a reflection of his profound regret for his role in the slave trade and his gratitude for what he saw as God's transformative grace in his life. The hymn stands as a testament to his personal journey from a perpetrator of human suffering to a powerful advocate for its abolition.
