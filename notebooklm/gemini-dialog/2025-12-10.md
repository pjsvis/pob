I. Executive Summary of Findings

This report delivers a definitive analysis of the legal and regulatory obligations of your bank concerning your Bounce Back Loan (BBL) and provides an expert opinion on the financial incentives governing the bank's conduct.
On Lender Communication Obligations: Your bank is unequivocally required to provide you with an annual statement for your Bounce Back Loan (BBL). This is a specific, contractual obligation stipulated in the BBLS Term Loan Lender Manual 1, which all accredited lenders were required to follow. This statement must detail all transactions on the loan and the outstanding balance.1 Furthermore, if you are in or approaching financial difficulty, the bank's communication obligations intensify, requiring them to provide clear and timely information on missed payments, all available remedies, and sources of free independent advice.1 A failure by the bank to provide such statements constitutes a clear breach of these scheme-specific obligations.
On the "Perverse Incentive" for Default: Your impression that the 100% government guarantee 3 creates a perverse incentive for lenders is logical and correct. This risk has been formally recognized by government oversight bodies, including the National Audit Office (NAO) 5 and the Public Accounts Committee (PAC), which noted that lenders "have no commercial incentive to assist with recovery".7
Expert Opinion and Synthesis: Your suspicion that the bank desires a quick default to "claim back their cash" is an oversimplification of the bank's actual, more complex set of incentives. The 100% guarantee is not a blank cheque. The British Business Bank (BBB), acting on behalf of the government, retains the right to audit lenders.5 Crucially, the BBB can—and does—"claw back" guarantee payments if the bank is found to have breached the terms of the scheme, for example, by not following the mandated "reasonable steps" or the "BBLS Recovery Principles".5
The "True" Incentive: The bank is not incentivized to pursue a quick default, as this would expose it to a 100% loss from a guarantee clawback. It is, however, structurally disincentivized from engaging in costly, bespoke, good-faith recovery efforts. The bank's true financial incentive is one of passive compliance and bureaucratic indifference. It is motivated to perform the bare minimum "tick-box" recovery process necessary to satisfy a future BBB audit, not to achieve a sustainable, positive outcome for you.
Your Experience: Your feeling of being disempowered and poorly treated is likely a direct symptom of this systemic failure. The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) has publicly censured major banks for "widespread" poor treatment of struggling SME borrowers, including BBLS holders. These documented failures include setting up "unaffordable" repayment plans, failing to support vulnerable customers, and maintaining "poor record keeping".8 Your experience (e.g., not receiving basic statements) is likely a direct result of these documented operational failings, not a malicious, secret strategy to make you fail.

III. The Unique Legal and Regulatory Framework of BBLS

To properly assess your bank's conduct, it is essential to understand the unique and often-confusing legal framework that governs Bounce Back Loans. A BBL is not a normal business loan, and the rules that apply are highly specific.
The Critical Exclusion: The Consumer Credit Act 1974
For most business lending in the UK under £25,000, the Consumer Credit Act 1974 (CCA) would apply. This Act provides a robust, statutory framework of borrower protections, including highly prescriptive rules for the content, format, and timing of annual statements and arrears notices.
However, the BBLS was explicitly excluded from these protections. Lenders (e.g., Bank of Scotland 17, Lloyds 18, Barclays 16) were required by the government to make it clear to all borrowers that they "will not have the benefit of the protection and remedies that would otherwise be available to them under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 or the Consumer Credit Act 1974".16
This exclusion is the single biggest source of confusion for borrowers and, arguably, for the banks' own staff. It created a "regulatory void." The bank was not bound by the strict, statutory CCA rules for statements, which may have led its automated systems and staff—who are acclimatized to normal lending—to incorrectly assume that no rules applied. This assumption was, and is, false.
The Protections that Did Apply (Filling the Void)
This "regulatory void" was immediately filled by two other powerful frameworks, which your bank is subject to:
The BBLS Scheme Rules (Contractual): As established in Section II, the Lender Manual 1 created new, specific, contractual obligations (such as the annual statement rule) that the bank agreed to as a non-negotiable condition of joining the scheme. These rules effectively replace the ones from the CCA.
The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) (Regulatory): The FCA, the UK's financial regulator, made clear that its own principles and rules would apply to BBLS, even without the CCA.
The Role of the FCA: CONC 7 and the Consumer Duty
The FCA's oversight is the primary source of your regulatory protection.
CONC 7: The FCA's "Consumer Credit Sourcebook (CONC) Chapter 7" sets out binding rules for the fair treatment of customers in default or arrears.11 The FCA was explicit that CONC 7 applies to BBLS debt collection where the borrower is a sole trader or a small partnership.13 This includes rules on treating customers with "forbearance and due consideration" and not pressuring them into "unreasonably short" repayment periods.9
Lending Standards Board (LSB): For borrowers not covered by CONC 7 (e.g., most limited companies), the regulatory landscape was, until recently, governed by the LSB's "Standards of Lending Practice for business customers".19 The FCA formally recognized these standards 21 and, critically, they were embedded into the "BBLS Recoveries Principles" 23 that all banks agreed to follow. These standards also demand fair, clear, and supportive communication.24
The Consumer Duty (The "Catch-All"): Most importantly, the FCA's new and powerful Consumer Duty has superseded many of these previous standards.26 This Duty, which came into full force for all existing products, requires firms to "act to deliver good outcomes" for retail customers. The FCA has explicitly confirmed that firms involved in BBLS collections "will therefore need to fully comply with the Consumer Duty".26
In summary, you are in a stronger position than you may realize. While the bank may try to internally justify poor service by pointing to the exclusion of the CCA 17, this is irrelevant. They are still bound by three superseding layers of rules: 1) The specific BBLS contractual rules (requiring an annual statement) 1, 2) The FCA's CONC 7 rules (if you are a sole trader/small partnership) 13, and 3) The FCA's all-encompassing Consumer Duty (which requires them to act for good outcomes).26 The failure to provide a statement is a clear breach of (1) and very likely a breach of (3).

IV. Expert Opinion: Deconstructing the Bank's Financial Incentives

This section directly addresses your "impression" that the bank is incentivized to see your company fail. This is a logical conclusion to draw, and this analysis will dissect the complex, competing financial incentives that are actually driving your bank's behavior.
The "Perverse Incentive": The 100% Government Guarantee
Your logic is sound and is shared by the UK's highest public auditors. The BBLS was unique in offering lenders a 100% government-backed guarantee against both the outstanding capital and interest.3
This structure immediately created what economists call a "moral hazard." The National Audit Office (NAO) and the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) identified this as the scheme's primary taxpayer risk from its inception. The PAC, in a 2024 report, stated that the government "behaved too passively" as lenders "have no commercial incentive to assist with recovery of taxpayers' money".7 The NAO was similarly blunt, noting in 2021 that "with a 100% guarantee, lenders have limited incentives to seek a full recovery".6
In a normal commercial loan, the bank would fight hard to recover its own money. In the BBLS, it is merely acting as a collection agent for the taxpayer's money. This lack of "skin in the game" is the root of the problem and the source of your correct impression.
The Critical Counter-Incentive: The Risk of Guarantee "Clawback"
This is the most crucial, and least understood, part of the BBLS mechanism. Your theory that the bank wants a quick default is incomplete because it misses this vital counter-incentive. The 100% guarantee is not an automatic, no-questions-asked payment.
The British Business Bank (BBB), which administers the scheme for the government, has the right to audit the lender's processes and files for BBLS guarantee claims.5
The NAO report on the BBLS provides the critical evidence. It states that the BBB "can claw back any guarantee payments where errors are found, for example where the lender has not followed the correct recovery protocols before claiming on the guarantee".5
This single fact fundamentally refutes the "quick default" theory. A bank that rushes a borrower into default without following the explicit, multi-step BBLS rules (e.g., offering PAYG, providing information, following its own standard processes) does not get a "quick" 100% payout. It gets a 100% "clawback" risk. A BBLS claim rejected by the BBB on audit turns the "guaranteed" asset into a total loss for the bank.
The bank's real incentive is therefore not to be fast, but to be auditable. It must build a "compliance file" for your loan that proves to a future BBB auditor that it followed the rules, however inefficiently.
What are the "Correct Recovery Protocols" the Bank Must Follow?
To make a valid guarantee claim that will withstand an audit, the bank must prove it followed several layers of rules.
"Business-as-Usual" (BAU) Standards: The "BBLS Recoveries Principles," which all banks signed, explicitly state that lenders must adopt their "business-as-usual (BAU) standards and processes".23 This means they must apply the same level of effort, procedure, and communication to your BBLS loan as they would to one of their own unsecured commercial loans.27 A "quick default" is almost never a BAU process and would be an immediate red flag for an auditor.
Mandatory Forbearance (PAYG): As established in Section II, the bank must have proactively offered you the PAYG options.12 A failure to evidence this offer (e.g., copies of emails or letters) would be a simple and catastrophic failure at a BBB audit, likely voiding the guarantee.
No Refusal to Negotiate: The BBLS Recoveries Principles explicitly state that a firm "must not operate a policy of refusing to negotiate with a borrower who is developing a repayment plan".23 They must engage.
"Reasonable Steps" to Recover: The guarantee terms require lenders to take "reasonable steps" to recover the debt.5 While an NAO-identified loophole allows a bank to claim before the recovery process is complete if "no further payment is likely" 5, the bank must be able to evidence this belief to an auditor.
Synthesis & Final Opinion on Incentives
Your impression of a perverse incentive is correct.6
However, the outcome of that incentive is not a malicious, quick default. The counter-incentive of the BBB "clawback" 5 is too great a financial risk.
The true resulting incentive is one of bureaucratic indifference. The bank's internal goal is to move your file from "performing" to "guarantee claim paid" with the least possible operational cost and effort, while simultaneously creating a paper trail that will satisfy a future BBB audit.
This model explains everything you are likely experiencing:
The bank will follow a process, but it will be rigid, automated, and inflexible.
It will not be commercially motivated to find a bespoke, creative, or time-consuming solution for you (as this is expensive and the upside is nil).
It will be motivated to get you into a standard plan (even an "unaffordable" one, as the FCA found), because this "ticks a box" on the compliance file.
It is disincentivized from spending money on good customer service (like properly staffed call centres or sending non-automated statements), as this does not directly impact the guarantee claim process.
This is what you are feeling: not a conspiracy to make you fail, but a system that has no financial interest in helping you succeed.

V. Evidence of Actual Bank Conduct vs. Obligations

My opinion in Section IV is that the BBLS incentive structure encourages "bureaucratic indifference" and a poor, "tick-box" approach to customer service. This is not just a theory; it is precisely what the UK's financial regulators have found in practice. Your experience is very likely a data point in a documented, systemic failure.
The FCA's Review of SME Collections (The "Smoking Gun")
In 2023 and 2024, the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) conducted a multi-firm review of how 11 of the major banks were treating their SME borrowers in financial difficulty, a group that includes BBLS holders.8
The findings 8 are a perfect, real-world reflection of the "bureaucratic indifference" model predicted by the incentive structure:
Setting Customers Up to Fail: The FCA found lenders were "arranging payment plans which are clearly unaffordable based on the information provided by the customer".8 This is the absolute hallmark of a "tick-box" process. The bank gets to tick the box "payment plan offered" (satisfying an auditor), knowing the client will default, at which point it can move to the next step of the collections process.
Incompetent, Untrained Staff: The review found "Staff not having the right training to provide effective support... and make fair decisions".8 This aligns perfectly with the "indifference" model—it is far cheaper to hire low-skilled staff to follow a rigid script than to invest in experienced, empathetic recovery specialists.
Failure on Vulnerable Customers: Lenders were found to have a "lack of clear policies to help staff identify and support vulnerable customers".8
Systemic Poor Record-Keeping: This finding directly relates to your query about statements. The FCA found "in many cases record keeping was poor, such that some could not provide complete customer files and it was not possible to determine if the customer had received a fair outcome based on the records".9
Parliamentary Scrutiny (The Macro View)
The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) has repeatedly and publicly criticized this exact passivity from the lenders.
In April 2023, the PAC "slammed the government for continuing to make slow progress on its counter-fraud activities" and, critically, for its "'lack of curiosity' regarding lenders' performance".30
In 2024, the PAC concluded that the government Department "behaved too passively by placing primary responsibility on lenders who... have no commercial incentive to assist with recovery of taxpayers' money".7
Your experience is not an isolated incident. You are not being paranoid. The failure to receive a basic annual statement 1 is a direct, documented symptom of the "poor record keeping" 9 and "bureaucratic indifference" 7 that the FCA and Parliament have both identified as systemic failures in the BBLS collections ecosystem. The bank's behavior, which 'feels' like they want you to fail, is the documented, real-world outcome of a guarantee scheme 5 that was designed to remove the commercial incentives 7 that would normally ensure good service, and which is policed only by the distant threat of an audit "clawback".5

VI. Concluding Analysis and Recommended Actions

Your Factual Rights: You have a clear, non-negotiable right to an annual statement under the BBLS scheme rules your bank contractually agreed to follow.1 Their failure to provide one is a breach of their agreement with the British Business Bank.
Your Bank's Incentives: Your suspicion of a perverse incentive is correct.6 However, this incentive does not lead to a simple or quick default. It leads to a lazy, passive, and operationally incompetent collections process, as the bank's only true financial driver is to create a 'compliance file' to protect its guarantee from a "clawback".5
Your Experience vs. Reality: Your feeling of being treated poorly and "set up to fail" is validated by the FCA's own investigations. These found widespread failures, "unaffordable" payment plans, and poor record-keeping across the entire sector.8 You are experiencing the documented, systemic failure of the BBLS collections process.
Recommended Actions:
Formal Written Complaint: You should raise a formal written complaint to your bank. This is a critical first step. In this complaint, you should:
State that you are aware of their specific obligations under the BBLS Term Loan Lender Manual 1 to provide an "annual statement" and have not received one.
State that their failure to do so is a breach of the BBLS scheme rules and, in your view, a breach of the FCA's Consumer Duty 26 to "act to deliver good outcomes."
Request that all outstanding annual statements be provided immediately to enable you to understand your position.
Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS): The bank's original BBLS agreement 16 and the scheme rules 1 confirm your right to escalate this complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS) if you are not satisfied with their final response. The FOS is empowered to handle BBLS complaints.
Engage Proactively: Do not assume the bank's silence or incompetence is a fixed strategy. It is more likely a systemic failing. You should proactively contact them in writing (e.g., email) to build your own evidence file.
Specifically reference your Pay As You Grow (PAYG) options 12, such as extending the loan term to 10 years or taking an interest-only period. These are your entitlements.
By doing this, you establish yourself as a "borrower who is developing a repayment plan," which the bank is explicitly forbidden from "refusing to negotiate" with under the BBLS Recoveries Principles.23 This action forces the bank to engage and moves them out of their passive, "tick-box" process.
