Since 30% of force is transmitted laterally, a "weak" muscle may fundamentally be a muscle with poor fascial connectivity or shear-force transmission efficiency. Hypertrophy training (increasing CE size) without fascial training (plyometrics/loading to increase stiffness and tensegrity) yields diminishing returns. A bodybuilder may have high CE potential but low "Effective Mass" in a strike due to poor fascial integration.[35]


### 6.2 The Ripple Effect of Densification
Sedentary behavior causes HA densification (gel state). This increases internal friction, requiring more muscle force to create the same movement (viscous drag). Furthermore, it desensitizes the embedded mechanoreceptors.[6]


- **The Cycle:** Immobility $\rightarrow$ HA Densification $\rightarrow$ Increased Friction + Proprioceptive Noise $\rightarrow$ Poor Motor Control $\rightarrow$ Injury.

### 6.3 Rehabilitation and Aging
Aging is characterized by sarcopenia (loss of muscle) and fibrosis (stiffening of fascia). Interestingly, the elderly utilize the increased passive stiffness of their fascia to compensate for muscle loss, relying more on passive torque for walking.[41] Rehabilitation must distinguish between *pathological* stiffness (fibrosis/adhesion) and *functional* stiffness (elastic tension).


## 7. Conclusion
The analysis conclusively demonstrates that the segregation of muscle and fascia is a biomechanical fallacy. The evidence supports a **Myofascial Continuum** model where:


1. **Fascia is a Primary Contributor:** It is not a passive wrapper but a dynamic transmission system providing **30% of force transmission**, **32% of dynamic energy return**, and the vast majority of **sensory feedback**.
2. **Symbiotic Limits:**

- **No Fascia:** Results in catastrophic structural and functional failure. The "bag of meat" cannot transmit force, store energy, or sense its position. Functional reduction is effectively **100%**.
- **No Muscle:** Results in a loss of agency but retention of structural integrity and passive elastic behaviors. The system remains a stable, energy-conserving tensegrity structure.

**Final Recommendation:** For the optimization of human performance—whether in rehabilitation, heavy lifting, or combat sports—interventions must target the properties of the *entire* unit. This necessitates a training regimen that integrates high-load strength work (for the Contractile Element), rhythmic plyometrics (for the Series Elastic Element), and multi-planar mobility work (to maintain the thixotropic fluidity of the Parallel Elastic Element). The "snap" of the punch, the bounce of the sprint, and the stability of the spine are all emergent properties of the healthy, integrated myofascial web.
