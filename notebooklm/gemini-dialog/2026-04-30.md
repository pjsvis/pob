# Comprehensive Longitudinal Analysis of Crew Health and System Performance Following the Artemis II Lunar Flyby Mission
The conclusion of the Artemis II mission on April 10, 2026, represents a transformative milestone in the history of human spaceflight, marking the first time in more than 52 years that a crewed vessel has navigated the translunar environment.[1, 2] The mission, which launched on April 1, 2026, from Kennedy Space Center's Launch Complex 39B, carried Commander Reid Wiseman, Pilot Victor Glover, and Mission Specialists Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen on a high-velocity free-return trajectory that reached a maximum distance of 252,756 miles (406,771 km) from Earth.[1, 3] This distance surpassed the previous record established by the Apollo 13 crew in 1970.[3, 4] Upon the successful splashdown of the Orion spacecraft, christened *Integrity*, in the Pacific Ocean off the coast of San Diego, the focus of the Artemis program shifted from the mechanics of orbital flight to the intensive physiological and psychological re-adaptation of the four crew members.[5, 6, 7]


The current status of the Artemis II astronauts is characterized by a multi-phase recovery process that includes immediate post-splashdown stabilization, intermediate clinical evaluations at the Johnson Space Center, and long-term longitudinal studies aimed at characterizing the effects of deep-space radiation and microgravity on the human body.[6, 8, 9] Initial reports from the recovery vessel, the USS John P. ## Vestibular Re-adaptation and Sensorimotor Performance
The most visible manifestation of the crew's re-adaptation to Earth's gravity has been the challenge of maintaining balance and gait stability. The human vestibular system, primarily the otolith organs in the inner ear, undergoes a neural re-weighting in microgravity.[9, 18] Without the constant acceleration of gravity to define "down," the brain begins to ignore vestibular signals and relies more heavily on visual and proprioceptive inputs.[8, 11] Upon return to the $1g$ environment, these otolith organs are once again stimulated, but the central nervous system has not yet recalibrated the gain of its balance-control loops, leading to significant postural instability.[9, 11]


NASA astronaut Christina Koch provided a high-fidelity account of this recovery process, noting that even a week after returning to Earth, she struggled with a standard "blindfold walk" test.[11] In this assessment, her inability to utilize visual cues forced her brain to rely on the still-unstable vestibular signals, resulting in a pronounced wobble and the need for attendants to provide physical support.[11] This phenomenon, colloquially termed the "space jitters" or "wobble," is a primary focus of the Standard Measures experiment, which seeks to characterize how quickly astronauts can regain the motor skills necessary to perform technical tasks after landing.[9] For future lunar surface missions, such as Artemis III, the speed of this recovery is paramount, as astronauts will be required to egress the landing vehicle and conduct surface activities in a $1/6g$ environment shortly after arrival.[9]

## Cardiovascular Deconditioning and Fluid Distribution
The transition to a microgravity environment immediately triggers a cephalad fluid shift, as the hydrostatic pressure gradient that normally pulls blood and interstitial fluids toward the lower extremities is eliminated.[8, 9] This leads to an increase in central venous pressure and the characteristic "puffy face" syndrome reported by Mission Specialist Jeremy Hansen.[8] The body compensates for this perceived fluid overload by inhibiting the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and increasing the secretion of atrial natriuretic peptide, resulting in a reduction of total plasma volume.[6, 8]


While the 10-day duration of the Artemis II mission was relatively short compared to a typical six-month stay on the International Space Station, the physiological impact was nonetheless significant.[8, 9] Upon re-entry and splashdown, the sudden re-imposition of Earth's gravity caused fluids to pool once again in the lower extremities, leading to a risk of orthostatic intolerance (dizziness or fainting when standing).[6, 8] To mitigate this risk, Jeremy Hansen and the other crew members utilized specialized orthostatic intolerance garments—compression suits that provide graded pressure to the lower body to assist in maintaining venous return and systemic blood pressure.[6]


The evaluation of these garments was a key objective of the mission's final days.[6] Preliminary data suggests that the use of compression therapy, combined with targeted fluid loading prior to re-entry, significantly reduced the incidence of pre-syncope among the crew.[6, 9] However, cardiovascular deconditioning, including a reduction in cardiac output and changes in stroke volume, remains a subject of ongoing study, as these factors directly influence the physical stamina of astronauts during the early stages of post-flight recovery.[9, 18]


## The AVATAR Investigation: Breakthroughs in Personalized Space Medicine
A primary focus of the Artemis II science mission was the AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response) experiment, which utilized human organ-on-a-chip technology to study the synergistic effects of deep-space radiation and microgravity at a cellular level.[12, 13, 14] This study represents a paradigm shift in space medicine, moving beyond generalized observations toward personalized, subject-specific research.[14, 19]


### Microphysiological Systems and the Astronaut "Avatar"
The AVATAR devices, developed in collaboration between the Wyss Institute at Harvard, Emulate, Inc., and NASA, are microfluidic culture systems roughly the size of a USB drive.[13, 14] Each chip contains hollow channels lined with living human cells that replicate the three-dimensional structure and biochemical environment of specific human tissues.[14, 19] For the Artemis II mission, cells were harvested from the four astronauts prior to launch to create bone marrow-on-a-chip models that were unique to each crew member.[12, 14, 19]


Bone marrow was selected as the target tissue because of its high sensitivity to ionizing radiation and its critical role in the hematopoietic and immune systems.[14, 19, 20] Previous studies on the ISS have shown that spaceflight can alter the production of red and white blood cells, but the isolation of variables has always been challenging in a low Earth orbit environment where Earth's magnetosphere still provides substantial radiation shielding.[8, 13] By sending these chips on a lunar flyby, researchers were able to expose the biological "avatars" to the true cosmic radiation environment of deep space.[13, 14, 21]


### Hardware and Experimental Autonomy
The chips were housed in a self-contained, automated life support system developed by Space Tango, which was mounted on the port Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) wall inside the Orion crew module.[13, 14] This hardware provided continuous environmental control at $37^\circ \text{C}$ and utilized a pressure-driven microfluidics system to deliver nutrients and remove waste at ultra-slow, precisely controlled flow rates.[13] The system was powered by internal lithium-ion batteries and operated autonomously for the duration of the 10-day mission, requiring only a daily status check from the crew.[13]

## Radiation Exposure and Genetic Integrity
Deep-space radiation represents one of the most significant barriers to long-duration human exploration.[8, 20, 22] Unlike the ISS, which is shielded by the Van Allen belts, the Artemis II crew was exposed to high-energy galactic cosmic rays (GCRs) and solar energetic particles (SEPs).[21, 22] These particles have high linear energy transfer (LET), meaning they deposit a significant amount of energy into the cells they penetrate, which can cause complex double-strand DNA breaks that are difficult for the body to repair correctly.[8, 20]


### Real-Time Monitoring and Space Weather

While the total dose received during the 10-day mission was within safe operational limits, the data gathered is essential for modeling the risks of the much longer missions to Mars.[8, 20, 22] The crew also wore passive dosimeters in their pockets to provide a cumulative record of their personal exposure.[20] These measurements are being correlated with post-flight blood samples to assess the presence of chromosomal aberrations, which serve as a biological "clock" of radiation-induced damage.[6, 14, 20]


### Epigenetics and DNA Methylation
The study of DNA methylation—the addition of methyl groups to DNA that can turn genes on or off without changing the underlying genetic sequence—is a key component of the Artemis II medical evaluation.[23, 24, 25] This epigenetic process is sensitive to environmental stressors such as radiation, confinement, and altered sleep-wake cycles.[21, 24, 26]


Preliminary findings from previous studies, such as the NASA Twins Study involving Scott and Mark Kelly, showed that spaceflight can cause widespread changes in DNA methylation, although many of these changes revert to baseline shortly after returning to Earth.[24, 25, 26] For the Artemis II crew, the longitudinal collection of saliva and blood samples before, during, and after the mission will allow researchers to characterize the "epigenetic signature" of deep-space travel.[6, 20] Of particular interest is the methylation of genes like *Myod1*, which regulates muscle cell differentiation and may play a role in the muscle atrophy observed during spaceflight.[27]
