### 1. David Hume and the Swamp of Damnation
David Hume, the great philosopher and historian, was famously a sceptic, and in the highly religious society of 18th-century Edinburgh, he earned the nickname "The Great Infidel." His cheerful atheism was a source of constant gossip and occasional alarm.


The story goes that while taking a shortcut home through the marshy ground of the drained Nor' Loch (where Princes Street Gardens are today), Hume slipped and found himself stuck fast in the bog. He was sinking, unable to get out, and his cries for help were eventually answered by an old fishwife.


She recognised him immediately. "Are ye no David Hume, the atheist?" she demanded.


"I am," he pleaded, "but good Christian woman, your religion should move you to do good to all, even your enemies."


"That I will," she replied cannily, "but ye shallna get oot o' there until ye become a Christian yersel'! Recite the Lord's Prayer and the Creed this instant!"


Stuck up to his chest in mud and with no other option, the great sceptical philosopher duly recited the Lord's Prayer and the Apostle's Creed. The old woman, satisfied with her battlefield conversion, hauled him out. Hume is said to have brushed himself off and, with a twinkle in his eye, remarked that the fishwife was the most astute theologian he had ever encountered.


### 2. Adam Smith, the Absent-Minded Professor
While David Hume was the life of the party, his dearest friend, Adam Smith, was the archetypal absent-minded genius. His mind was so consumed by his theories on economics and morality that he was often completely oblivious to the world around him.


One famous story tells of a Sunday morning when Smith, deep in thought about some economic principle, got out of bed and, clad only in his nightgown, walked out of his house in Kirkcaldy. He continued walking, completely lost in his own world, for nearly 15 miles. He was only snapped out of his reverie by the sound of church bells in the next town, Dunfermline, at which point he realised his state and had to make a rather embarrassed journey home.


On another occasion, while entertaining a distinguished guest, he began to lecture on his favourite topic of free trade while making tea. He meticulously explained his points, all the while distractedly putting bread and butter into the teapot instead of tea leaves. After tasting the resulting brew and declaring it the worst tea he had ever had, a friend gently pointed out his mistake. Smith was not embarrassed, merely fascinated by the unexpected (and disgusting) result of his experiment.


### 3. The Poker Club: Serious Drinking for a Serious Cause
Much of the Enlightenment's energy came from its many clubs. One of the most famous was **The Poker Club**, founded in 1762. Its name had nothing to do with cards; its purpose was to "poke" the fire of debate regarding a controversial political issue: the establishment of a Scottish Militia.


The members included luminaries like Hume, Smith, Adam Ferguson, and William Robertson. Their meetings in Thomas Nicholson's tavern were legendary for two things: high-level intellectual debate and heroic consumption of claret. The club's secretary meticulously recorded the amount of wine consumed at each meeting. To be a "one-bottle man" was considered moderate; serious participants were expected to handle two or more.


A wonderful rule of the club was that members would be fined for using Scotticisms in their speech, in an effort to polish their English. However, David Hume proposed a clever counter-rule: any member who was so anglicised that they *couldn't* understand a common Scots word should be fined a bottle of claret for the table. This perfectly captures their pride and their playful intellectualism.


### 4. James Hutton and the View from Arthur's Seat
James Hutton, the father of modern geology, was another key figure in this circle. He was a member of the **Oyster Club**, which he, Adam Smith, and the chemist Joseph Black founded for weekly meetings.


Hutton’s revolutionary idea was that the Earth was immeasurably old, a concept now known as "deep time." This idea didn't come to him in a sterile laboratory. It was formed by walking the land he loved, particularly the dramatic hills in the heart of Edinburgh.


Standing on Salisbury Crags or Arthur's Seat, Hutton could see the evidence with his own eyes. He observed layers of sedimentary rock lying horizontally on top of older, vertical layers of rock—an "unconformity" that spoke of two distinct geological epochs separated by an immense gulf of time. It was from these observations, discussed and debated over oysters and wine with Smith and Black, that he came to his famous conclusion that the Earth showed "$no \ vestige \ of \ a \ beginning, \ no \ prospect \ of \ an \ end$." The very landscape of the city was a textbook for its greatest thinkers.


### 5. The Window Story: A Tale of Two Towns
Finally, to the story you referenced. It perfectly symbolises the shift from the cramped, medieval Old Town to the spacious, rational New Town. The great jurist Lord Kames, one of the elder statesmen of the Enlightenment, was one of the first to move from the Old Town to a grand new house on Queen Street.


He held a housewarming party for his old friends, a boisterous group of judges and philosophers. After an evening of profound discussion and, inevitably, many bottles of claret, the time came to leave. The guests, finding the broad, formal staircase of the New Town house a bit too much of a challenge in their inebriated state, simply opted for the most direct route. They opened the large ground-floor drawing-room window and climbed out into the street.


This act, beyond being comical, was deeply symbolic. They were literally stepping out of the new, rational order into the night, carrying with them the boisterous, chaotic, and brilliant spirit of the Old Town taverns where their great ideas were born.
