## The Institutional Genesis: Richard Orton and the York Studio
The University of York Electronic Music Studio was established in 1968, appearing shortly after the university itself was founded as part of the post-war expansion of British higher education.[1, 9] Under the direction of Richard Orton, who had been a member of the influential live electronic ensemble Gentle Fire, the studio was designed as a democratic space for sonic exploration.[1, 10] Initially, the facility existed on a makeshift basis, housed in temporary quarters before moving to its permanent location on the first floor of the Lyons Concert Hall building.[1] This room, measuring 20 feet by 16 feet, provided a panoramic view of the campus trees and grass, an environment that subtly influenced the "landscape" oriented compositions of students like Trevor Wishart and Andrew Bentley.[1, 11, 12]

## The Briefcase Synthesis: EMS VCS3 and Synthi A
The "VCS-3 briefcase format device" remembered by participants of the York scene refers to the EMS Synthi A, a portable version of the original VCS3 "Putney".[4, 14] Launched in 1971 by Electronic Music Studios (London) Ltd., the Synthi A was housed in a Spartanite attaché case, making it the first truly portable professional-grade synthesizer in the world.[4, 15] Its lack of a traditional keyboard—later addressed by the Synthi AKS with its touch-sensitive keyboard and sequencer—appealed to the York composers who were more interested in non-tempered soundscapes and environmental sound manipulation than in standard melodic composition.[4, 14, 16]


The technical heart of the VCS3 and Synthi A was the 16x16 matrix plugboard, which allowed for complex signal routing without the need for patch cables.[4, 14] This matrix encouraged experimental signal flows, such as using an oscillator to frequency-modulate another oscillator while simultaneously filtering an external input, like an electric guitar or a microphone.[4, 17]


### Signal Processing and Synthesis Physics
The flexibility of the VCS3 was often exploited to create "morphing" textures where one sound type would seamlessly transition into another. This is modeled mathematically by the interaction of the voltage-controlled oscillators (VCOs) and the filter cutoff frequency ($f_c$):


$$V_{out}(t) = \int [V_{in}(t) \cdot H(f_c, Q)] dt$$


where $H(f_c, Q)$ is the transfer function of the voltage-controlled filter (VCF), with $Q$ representing the resonance.[4, 14] The "joystick" on the bottom right of the unit provided a two-dimensional control surface, allowing performers to manually sweep the $X$ and $Y$ parameters of the patch—often assigned to filter frequency and oscillator pitch—in real-time, creating the "space-rock" or "burbling" textures characteristic of the era.[4, 18]


## Architectural Naming: The Phase Series and University Construction
The nomenclature of the Phase-1 and Phase-2 multimedia events likely derived from the University of York’s own physical development.[19] The university was built in distinct architectural phases, with "Phase 1" including the initial campus core and colleges like Derwent and Langwith, while "Phase 2" expanded into Vanbrugh, Goodricke, and the Central Hall complex.[19] Student organizers, living and working within these designated "Phases," adopted the terminology for their series of happenings.[19, 20]


The "distant colleges" mentioned in recollections typically refer to Langwith or Derwent, which were located further from the central Lyons Concert Hall and administrative core.[19] These venues provided a degree of autonomy and isolation from the formal music faculty, allowing for the "safe" execution of radical multimedia experiments that might have been deemed too chaotic for the main concert hall.[21]

## Multimedia Components: Slides, Liquid Light, and Surrealism
The visual dimension of the York performances was as critical as the sonic output, reflecting the influence of the 1960s psychedelic underground on 1970s academic experimentation.[5, 6] The use of slide projectors with "surrealistic slides" and liquid light projectors provided a non-linear visual narrative that mirrored the abstract nature of the electronic soundscapes.[6, 22]


Liquid light shows were achieved using overhead projectors and glass clock-faces containing a mixture of water, colored oils, and dyes.[6, 23] When heated and manipulated by the performer, the liquids would expand and ripple, creating organic, evolving textures that could be rhythmicized by tapping on the glass.[6, 23] This technique was popularized in the UK by light-show artists who had transitioned from the London club scene into more experimental environments.[6] At York, students like Richard Pickett integrated these visuals directly into their compositions, as seen in the 1975 work *The Naked Light Show*, which utilized tape with film and mime.[1]

## The Patrick Harrex Connection and the Electric Guitar
The mention of "Patrick's brother on electric guitar" at the "nearer college" gig points to the significant influence of the ensemble Gentle Fire on the York student scene.[27] Patrick Harrex was a foundational member of Gentle Fire, alongside Richard Orton, Richard Bernas, and Graham Hearn.[10, 27] Gentle Fire was dedicated to the performance of live electronic compositions and was particularly noted for its ability to mix acoustic and electronic elements.[28]


The electric guitar served as a bridge between the high-modernist electronic studio and the broader rock and folk traditions.[16, 20, 29] In the York context, the guitar was rarely played in a conventional manner; instead, it was used as a sound source to be filtered and mangled through the VCS3’s input amplifiers.[4, 14, 17] Neil Lamb is credited as the primary electric guitarist on the *York Pop Music Project* LP (*All Day*), a recording that exemplifies the collaborative spirit of the department.[20]


### The Role of Electric Guitar in York’s Avant-Rock Fusion
The use of the guitar in these "happenings" often involved extreme amplification and the use of feedback as a controlled musical parameter.[8, 30] This approach was heavily influenced by the American experimental tradition, specifically the work of Gordon Mumma and Robert Ashley, whom the York students had invited to the campus for a "revelatory" workshop.[10, 16] The "Patrick’s brother" mentioned likely refers to a family member of Patrick Harrex or a frequent collaborator within the tightly knit circle of Gentle Fire members who dominated the university's experimental music sessions.[10, 27]


## Acoustic Science and the Stairwell "Happenings"
The performance in the "resonant stairwell" with "dry ice" represents a deep dive into the physics of acoustic space.[3, 11] The architecture of the University of York, characterized by its brutalist-inspired concrete structures, offered unique reverberant environments that acted as natural filters.[3] Composers like Andrew Bentley and Trevor Wishart explored these spaces to "narrativize" music, moving beyond the studio to find sounds that carried spatial and anecdotal symbolism.[11, 31]


Dry ice was used not only for its visual effect but for its ability to create a "cloud" that could envelop both the performer and the sound source, reinforcing the acousmatic tradition of separating the sound from its visible cause.[25, 32, 33] Scientifically, the sublimation of solid carbon dioxide into gas can subtly change the density of the air in a confined space like a stairwell, which some experimentalists believed would affect the "shimmer" of high-frequency electronic tones.[26, 34]


### The Physics of Resonant Stairwell Performance
The reverberation time ($RT_{60}$) of a concrete stairwell can be modeled by the Sabine equation:


$$RT_{60} = \frac{0.161 \cdot V}{A}$$


where $V$ is the volume of the stairwell and $A$ is the total absorption.[3] In the York stairwells, where $A$ was extremely low due to the hard concrete surfaces, the $RT_{60}$ could exceed several seconds, allowing for the creation of dense, overlapping "feedback clusters" when paired with a VCS3 or a microphone.[3, 8] The "weird guy with the dry ice" was likely a student or "hanger-on" specializing in environmental effects, a common role in the interdisciplinary happenings of the 1970s.[8, 25, 32]


## Documentation and Records: The York Pop Music Project
The most tangible evidence of this era is the 1973 private-press LP *All Day – York Pop Music Project* (YES 1).[7, 20] This record, produced by Richard Orton and engineered by Andrew Bentley, featured an incredible assortment of York students and faculty, including Steve Beresford on bass, Jan Steele on saxophone and flute, and the Fitzwilliam String Quartet.[20] The album was sold mainly to fellow students and family, and its "legendary status" among collectors today stems from its unique blend of folk, progressive rock, and academic electronics.[7]


Following the success of *All Day*, the studio released the triple LP box set *Electronic Music from York* (YES 2-4), which focused more explicitly on avant-garde and tape-music works.[8, 35] This set included seminal early pieces by Trevor Wishart (*Machine*), Richard Orton (*Kiss*, *Clock Farm*), and Andrew Bentley (*Moan*).[1, 8]

## The "Safe End": Why the Gigs Stopped
The user’s observation that the events "ended safely, but notably was not repeated" highlights a significant shift in the university’s culture around 1975.[21] Several factors likely contributed to the cessation of the multimedia happenings. First, the professionalization of the Electronic Music Studio under Richard Orton and the subsequent technological shifts toward more "serious" computer-based composition—culminating in the Composers Desktop Project of the mid-1980s—diverted energy away from informal college gigs.[36, 38]


Second, the departure of the primary "drivers" of the scene—students like Andrew Bentley moving to Finland in 1976 and Trevor Wishart completing his PhD—left a leadership vacuum in the student social organizations that had organized the Phase events.[2, 36, 39] Finally, the economic recession of the mid-1970s and subsequent funding freezes at the University of York, mentioned in the biographies of Vice-Chancellor Morris Carstairs (1973–1978), likely impacted the budget for student-led extracurricular happenings.[21]


### Factors Influencing the Cessation of Multimedia Gigs

- **Academic Shift:** Transition from live "happenings" to academic acousmatic research and computer music.[2, 12]
- **Personnel Turnover:** Graduation of key organizers and the disbanding of Gentle Fire in 1976.[2, 16]
- **Regulatory Pressures:** Increasing fire safety concerns regarding the use of liquid light (hot oil) and dry ice in student residential halls.[26, 40]
- **Economic Context:** The 1970s recession limiting university expansion and extracurricular funding.[21]

## Synthesis and Legacy
The period of 1970–1975 at the University of York stands as a unique juncture in the history of British music, where the briefcase-format VCS3 became the catalyst for a new kind of audiovisual theater. The Phase-1 and Phase-2 events were not just concerts; they were "happenings" that utilized every available technological and architectural resource to create an immersive, surrealist experience.[5, 6] While the "weird guy with the dry ice" and the surrealistic slides have faded from the institutional memory, their impact persists in the pioneering work of Wishart, Bentley, and Smalley, who transformed the raw energy of these college gigs into a disciplined and influential school of electroacoustic thought.[2, 12, 36] The "safe" ending of this period was the necessary prelude to the studio’s evolution into one of the world's leading centers for digital audio research, a transformation that nonetheless owes its creative spark to the experimental "thangs" of the early 1970s.[38, 41]


The legacy of the *York Pop Music Project* and the *Electronic Music from York* triple LP remains the most potent evidence of this era, proving that in the resonant stairwells of York, the "spirits were high," and the boundaries of musical possibility were permanently expanded.[7, 8, 20, 42]
