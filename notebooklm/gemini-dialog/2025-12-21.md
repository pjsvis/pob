## 4. The Mechanics of the Void: Hyper-Space and Vector Theory
The defining innovation of *4000 A.D.*—and the feature that likely "rings a bell" for anyone who played it—is its movement system. ### 4.1 The Expanding Sphere of Probability
The game's manual describes hyper-space not as a tunnel or a warp lane, but as a dimension co-existent with normal space. This is a profound ludological implementation of quantum uncertainty principles (albeit simplified). While a fleet is in warp, it is *potentially* everywhere on the surface of that expanding sphere simultaneously. It only "collapses" into a definite position when the player chooses to exit warp.[3]


### 4.2 The Mechanics of Hidden Intent
In gameplay practice, this theory translates into a system of "Open Data, Hidden Destination." The procedure for movement is rigorously defined:


1. **Departure (Public Information):** The player announces that a fleet is leaving a specific star system at a specific time. A Warp Marker is placed to signify this event.
2. **Transit (Partial Information):** The player tracks the *distance* the fleet has traveled. In some editions, this involves a slider or a secret log, while the opponents can see the *magnitude* of the move but not the vector.
3. **Arrival (The Reveal):** A ship may leave warp "at any time".[4] When the player decides to materialize the fleet, they reveal the destination.

The tension arises because the opponents know the *radius* of the threat but not the specific *angle*. If a fleet has been in warp for three turns, every enemy star system within a three-turn radius is a potential target. This forces opponents to defend probabilistically. They must look at the map and ask, "Which of my stars is the most valuable? Which is the most vulnerable?" and allocate their defenses accordingly. This creates a psychological game of poker played with starships, where bluffing—launching a fleet that *could* hit a vital target but actually loops back to reinforce a different sector—is a valid tactic.[5]

This intellectual rigor is a hallmark of the game and a primary reason it is remembered as "pure strategy" with no chance elements.[1]

This system is an extreme application of Lanchester's Square Law, which models attrition warfare. In *4000 A.D.*, however, the "efficiency" of superior numbers is infinite. This design choice has profound strategic implications:


1. **The Imperative of Concentration:** There is absolutely no benefit to splitting forces unless it is a calculated feint. The "Doomstack" (a term from modern strategy gaming referring to a single, massive army) is the optimal strategy.

**The Calculation of Superiority:**
If Player A powers their Beam to level 8, and Player B powers their Screen to level 5, the calculation is a simple subtraction:
$$\text{Damage} = \text{Beam Strength} (8) - \text{Screen Strength} (5) = 3 \text{ Damage}$$
