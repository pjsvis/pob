| **Credibility** | Often reduces the perceived sociability and trustworthiness of the speaker.[18, 20] | May be viewed as a sign of deceit or lack of self-control by critical audiences.[20] |
| **Attention** | Commands more attention and can improve short-term memory of the message.[18] | Helps a message break through a crowded media environment.[18, 19] |


The effectiveness of swearing as a persuasive tool often occurs without the audience's conscious awareness. For instance, while respondents may perceive swearing as a sign of deceit, its presence can actually increase the believability of certain statements by signaling "intensity" and "sincerity".[20] This positive reinforcement creates a feedback loop: politicians learn from experience that transgressive language reinforces their message, leading to the further normalization of incivility.[20]


### Digital Mimicry and the Contagion of Incivility
The "Trump effect" on rhetorical styles is perhaps most visible in the digital sphere, where transgressive behavior is amplified by the design of social media networks.[21] Research examining comments on YouTube found that offensive behavior is highly contagious through two primary mechanisms:


1. **Mimicry Effect:** This is an automatic and often unconscious interpersonal synchronization where users imitate the emotional and behavioral cues of others, including gestures, postures, and linguistic styles.[17, 22]
2. **Social Interaction/Norm Learning Effect:** Users conform their expressions to the perceived norms of their social network to maintain a sense of belonging. Moral outrage and swearing are especially likely to increase over time through social reinforcement (likes and shares).[21, 23]

In the context of the 2016 campaign, "public swearing" (general venting of emotion) in a parent comment was shown to trigger chains of "interpersonal swearing" (direct attacks on individuals) in child comments.[17, 23] This suggests that a leader’s display of offensiveness does not just "trickle down"—it transforms the community-wide linguistic norms, making aggression a spreadable social practice.[23, 24]


## Mechanisms of Democratic Backsliding and Backlash
The normalization of deviance and the decay of decorum are not isolated aesthetic failures; they are core components of democratic backsliding. This process is characterized by the gradual undermining of institutions that sustain competition and accountability, often led by executives who capitalize on popular dissatisfaction with "establishment" norms.[25, 26]


### The Playbook: Populism, Polarization, and Post-Truth
The "Anti-Autocracy Handbook" identifies a common playbook built around the "3 Ps," which serve to erode democratic resilience.[26]


- **Populism:** Leaders present themselves as the exclusive voice of "the people" against a "corrupt elite." This justifies the dismantling of institutional checks and balances as being inherently democratic.[26, 27]
- **Polarization:** Societal divisions are inflamed, creating an "us versus them" mentality. As affective polarization increases, partisans become more likely to favor institutional norms that offer their own side a strategic advantage, leading to "hypocritical" support for democracy.[13, 26]
- **Post-Truth:** Facts and expertise are undermined to avoid accountability. This includes the "Serengeti Strategy," where autocrats selectively target individual scholars or institutions to silence dissent.[26]

### The Erosion of Forbearance and Mutual Toleration
Democratic stability relies on "informal norms" that go beyond the written law. Chief among these are mutual toleration—the recognition of political opponents as legitimate rivals—and institutional forbearance—the restraint practiced by officials in the exercise of their legal powers.[7, 28]


Institutional forbearance involves refraining from actions that are "strictly speaking, compatible with democracy's rules, but are not commonly known to be" or that violate the "spirit" of the law.[28] When leaders abandon forbearance—such as by refusing to recognize the legitimacy of an election or by using executive orders to bypass the legislature—they create uncertainty that undermines the ability of citizens to coordinate a response to genuine abuses of power in the future.[7, 28]


### Resistance and Its Limits
Research suggests that the effectiveness of resistance to backsliding depends on the domain being attacked and the degree of legislative control held by the backsliding leader.


| Domain of Resistance | Relative Effectiveness | Key Determinant |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **The Judiciary** | Most Effective | Ability to maintain institutional autonomy and public trust.[29] |
| **The Bureaucracy** | Moderately Effective | Resilience of professional and technocratic norms against political interference.[29] |
| **Civil Society** | Least Effective (Initially) | Effectiveness is "accumulative" and requires non-polarized, mass mobilization.[29] |


Institutional resistance is nearly neutralized when a backsliding leader gains supermajority control of the legislature, as opposition actors can be disqualified and the electoral playing field tilted through "legal" channels.[29] This underscores the importance of the judiciary as the final line of defense against executive overreach.[15, 29]


## Correctives and Institutional Safeguards
Reversing the normalization of deviance and restoring institutional integrity requires a combination of structural reforms and a recommitment to the norms of conduct.


### Proposals for Supreme Court Reform
To address the perception of "ownership" and restore public trust, a growing consensus of scholars and policymakers has proposed significant reforms to the Supreme Court.[14, 30]


1. **Term Limits and Staggered Appointments:** The establishment of 18-year terms for justices would ensure that a vacancy opens every two years, allowing each president to make two appointments per term.[30, 31] This would lower the "toxicity" of the confirmation process by reducing the stakes of any single nomination and preventing any individual from wielding unchecked power for decades.[14, 30]
2. **Enforceable Ethics Code:** Proposals for a binding code of conduct aim to replace the Court's current self-regulatory scheme, which critics view as "toothless".[14, 32] Such a code would require justices to recuse themselves in cases of financial or ideological conflict, ensuring that "nobody is the judge in their own case".[14, 32]
3. **Institutional Forbearance as a Corrective:** Beyond legal changes, leaders are encouraged to return to the practice of forbearance. This involves a "self-enforcement mechanism" where officials refrain from maximizing their prerogatives to avoid triggering mass resistance or permanently damaging the political culture.[28, 33]

### Stigma Management and the Reversal of Deviance
Deviance can also be resisted through "stigma management." States and institutions that are stigmatized as "deviant" by the international community or by their own citizens can employ several strategies:


- **Stigma Recognition:** Acknowledging the deviance and working to rectify it (e.g., post-war Germany).[34]
- **Stigma Rejection:** Denying the label and asserting normality.[34]
- **Counter-Stigmatization:** Turning the tables on the stigmatizers, labeling them as the truly "transgressive" ones.[34, 35]

In a domestic context, "politicizing regular deviance" can sometimes be used to reverse the degradation of a group's identity. By framing deviant practices as "conscious acts of resistance," marginalized groups can create "counter-publics" that challenge dominant, normalized frameworks.[36, 37]


## The Role of the Responsible Citizen
In a period of democratic backsliding and normalized deviance, the role of the responsible citizen is to maintain vigilance and engage in practices that support institutional and societal resilience.


### Psychological Strategies for Objective Perspective
Individual-level psychological processes contribute significantly to polarization. Citizens can employ several techniques to mitigate these effects:


- **Perspective Taking:** Actively considering the world through the eyes of another can increase empathy and reduce side-taking, which is often encouraged by social media algorithms.[38]
- **Reducing Psychological Distance:** Issues like institutional decay often feel distant. Research shows that when threats feel "personally relevant" and "close," people are more likely to perceive them as morally objectionable and act upon them.[39]
- **Correcting Misperceived Norms:** "False polarization" occurs when citizens overestimate the extremity of the "other side." Interventions that correct these misperceived norms can soften opposing stances and facilitate cooperation on superordinate goals like national stability.[40, 41]

### Engaging in Constructive Public Discourse
The re-establishment of civil discourse is essential for democratic survival. This involves an intentional exchange of ideas that is constructive and respectful.[42]


- **Self-Reflection:** Before entering a discussion, individuals should assess their own biases and emotional triggers.[42]
- **Active Listening and Validation:** Demonstrating a commitment to understanding rather than debating by paraphrasing the other's perspective and asking open-ended questions.[42, 43]
- **Use of "I" Statements:** Framing disagreements in a way that minimizes personal attack and focuses on the issue (e.g., "I feel concerned when..." rather than "You are wrong to...").[42, 44]
- **Finding Common Ground:** Identifying shared underlying values even when policy preferences differ.[42, 43]

### Strategic Action and Digital Safety
The "Anti-Autocracy Handbook" provides a tiered framework for citizens and scholars to navigate varying levels of personal risk.


1. **Risk-Independent Steps:** Engaging in self-care, building awareness of the authoritarian playbook, and maintaining social capital within trust-based networks.[26, 45]
2. **Documentation and Archiving:** Creating distributed archives and ongoing documentation of institutional changes to prevent the erasure of history and maintain future accountability.[26]
3. **Strategic Litigation and Advocacy:** Supporting legal efforts that challenge executive overreach, protect voting rights, and respond to the intimidation of judicial actors.[46]
4. **Citizens Assemblies:** Participating in representative assemblies that bring diverse citizens together to deliberate on social issues, creating a "jury duty for political deliberation" that can bypass partisan impasses.[38]

## Synthesis: Re-establishing the Standards of Democracy
The normalization of deviance is not an inevitable fate but a consequence of a collective decrease in vigilance and the prioritization of "faster, better, cheaper" political outcomes over long-term institutional health.[6] The perception of a "captured" judiciary and the contagion of incivility are warning signs of a system migrating toward the boundary of functional failure. Reversing this drift requires an "exhaustive fight for standards every single day," injecting energy into the system through both structural reform and the daily practice of institutional forbearance and civil discourse.[4]


The responsible citizen must recognize that the "crown jewels" of the Republic—its independent courts and its norms of mutual toleration—are not self-sustaining.[5] They require active protection against the corrosive feedback loops that reward norm-breaking in the pursuit of temporary efficiency or partisan gain. By maintaining an objective perspective, correcting misperceived norms, and holding leaders accountable to the "Work-as-Imagined" of the constitutional order, the citizenry can serve as the ultimate corrective to the normalization of deviance.[4, 26, 40]


The future of American democracy depends on the ability of its participants to resist the "comforting narrative" that disasters are only caused by a few "bad apples" and to instead confront the cultural and social processes that lead entire systems to "unknowingly accept doing the wrong thing".[4, 6] Only through such a comprehensive and disciplined approach can the Republic restore its institutions and its public discourse to the standards of excellence required for a stable and free society.
