### Gemini

Managing a fleet of silos at corporate scale requires moving from "manual craft" to "industrial orchestration." If every silo is an island, you end up with **Agent Sprawl**, where costs become opaque and logic is duplicated.


The strategy for 2026 is a **Hierarchical Hub-and-Spoke** model. You don't just "make more silos"; you create specialized **Orchestrator Silos** (the Hubs) that manage the lifecycle and state of **Worker Silos** (the Spokes).


I've updated the business case to include a "Scaling & Orchestration" section that outlines how to use `td`, `sidecar`, and your current stack to manage this complexity.


### Key Orchestration Principles:

1. **Shared Meaning, Not Just Data:** Don't just pass strings between silos. Use structured JSON payloads (enforced by your Hono/Bun layer) so the child silo knows exactly what its "Stuff" is.
2. **Explicit Handoffs:** Use the `outbox/` and `inbox/` folders. When a worker finishes, it writes to `outbox/`. The Orchestrator's `sidecar` detects the file, ingests it, and passes it to the next worker's `inbox/`.
3. **Bifrost as the Governor:** You can set a "Budget Cap" in Bifrost for an Orchestrator. If its children are too chatty, the Hub can kill the processes before they burn the quarterly budget.

How do you want to handle the "Silo Registry"—as a simple git-based manifest or a more dynamic API-driven service?


For a deeper dive into the mechanics of these multi-agent patterns, you may find [Multi-Agent Design Patterns: Skills, Sub-Agents, HandOff, and Router](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YGhH2hMdw8g) useful.


This video is relevant because it explains the exact structural transition from single agents to the hierarchical "Supervisor" and "Router" patterns needed to manage scaling complexity in corporate environments.
