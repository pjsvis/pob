There are two types of companies today:

1. Startups birthed out of AI
2. Existing companies figuring out how to adapt to AI

This week’s episode is all about #2 because we get to hear from Ioana Teleanu (  / ioanateleanu  ) who was the first AI designer at Miro and is now a dedicated AI+Design consultant.

What to do if you’re already feeling AI fatigue

### Gemini

In a recent episode of the Dive Club 🤿 podcast, host Rid invited Ioana Teleanu, the first AI designer at Miro and now an AI+Design consultant, to discuss the strategic integration of artificial intelligence into existing products. Teleanu drew upon her extensive experience to offer a nuanced perspective on the challenges and opportunities that AI presents to established companies.


A central theme of the conversation was the immense pressure companies feel to adopt AI, often leading to poorly integrated features that detract from the user experience. Teleanu contrasted the successful, almost "invisible" AI implementation by companies like Loom, which addresses clear user friction points, with the "ridiculous" and unhelpful AI suggestions within WhatsApp's search function. She argued that the most effective use of AI is to solve genuine user problems and streamline workflows, rather than adding "sparkles everywhere in a pointless, mindless way."


Drawing on her time at Miro, a platform beloved for its open-ended and collaborative nature, Teleanu highlighted the delicate balance between leveraging AI for efficiency and preserving the human elements of creativity and collaboration. She questioned whether increased AI usage is always a positive metric, suggesting that in some contexts, it can create "crutches" and hinder the valuable "grunt work of thinking." For instance, while AI can synthesize sticky notes from a workshop, it may also eliminate the crucial human process of nuanced discussion and collaborative sense-making.


Teleanu emphasized the importance of a human-centered and professional approach to AI design, advocating for a deep understanding of user pain points as the starting point for any AI integration. She shared that at Miro, the most successful AI applications, or "accelerators," were those that helped users overcome moments of adversity, such as starting with a blank canvas or making sense of unstructured data.


Looking to the future, Teleanu predicted a convergence of design, product management, and even engineering roles into a single "creator" or "builder" position. She advised designers to broaden their skill sets by developing a strong "product mind," which includes understanding business acumen, company strategy, and cross-functional collaboration. She believes that "taste" – the ability to discern and create a beautiful and effective user experience – will become a key differentiator for designers in an increasingly automated world.


Addressing the prevalent issue of "AI fatigue," Teleanu empathized with designers feeling overwhelmed by the hype. Her advice was to focus on a trusted few sources of information and to engage with new technologies out of genuine curiosity rather than pressure. She concluded by stressing that designers have a "moral duty" to experiment with AI, as their collective insights and critiques will be instrumental in shaping a more thoughtful and human-centric technological future. Ultimately, she believes the current frenzy will normalize, and AI will become an embedded, less explicit part of the design process.
