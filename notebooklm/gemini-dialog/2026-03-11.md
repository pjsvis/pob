# Strategic Reorientation of Saudi Arabian Petroleum Logistics: The Red Sea Corridor and the Petroline Infrastructure
The global petroleum market has historically operated under the shadow of the Strait of Hormuz, a maritime chokepoint through which approximately 20 million barrels per day of crude oil and petroleum products transit, representing nearly 25% of the world’s seaborne oil trade.[1, 2] For the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, the world's preeminent crude oil exporter, this geographical reality has long presented a strategic vulnerability. The Kingdom's primary production centers and its two largest export terminals, Ras Tanura and Ju'aymah, are situated on the Persian Gulf, making the majority of its 6 to 6.4 million barrels per day in exports dependent on the stability of the Strait.[1, 3] To mitigate this risk, Saudi Arabia has spent decades developing a western logistical axis centered on the city of Yanbu and the East-West Pipeline, also known as the Petroline.[3]


In the wake of the March 2026 regional crisis, which resulted in the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the forced shutdown of the Ras Tanura refinery following aerial attacks, this western corridor has transitioned from a strategic reserve to the primary lifeline of the Saudi energy sector.[3, 4] The extent to which the Kingdom is currently utilizing the Yanbu facility reflects an unprecedented emergency activation of cross-country transport capacity, refinery integration, and international pipeline cooperation.[5, 6, 7]


## The Technical Evolution of the East-West Pipeline System
The East-West Pipeline is a 1,200-kilometer (746-mile) engineering achievement that spans the width of the Arabian Peninsula, connecting the world's largest crude processing facilities at Abqaiq in the Eastern Province to the Red Sea port of Yanbu.[3, 8] The system was originally commissioned in 1981, during the height of the Iran-Iraq War, as a sovereign insurance policy against the exact maritime interdiction scenarios currently unfolding.[3, 8] The pipeline consists of twin 48-inch diameter pipes, supported by 13 high-capacity pumping stations that maintain the necessary pressure to move diverse crude grades across the kingdom's central plateau.[3, 8]


Historically, the Petroline’s nameplate capacity was rated at 5 million barrels per day (mbpd).[3, 4, 5] However, recent strategic assessments and infrastructure "debottlenecking" programs have expanded this theoretical throughput to 7 mbpd.[5, 9] This expansion is achieved through the tactical conversion of adjacent natural gas liquids (NGL) pipelines, which are normally dedicated to transporting 300,000 bpd of gas feedstock to the western industrial cities.[1, 3, 4] During periods of critical supply disruption, such as the 2019 Abqaiq crisis and the 2026 Hormuz closure, these NGL lines are reconfigured to carry crude oil, providing a vital 2 mbpd "surge" capacity.[3, 4]


### Historical and Surge Throughput Metrics
The utilization of the Petroline has traditionally remained well below its maximum limits, as the Kingdom prioritized the lower-cost maritime routes through the Persian Gulf. In the period preceding the current crisis, the pipeline was operating at approximately 3.2 mbpd, primarily supplying western domestic refineries and a modest volume of Red Sea exports.[3] As of March 2026, Saudi Aramco has reached the operational zenith of this system, with throughput approaching the full 7 mbpd mark.[5, 9, 10]

## Yanbu as a Strategic Refining and Storage Hub
Yanbu is not merely a terminal for the export of raw crude; it is a sophisticated industrial ecosystem designed to capture value across the hydrocarbon chain. The integration of domestic refining with the Petroline system ensures that the Kingdom can meet its internal energy needs while maintaining an export surplus.[5] Nearly 2 mbpd of the Petroline's current 7 mbpd capacity is dedicated to supplying three major refineries located in Yanbu.[5, 13]


- **Yanbu Aramco Sinopec Refining Company (YASREF):** A joint venture with a capacity of 400,000 bpd, optimized to produce high-specification transportation fuels for global markets.[13, 14]
- **Saudi Aramco Mobil Refinery (SAMREF):** A 400,000 bpd facility that plays a critical role in regional fuel distribution.[13]
- **Aramco Yanbu Refinery:** A wholly-owned facility with a capacity of 240,000 bpd, primarily serving the domestic consumption needs of the Western Province.[13]

This refining cluster acts as a buffer for the Petroline. When maritime export conditions in the Red Sea are unfavorable—such as during periods of heightened Houthi missile activity at the Bab el-Mandeb—the crude can be diverted to these refineries, ensuring that the pipeline remains operational and that production at eastern oil fields does not have to be shut in immediately.[5, 13, 15]

During the 2026 crisis, the volume of Saudi crude entering the SUMED system has doubled.[20, 22] VLCCs loading at Yanbu sail north to Ain Sokhna, where they discharge their cargo into the pipeline.[10, 17, 21] This process is particularly vital because fully laden VLCCs cannot transit the Suez Canal; they must discharge part of their load into SUMED, transit the canal at a lighter draft, and then reload the volume at the Mediterranean terminal of Sidi Kerir.[10, 17]

## Geopolitical Risks and Constraints on the Western Axis
While the Petroline and Yanbu offer a significant bypass of the Strait of Hormuz, they do not provide absolute immunity from geopolitical risk. The "extent" of their utilization is currently curtailed by several factors that prevent the Red Sea from being a total substitute for the Persian Gulf.


### The Bab el-Mandeb Chokepoint
Shipments from Yanbu destined for Asia-Pacific markets—traditionally Saudi Arabia's largest customers—must pass south through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait.[10, 13, 17] This waterway is currently under threat from Houthi militants in Yemen, who have resumed attacks on commercial shipping in 2026.[10, 13] This forces tankers into a difficult choice: transit a second dangerous chokepoint or sail north through Suez and around the Cape of Good Hope, a 14-day detour that adds significant freight costs and sailing time.[10, 13, 17]


### Field Shut-ins and Production Realities

By early March 2026, the Marjan, Zuluf, Safaniya, and Abu Safa fields were reportedly shut in, taking approximately 2 million to 2.5 million bpd of regional supply offline.[15, 25] This reality underscores that while the Kingdom can reroute nearly 50% of its exports, it cannot yet bypass the Strait of Hormuz for its entire production capacity.[3]
