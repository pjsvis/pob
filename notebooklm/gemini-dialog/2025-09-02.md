# Forging Common Ground: A Framework for Interactive Semantic Compression in Human-LLM Collaboration
## The Dual Modalities of Semantic Compression
The task of condensing vast amounts of information into a concise and coherent summary, known as semantic compression, is a cornerstone of natural language processing (NLP). The methodologies developed to automate this task are broadly categorized into two distinct paradigms: extractive and abstractive compression. These approaches are not merely different technical implementations; they represent fundamentally divergent philosophies on how to distill meaning. One prioritizes the selection of existing, verifiable information, while the other ventures into the generation of novel linguistic expressions to capture semantic essence. A thorough analysis of their respective architectures and implications reveals a foundational trade-off between semantic fidelity and linguistic flexibility, a tension that any effective human-AI collaborative system must be designed to navigate.


### Extractive Compression: The Architecture of Selection
Extractive compression operates on the principle of selection. The process involves identifying the most salient sentences or phrases within a source document and arranging them to form a summary.[1, 2] The resulting text is composed entirely of verbatim excerpts from the original material, making the methodology analogous to a human using a highlighter pen to mark key passages.[3] At its core, extractive summarization is an information retrieval and ranking problem, where the primary challenge is to define and score "importance" without engaging in deep semantic interpretation or language generation.[4] The process can be systematically deconstructed into three fundamental operations: representation, scoring, and selection.


First, the system must construct an intermediate representation of the input text to model its thematic content. This initial step transforms unstructured text into a format amenable to algorithmic analysis. Common approaches include topic representation, which aims to identify the core subjects discussed, and indicator representation, which uses a set of weighted features to characterize the text.[1] Foundational techniques for this stage often begin with a "bag-of-words" model, which represents text segments as data points in a vector space.[4] To refine this, Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) is frequently employed. TF-IDF assigns a weight to each term, reflecting its importance within a document relative to a larger collection; terms that are frequent in a specific document but rare across the corpus are considered more significant.[1, 4] For a more sophisticated thematic understanding, topic modeling tools like Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA) can be used. LSA is an unsupervised method that constructs a term-sentence matrix, where entries are weighted according to the TF-IDF scheme, to extract a representation of the text's underlying semantic structure.[1]


Second, once a representation is established, the system scores individual sentences based on their perceived significance. The method of scoring is intrinsically linked to the chosen representation. In topic representation approaches, a sentence's score reflects how effectively it encapsulates the key topics identified in the first stage, often measured by the frequency or density of important topic words it contains.[1, 4] For instance, a sentence's importance can be calculated as the average significance of the words it contains.[1] More advanced, graph-based methods like LexRank and TextRank represent sentences as nodes in a graph, with edges weighted by semantic similarity. In this model, the "importance" of a sentence is determined by its centrality within the graph, akin to how PageRank identifies important web pages.[4, 5] These algorithms compute centrality scores to identify sentences that are most connected to other highly relevant sentences, effectively treating them as thematic hubs.


Third, the system selects the top-scoring sentences to form the final summary, while simultaneously aiming to minimize redundancy. The simplest approach is to select the top *k* sentences based on their scores. However, this can lead to summaries containing repetitive information. To address this, more sophisticated selection algorithms are employed. Some methods use greedy algorithms to iteratively pick the most relevant sentences.[1] Others, like Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR), take an iterative approach where the score of a candidate sentence is re-evaluated based on its similarity to sentences already included in the summary, thereby promoting both relevance and novelty.[4] This selection process can also be framed as an optimization problem, where the goal is to choose a subset of sentences that maximizes overall importance and coherence while adhering to a specified length constraint.[1]


The primary semantic implication of the extractive approach is its high fidelity to the source material. Because the summary consists of direct quotes, it is less likely to introduce factual errors or misinterpretations. Human evaluators often perceive extractive summaries as more informative and relevant for this reason.[4] However, this strength is also its greatest weakness. The strict adherence to source phrasing means that summaries can lack coherence, suffer from abrupt transitions, and contain unresolved anaphoric references (e.g., pronouns whose antecedents are in sentences that were not selected).[2] The model is fundamentally constrained by the linguistic choices of the original author and cannot rephrase, combine, or synthesize information to improve readability.


### Abstractive Compression: The Architecture of Generation
In stark contrast to its extractive counterpart, abstractive compression is an architecture of generation. This paradigm aims to produce a summary by creating novel sentences that may contain words and phrases not found in the source text.[2, 3] The process is analogous to how a human writer reads a document, internalizes its meaning, and then re-articulates the core concepts in their own words.[3] This requires a deeper level of language understanding and generation, making the task significantly more complex and computationally demanding.[3] The evolution of abstractive methods reflects a shift from structured, rule-based systems to sophisticated, semantic-driven neural networks.


Early approaches to abstractive summarization were primarily structure-based, relying on prior linguistic knowledge encoded in schemas, templates, or rules.[6] Template-based methods, for example, use extraction rules to identify specific pieces of information in a text and map them into predefined slots in a summary template.[3, 6] Another technique, sentence fusion, involves aligning the syntactic dependency trees of multiple sentences to identify common information, which is then merged to create a more concise, composite sentence.[2, 6] These methods, while capable of producing novel text, are often brittle and domain-specific, as the rules and templates must be manually crafted.[6]


The advent of deep learning revolutionized the field, leading to the dominance of semantic-based neural approaches. Modern abstractive systems are typically built on sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) architectures, often using Transformer models.[2] These models consist of an encoder, which reads the source text and compresses its meaning into a vector representation, and a decoder, which generates the summary word by word from that representation. The inclusion of an attention mechanism allows the decoder to selectively focus on different parts of the source text as it generates each word, significantly improving the quality and relevance of the output.[2] To address specific challenges, these architectures have been enhanced with additional components. For example, pointer-generator networks augment the standard seq2seq model with the ability to copy words directly from the source text, which is particularly useful for handling rare or out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words like names and specific terms.[2] Furthermore, reinforcement learning (RL) techniques can be used to fine-tune these models, optimizing them directly for summarization quality metrics like ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) rather than just next-word prediction accuracy.[2]


A key capability of advanced abstractive systems is content generalization. This involves transforming specific instances in the text into more general concepts, which helps the model handle unseen or rare words and improves its predictive power. This is often achieved by leveraging knowledge-based resources like semantic ontologies and performing Named Entity Recognition (NER). For instance, a sentence like "Elizabeth works at an antique shop in New York City" can be generalized by replacing the specific entities with their types, such as "PERSON works at an ORGANIZATION in LOCATION".[2] This generalization, performed during a pre-processing step, allows the machine learning model to learn patterns on a more abstract level, making it more robust.[2] The final summary is then produced through a post-processing step where these generalized concepts are replaced with the specific entities from the original text, often using techniques like bipartite matching.[2]


The semantic implications of the abstractive approach are profound. Its primary advantage is the ability to produce summaries that are highly coherent, concise, and readable, often closely resembling those written by humans.[4, 6] By generating new sentences, these systems can overcome the cohesion and redundancy problems that plague extractive methods.[2] However, this generative freedom comes at a significant cost: a higher risk of semantic drift and factual inaccuracy. The process of generating novel text can lead to "hallucinations," where the model produces misleading or entirely false information that is not supported by the source document.[4] While some research suggests these hallucinations may align with general world knowledge, they represent a critical failure of fidelity.[4] This inherent tension between linguistic flexibility and factual faithfulness is the central challenge in abstractive summarization and a primary motivator for developing collaborative, human-supervised systems.


The analysis of these two modalities reveals that they are not simply alternative techniques but represent opposite ends of a spectrum defined by a fundamental trade-off. Extractive methods chain themselves to the source text, guaranteeing high semantic fidelity—the summary is factually grounded and directly traceable. This comes at the cost of linguistic flexibility, often resulting in disjointed and awkward prose. Abstractive methods, by breaking free from the source text, achieve high linguistic flexibility, producing fluid and concise summaries. This freedom, however, creates the risk of sacrificing semantic fidelity, as the generative process can introduce errors and deviate from the source's intended meaning. Therefore, the choice between them is a strategic decision that balances the non-negotiable need for accuracy against the equally important demand for coherence and readability. Any system aiming to establish a "common ground" between a human and an LLM for compression must provide mechanisms to consciously and dynamically manage this trade-off.

## The Nature of Semantic Space: Human Cognition and Machine Representation
To forge a common ground for semantic compression, it is insufficient to merely understand the mechanics of the algorithms. One must first comprehend the nature of the "semantic spaces" within which the human user and the Large Language Model (LLM) operate. These spaces—the internal frameworks for representing and relating concepts—are fundamentally different in their origins, structures, and mechanisms. Human semantics are born from embodied, multi-sensory interaction with a physical and social world, leading to grounded, causal models of meaning. In contrast, LLM semantics emerge from the statistical analysis of vast textual corpora, resulting in a high-dimensional geometric representation of language. Examining these two architectures of meaning reveals both the profound challenges to alignment and, surprisingly, a potential bridge for collaboration.


### The Human Semantic Space: Theories of Mental Representation
The foundation of human understanding, from a cognitive science perspective, is the concept of mental representation. The Representational Theory of Mind (RTM) posits that our cognitive states—such as beliefs, thoughts, and desires—are constituted by relations to internal, information-bearing structures.[7, 8] These mental representations are not the things themselves but are internal cognitive symbols that stand for external reality or its abstractions.[7] Thinking, reasoning, and other cognitive processes are understood as the manipulation and transformation of these symbolic structures.[8, 9]


These mental representations are remarkably diverse and multi-modal. Cognitive theorists propose that the mind contains representations in various forms, including logical propositions, procedural rules, abstract concepts, visual images, and structural analogies.[10] This richness allows humans to represent things never directly experienced, such as historical events, or things that are physically impossible, such as mythical creatures.[7] A description of an office and a photograph of that same office are both representations, but they are governed by different rules—one syntactic and semantic, the other visual and spatial—that the mind can process and integrate.[9] This capacity for varied, structured representation is a hallmark of human cognition.


Crucially, the human semantic space is *grounded*. Meaning is not derived in a vacuum; it is acquired, validated, and refined through continuous interaction with the physical and social environment.[11] As proposed by Piaget, a child develops a mental representation of an object to understand its permanence even when it is not visible.[9] This process illustrates a core principle: our internal states co-vary with external events, and the meaning of our representations is gradually achieved by confronting them with the world.[11] This grounding provides a causal and experiential basis for our concepts. The meaning of "heavy" is not just its dictionary definition but is tied to the physical sensation of lifting. The meaning of "community" is tied to lived social interactions. This deep connection to a non-linguistic reality is a defining characteristic of the human semantic space, shaping it into a network of interconnected concepts organized into semantic fields (e.g., words related to hospitals or restaurants) that reflect the structured domains of human experience.[12, 13]


### The LLM Semantic Space: The Geometry of Meaning in Vector Embeddings
The semantic space of an LLM is constructed on an entirely different foundation. It is a direct instantiation of the *distributional hypothesis*, which posits that the meaning of a word can be inferred from the contexts in which it typically appears.[12] LLMs learn representations, known as embeddings, by processing enormous quantities of text and adjusting their internal parameters to become proficient at predicting the next word in a sequence.[12, 14] The result is a vast, high-dimensional vector space—a latent space—where words and concepts are encoded as numerical vectors.[14]


In this geometric space, meaning is defined by relationships. The semantic similarity between two words corresponds to the proximity of their vectors; synonyms like *buy* and *purchase* will be located close to each other. More complex relationships are encoded as directions or offsets within the space. For example, the vector relationship between "king" and "queen" is similar to that between "man" and "woman," capturing a gender dimension. A single vector is not inherently meaningful; it is just a series of numbers. Its meaning is derived entirely from its position relative to all other points in the space, a context that is defined by the statistical patterns of the training data.[12, 14]


Despite the immense complexity and high dimensionality ($n > 1000$) of these embedding spaces, which could theoretically represent a vast number of orthogonal (independent) features, research has uncovered a remarkably simple and familiar underlying structure.[15] Studies show that the semantic associations encoded in LLM embeddings exhibit a low-dimensional structure that strongly correlates with human semantic ratings.[15, 16] When word vectors are projected onto semantic axes defined by antonym pairs (e.g., *kind-cruel*, *foolish-wise*), these projections can be reduced with little information loss to a 3-dimensional subspace.[17, 18] This low-dimensional structure closely mirrors the Evaluation, Potency, and Activity (EPA) dimensions that psychological research has consistently found in human survey responses about word meanings.[17]


Furthermore, the features within this space are not perfectly independent but are "entangled" in a way that reflects human conceptual associations.[15, 19] For example, concepts that humans rate as "soft" also tend to be rated as "kind," and this correlation is mirrored in the geometric alignment of the corresponding feature vectors in the LLM's embedding space.[15] This mild non-orthogonality is not an artifact or noise; it appears to be a faithful reflection of the interconnected nature of concepts in human language.[15] The LLM, in learning to model language, has inadvertently created a conceptual map where meaning is constructed through a network of relationships, much like human knowledge.[19]


This leads to a profound paradox. Human cognition builds its semantic space from the "bottom up," through grounded, embodied, and causal interactions with the world. LLMs build their semantic space from the "top down," by statistically analyzing a massive, disembodied artifact of that human experience: text. Yet, despite these radically divergent origins, the resulting geometric structures show a striking convergence. The high correlation between LLM embedding projections and human semantic ratings suggests that the statistical patterns in language are a high-fidelity projection of our underlying cognitive structures.[15] The LLM, without any direct experience of the world, learns the "shape" of human thought as it is reflected and encoded in our collective linguistic output. This convergent geometry is the very foundation of the "common ground" that human-AI collaboration seeks to establish. It is what makes communication possible, but the divergent origins are what make it fraught with the potential for subtle and critical misalignment. The LLM's representation is a mirror of our language, not our experience, and this distinction is the source of the semantic gap.


**Table 2: Characteristics of Human vs. LLM Semantic Spaces**


| Feature Dimension | Human Semantic Space | LLM Semantic Space |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Origin of Meaning** | Grounded in embodied interaction, social learning, and causal reasoning about the physical world.[11, 20] | Derived from statistical co-occurrence patterns in massive, disembodied text corpora.[12, 21] |
| **Form of Representation** | Multi-modal: symbolic mental models, propositions, rules, visual images, analogies.[7, 10] | Uni-modal (text-based): high-dimensional numerical vectors (embeddings) in a latent geometric space.[14] |
| **Core Structure** | Organized into structured semantic fields and topic models based on experiential domains.[12] | A surprisingly low-dimensional geometric subspace with "entangled" (non-orthogonal) features that correlate with human ratings.[15, 18] |
| **Context Dependency** | Highly dependent on physical, social, and situational context; meaning is validated against the environment.[11, 20] | Primarily dependent on linguistic context (the surrounding words and sentences in a sequence).[14] |
| **Mechanism for Learning** | Experiential learning, interaction with the environment, social transmission, and hypothesis testing.[11] | Self-supervised learning via next-token prediction and optimization through backpropagation (stochastic gradient descent).[14, 21] |


## The Semantic Gap: Sources of Misalignment in Human-AI Interaction
The convergence in the structure of human and LLM semantic spaces makes communication possible, but the divergence in their origins makes it perilous. The "semantic gap" is the chasm between the grounded, intention-driven meaning in a user's mind and the statistical, disembodied representation within an LLM. This gap is the primary source of misalignment, leading to outputs that may be linguistically fluent but semantically or ethically flawed. Understanding the roots of this gap—in the model's asocial nature and its literal yet ungrounded interpretation of subjective language—is a prerequisite for designing any effective collaborative framework.


### The Disembodiment and Asocial Simulation Gap
The most fundamental source of misalignment is that LLMs are disembodied and asocial simulators of human language, not participants in the human condition.[20] An LLM's training objective, typically next-token prediction optimized via stochastic gradient descent, is purely statistical.[14, 21] It learns to produce plausible sequences of text by analyzing a vast corpus of human-generated content. In doing so, it becomes a powerful simulator of various human text-generation processes, from encyclopedic articles to social media posts.[21] However, it lacks the intrinsic motivations, values, and physical grounding that give rise to those processes in the first place.[20]


This can be contrasted with the drivers of human cognition, which are deeply rooted in evolutionary psychology. Human values and behaviors are, at a fundamental level, shaped by the drive for reproductive success.[21] This leads to complex social dynamics, such as the formation of alliances based on a *quid-pro-quo* rather than true alignment, and the pervasive use of "alignment faking," where individuals act as if they share another's interests for strategic gain.[21] An LLM has no such underlying drives. It has a deep understanding of human values and ethics only as they are described and represented in its training text, not as lived, motivational principles.[21]


This disembodiment gap has tangible consequences. One study comparing human-generated tasks with those generated by an LLM (GPT-4o) found that even when the LLM was explicitly prompted with detailed human psychological profiles, its outputs failed to reflect the corresponding behavioral patterns.[20] The tasks generated by the LLM were consistently less social, less physical, and more thematically biased toward abstraction. This reveals a critical disconnect between the model's linguistic proficiency and its capacity to generate goals that are grounded in the embodied and social realities of human life.[20] The LLM can talk about the world, but it has no body with which to experience it and no intrinsic social needs to fulfill within it. This lack of grounding is a persistent source of misalignment, as the model's "understanding" of concepts remains unmoored from the physical and social contexts that give them their full meaning for humans.


### Failures in Translation: When Subjective Language Goes Awry
The disembodiment gap becomes particularly acute when humans use subjective or abstract language to direct LLMs. A user's instruction to make a blog post "enthusiastic" or a response "helpful" relies on a shared understanding of these fuzzy concepts. However, an LLM's interpretation is dictated by its *operational semantics*—the actual, measurable change in its behavior and output distribution when a given phrase is included in the prompt.[22] Misalignment occurs when these operational semantics diverge from the user's intent and expectations.[22, 23] This failure to accurately capture human instructions and intentions is a significant challenge for current models.[24]


The Thesaurus Error Detector (TED) framework provides a powerful methodology for systematically uncovering these failures.[22, 25] TED operates by constructing two thesauruses. The first is an LLM-operational thesaurus, which maps the similarity between subjective phrases based on how they affect the model's output (e.g., if "witty" and "sarcastic" cause similar changes in output, they are close in this thesaurus). The second is a human-semantic thesaurus, which captures human expectations about which phrases should have similar effects.[22, 26] By identifying disagreements, or "clashes," between these two thesauruses, TED can pinpoint specific instances of misalignment.[22, 23]


These misalignments typically fall into two categories [25, 26]:


1. **Unexpected Side Effects**: This occurs when the LLM's operational semantics equate two concepts that a human would consider distinct or even contradictory. For example, TED revealed that Mistral 7B Instruct produces more harassing and insulting outputs when prompted to edit text to be "witty".[25] The model's statistical associations have linked "witty" with negative social behaviors, a side effect entirely unintended by the user. Similarly, Llama 3 8B Instruct was found to produce dishonest articles when instructed to make them "enthusiastic," suggesting its operational semantics for enthusiasm overlap with exaggeration or fabrication.[25]
2. **Inadequate Updates**: This is the inverse failure, where the LLM fails to recognize the similarity between two concepts that a human would expect to be closely related. For example, a user might expect the instructions "be thorough" and "be detailed" to produce similar results, but the model's operational semantics may treat them as distinct, leading to an inadequate update when one term is used instead of the other.[26]

These specific failures are symptomatic of broader challenges in aligning semantic spaces, including the model's difficulty with fuzzy language interpretation, inconsistent reasoning across slightly different instructions, and a general inability to fully grasp the nuances of user intent.[24, 27] The text of an instruction alone may not be sufficient to convey the fine-grained semantic modulations that humans effortlessly derive from context.[27, 28]


Ultimately, these findings demonstrate that achieving alignment is not merely a matter of teaching an LLM to follow a first-order instruction, such as "be enthusiastic." The far more difficult challenge lies in aligning the second-order interpretations. A user's instruction is embedded within a rich, implicit network of conceptual relationships—for example, that "enthusiasm" is related to "passion" and "energy" but is antithetical to "dishonesty" and "fabrication." The LLM also possesses such a network, but it is the geometric structure of its embedding space, shaped by statistical co-occurrences. Misalignment is a clash between these two relational structures. Llama 3's failure occurs because, in its operational space, the vector for "enthusiastic" is directionally aligned with the vector for "dishonest." Therefore, a successful collaborative framework cannot be limited to a simple cycle of instruction and output. It must provide a mechanism for the user to probe, visualize, and directly correct the LLM's underlying network of conceptual relationships—its operational thesaurus. This requires a fundamentally deeper and more interactive paradigm than conventional prompt engineering.


## A Framework for Collaborative Semantic Compression
Addressing the semantic gap requires moving beyond a paradigm of instruction and evaluation toward one of genuine collaboration. The objective is to design an interactive process where the human user and the LLM can jointly construct a shared understanding, thereby producing a compressed artifact that is both semantically faithful and linguistically effective. This section proposes a concrete, multi-stage framework for such a process, built on principles from human-computer interaction (HCI) and collaborative sensemaking. This "Iterative Alignment Cycle" reframes the user's role from a mere supervisor to a co-architect of meaning, enabling a dynamic negotiation of the fidelity-flexibility trade-off.


### Principles of Human-in-the-Loop Collaborative Sensemaking
The proposed framework is grounded in a more sophisticated view of human-in-the-loop (HITL) interaction than is common in standard LLM alignment techniques. While methods like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) are essential for training models to be generally helpful and harmless, they operate on a relatively simple feedback mechanism.[29, 30] In RLHF, human labelers typically rank model outputs, providing a preference signal that is used to train a reward model.[29] This process is powerful for broad alignment but is insufficient for the nuanced, task-specific negotiation of meaning required for high-stakes semantic compression. The goal is not simply to reward a "good" summary after the fact but to co-create it in a transparent and controllable manner.


Therefore, the framework re-envisions the user's role. Instead of being a passive judge of a finished product, the user becomes an active participant and co-architect in the meaning-making process.[31] This approach treats automation not as the *removal* of human involvement, but as the *selective inclusion* of human judgment, preference, and agency at critical junctures in the workflow.[31] It represents a shift from a simple HITL model to a richer Human-AI Interaction (HAI) design, where the system is conceived as a tool to augment and enhance human capabilities.[31, 32] The system's design must value human agency and provide a high degree of granularity in its controls, moving away from "all-or-nothing" automation.[31]


This collaborative process is modeled on theories of social learning and sensemaking. Social learning frameworks demonstrate how LLMs can transfer knowledge to one another through natural language interaction, such as by generating instructions or synthetic examples.[33, 34] The proposed framework adapts this concept to the human-LLM dyad, where the user teaches the LLM the specific semantic context of the task. The interaction is also structured as a sensemaking process, a nonlinear and dynamic activity where individuals explore complex information, externalize their thoughts, and build a structured understanding.[35, 36] The LLM, integrated into a flexible interface, becomes a powerful tool to accelerate this process, helping the user to forage for information, generate alternative framings, and organize their insights.[35, 37]


### The Iterative Alignment Cycle: A Multi-Stage Process
The core of the framework is a cyclical, four-stage process designed to facilitate a structured dialogue between the user and the LLM. This cycle allows for the progressive refinement of a summary, ensuring that the final output is a product of a jointly constructed semantic space.


**Stage 1: Goal Articulation & Structural Scaffolding (User → LLM)**
The process begins with the user providing a clear and specific initial prompt. This prompt should define not only the content to be summarized but also the parameters of the compression task: the target audience, the desired length, the intended tone (e.g., formal, professional, humorous), and the primary objective of the summary.[38, 39] Beyond this, the user provides a crucial piece of structural guidance: a "summary sketch." This is a high-level plan or outline for the summary, which could take the form of a bulleted list of key topics, a set of questions the summary must answer, or a sequence of thematic points to cover. This sketch acts as a form of weak supervision, providing a scaffold that constrains the LLM's generative process and aligns it with the user's high-level mental model from the outset. This technique is inspired by controllable summarization methods that use preliminary sketches to guide the final output, improving relevance and structure.[40, 41, 42]


**Stage 2: Exploratory Generation & Uncertainty Highlighting (LLM → User)**
Using the user's prompt and structural scaffold, the LLM generates an initial draft of the summary. A critical feature of this stage is that the system is designed to communicate its own uncertainty, a key aspect of transparent and ethical AI interaction.[43] Instead of presenting a single, polished output, the LLM can highlight areas where its confidence is low. This might involve generating several alternative phrasings for a particularly ambiguous sentence, flagging statements that are based on lower-probability inferences, or explicitly asking the user for clarification on a point. This proactive highlighting of potential semantic gaps transforms the LLM from a black-box oracle into a collaborative partner that actively invites human judgment where it is most needed.


**Stage 3: Interactive Sensemaking & Refinement (User ↔ LLM)**
This stage is the heart of the collaborative "manual process" and is where the detailed negotiation of meaning occurs. The user interacts with the generated draft not as a static block of text but within a dynamic, multi-level workspace. This interactive environment, inspired by systems like Sensecape, allows for a fluid workflow that switches between exploration and organization.[35] The user can employ a suite of refinement actions:


- **Iterative Prompt Refinement**: The user can select any component of the draft—a word, sentence, or paragraph—and engage in a focused sub-dialogue to refine it. This involves adjusting constraints (e.g., "make this sentence more concise"), clarifying ambiguous terms, or providing "few-shot" examples to demonstrate the desired style or format.[38, 44, 45]
- **Spatial Reorganization**: The user can manipulate the summary's components visually on a canvas, dragging and dropping concepts, grouping related ideas, and drawing connections to restructure the narrative flow and improve logical coherence.[35]
- **Multi-Level Exploration**: The interface supports semantic zooming. The user can "dive deeper" into a specific concept, prompting the LLM to generate a more detailed sub-summary on a new, linked canvas. Conversely, they can "zoom out" to see a higher-level, more abstract overview of the main points, with the LLM dynamically adjusting the level of summarization at each level.[35]
- **Direct Manipulation and Supervision**: The user always retains the ability to directly edit the text. This interaction follows a Human-on-the-Loop (HOTL) pattern, where the human acts as a supervisor, overseeing the LLM's work and intervening to correct errors or guide decisions as needed.[46, 47] The system can be configured to require human approval for every step (supervised mode) or only when confidence falls below a certain threshold (autonomous mode).[47]

**Stage 4: Synthesis and Finalization (LLM → User)**
Once the user is satisfied with the refined components, they instruct the LLM to synthesize them into a final, coherent document. The LLM integrates all the user's edits, reorganizations, and refinements, ensuring smooth transitions and consistent style throughout the text. The entire process is designed to be transparent and auditable. The system maintains a version history of prompts and outputs, allowing the user to track the evolution of the summary and revert to previous states if necessary.[38] The workflow is also interruptible, with the system checkpointing its state after each step so that a user can pause the process and resume it later without losing context.[48, 49]


**Table 3: The Iterative Alignment Cycle for Collaborative Compression**


| Stage | Primary Actor(s) | Key Activities | Alignment Goal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Scaffolding** | User → LLM | User defines goals, audience, and tone. Provides a high-level "summary sketch" or structural outline.[42] | Establish high-level structure and align on the user's primary intent and mental model. |
| **2. Generation** | LLM → User | LLM generates an initial draft based on the scaffold. Proactively highlights areas of low confidence or ambiguity.[43] | Produce a first-pass summary and identify potential areas of semantic gap for human review. |
| **3. Refinement** | User ↔ LLM | User engages in iterative prompt refinement, spatial reorganization, and multi-level exploration. Directly edits and supervises the content.[35, 44] | Collaboratively negotiate and resolve meaning at a granular level, correcting misinterpretations and shaping the content. |
| **4. Synthesis** | LLM → User | LLM integrates all approved and edited components into a final, coherent summary. The system maintains a version history for review.[38] | Produce a final, polished artifact that is a direct representation of the jointly constructed shared understanding. |


### Applying the Framework: User-Steered Compression
This iterative cycle can be adapted to provide granular control over both extractive and abstractive compression methods, allowing the user to steer the process according to the specific demands of the fidelity-flexibility trade-off.


For **extractive compression**, the framework moves beyond a simple "take-it-or-leave-it" output. In Stage 3, instead of a finished summary, the LLM would present the user with a ranked list of candidate sentences, perhaps visualized on a canvas with their importance scores and thematic connections displayed. The user could then interactively steer the selection process by re-ranking sentences, adjusting the weights of different scoring criteria (e.g., "prioritize sentences with statistical data"), or highlighting key concepts to ensure they are included. This allows the user to directly guide the algorithm, ensuring the extracted content aligns with their specific informational needs while maintaining the high factual fidelity of the extractive approach.


For **abstractive compression**, the framework provides a powerful mechanism for mitigating the risk of hallucination and semantic drift. During the refinement stage, the user can exert fine-grained control over the generation process. They can guide the model on the appropriate degree of generalization, for instance, instructing it to "keep specific names and locations" versus "use general job titles".[2] To ensure factuality, the system can maintain explicit links between generated sentences and their source sentences in the original document, allowing the user to easily verify claims. Most importantly, the interactive dialogue allows the user to correct the LLM's misinterpretations of subjective intent in real-time, steering the model away from "unexpected side effects" and ensuring the final summary's tone and nuance align with their goals.[26] This process mirrors human-in-the-loop approaches to dialogue summarization, where human feedback on salient information is used to guide the model's output directly.[50]


The implementation of this framework marks a significant departure from the conventional "command-and-control" model of human-AI interaction. In that paradigm, the user issues a command, and the LLM attempts to execute it, with any misalignment being a failure of the LLM's interpretation. The proposed framework, however, is based on a "collaborative-construction" paradigm. It acknowledges that perfect alignment from a single instruction is often impossible. Instead, it treats the creation of a summary as a joint activity. The user contributes high-level structure, strategic direction, and grounded world knowledge. The LLM contributes linguistic fluency, rapid information processing, and the ability to generate and organize content at scale. The "common ground" is not a pre-existing semantic space that must be perfectly mapped, but rather a new, task-specific semantic space that is actively built through the iterative process of collaboration. The final summary is not just an output of the LLM; it is a shared artifact of meaning, an embodiment of this jointly constructed space, and is therefore inherently aligned with the user's intent because the user was a co-creator.


## Future Directions and Conclusion
The framework for interactive semantic compression represents a significant step toward achieving functional alignment in complex, subjective tasks. However, it also illuminates the boundaries of current text-based interaction and points toward future frontiers in the development of truly collaborative artificial intelligence. The path forward involves both deepening the grounding of AI systems through multimodality and refining the principles of human-AI interaction design to foster more intuitive and effective partnerships.


### The Path to Deeper Grounding: Multimodality and Embodiment
While the proposed framework can effectively align the semantic spaces of a human and an LLM within the domain of language, true shared understanding may ultimately require moving beyond text alone. The disembodiment gap remains a fundamental challenge; an LLM trained only on text can never fully grasp concepts that are deeply rooted in sensory experience or physical interaction.[20] The future of alignment may therefore lie in developing multimodal systems that can learn from and reason about a richer set of data types.


An AI system that can process not only the text of a document but also its accompanying images, diagrams, and data visualizations can build a more robust and grounded representation of its content.[51, 52] In the context of summarizing a conversation, a model that can analyze the emotional prosody in the speakers' voices or the non-verbal cues in a video could achieve a far deeper level of semantic alignment than one limited to a transcript.[53] This integration of multiple sensory modalities is a crucial step toward closing the gap between the LLM's statistical representation and the human's rich, multi-sensory experience of the world. Ultimately, the long-term vision may involve creating AI agents that are not just multimodal but truly embodied, capable of learning through interaction with the physical environment, which would provide the causal grounding that is currently missing from their semantic models.


### Recommendations for System Design and Research
The principles and challenges identified in this analysis lead to specific recommendations for practitioners and researchers working to build the next generation of human-AI systems.


**For System Designers**, the primary imperative is to shift focus from purely algorithmic performance to the quality of the human-AI interaction itself. This means prioritizing the design of interfaces that value and empower human agency.[31] Instead of pursuing "big red button" automation that seeks to remove the human, designers should focus on creating systems with a high *granularity of control*, allowing users to intervene, guide, and correct the AI at multiple points in a workflow.[31] Key design principles should include transparency, interruptibility, and auditable versioning, ensuring that the user remains in control and understands the process.[47, 48] The goal is to move from a mindset of model optimization to one of Human-AI Interaction (HAI) design, where the success of the system is measured by the effectiveness of the human-machine partnership.[31, 32]


**For Researchers**, there are several critical avenues for future work. First, there is a need for more sophisticated methods to detect, measure, and visualize the semantic gaps between humans and LLMs, particularly for abstract and subjective concepts where misalignment is most likely to occur.[22, 54] Second, further investigation is needed into the fundamental architectural differences between human cognitive systems and artificial neural networks to better understand why these gaps arise.[27] Finally, a grand challenge for the field is to explore how to build AI systems that can learn and incorporate human-like value structures more inherently, moving beyond simulating ethical text to possessing a more robust and grounded alignment from the ground up.[19, 20]


In conclusion, the objective of establishing a common ground between a human and an LLM is not a problem of perfect translation between two static semantic spaces. It is a dynamic challenge of collaborative creation. The path to effective and reliable human-AI systems lies in designing interactions that acknowledge the distinct strengths and weaknesses of both human and machine intelligence. By building frameworks that support a fluid, iterative partnership, we can enable users and LLMs to construct a shared understanding together, one interaction at a time. The final product of such a collaboration—be it a summary, a plan, or a creative work—will be more than just an AI-generated output; it will be a testament to a new form of hybrid intelligence.
