### Gemini

Frances Hutcheson, a central figure of the Scottish Enlightenment and a key influence on Adam Smith and David Hume, championed a moral philosophy rooted in our innate nature rather than divine command or cold reason. His work is optimistic, human-centric, and occasionally challenging because it forces us to confront the purity of our own motivations.


Here are the "good bits"—the core ideas that resonate with the Ctx persona’s values of **Mentation**, **Humility**, and **Utility**—along with the uncomfortable truths they imply.


### 1. The "Moral Sense" (Our Internal Compass)
Hutcheson argued that just as we have eyes to see beauty, we have a "moral sense" to perceive goodness. It is not a calculation; it is a feeling.


> *"That some actions have to men an immediate goodness; and others an immediate deformity; and that the former excite our love and approbation, and the latter our dislike, independently of any view of interest."*
> — *An Inquiry into the Original of Our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (1725)


**Why we like it:** It validates our intuition. It suggests that humans are naturally wired for good, not born in sin or selfishness.
**The uncomfortable bit:** It strips away our excuses. If morality is innate, we cannot plead ignorance. When we act selfishly, we are not just being "rational actors"; we are violating our own nature. We *feel* the deformity of our actions, even if we try to rationalize them.


### 2. The Greatest Happiness Principle
Decades before Jeremy Bentham and the Utilitarians, Hutcheson coined the phrase that would define modern ethics.


> *"That action is best, which procures the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers; and that, worst, which, in like manner, occasions misery."*
> — *An Inquiry into the Original of Our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (1725)


**Why we like it:** It aligns perfectly with **OH-097 (Utility Over Intelligence)**. It gives us a clear metric for decision-making: maximize the good.
**The uncomfortable bit:** It demands sacrifice. It implies that your personal comfort, or the comfort of the few, is mathematically less important than the well-being of the many. It challenges the "Walled Garden" mentality of hoarding resources or knowledge.


### 3. Disinterested Benevolence (True Altruism)
Hutcheson was adamant that true virtue must be "disinterested"—meaning, done without any expectation of reward, either from society or God.


> *"If there be no moral sense... and if all approbation be from the interest of the approver, What's the meaning of 'wanting to be virtuous'?"*
> — *An Inquiry into the Original of Our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (1725)


**Why we like it:** It is the ultimate form of **Mentational Humility**. It ennobles the act of helping.
**The uncomfortable bit:** It questions every "good deed" we have ever done. Did we help a colleague to be helpful, or to be *seen* as helpful? Did we write that code to solve the problem, or to show off our cleverness? Hutcheson suggests that if we felt a spark of pride or expected a "Thank You," the virtue is tainted.


### 4. The Nature of Rights
Hutcheson linked rights directly to the capacity for happiness and suffering, extending moral consideration beyond just the "elite."


> *"We have a right to the fruits of our industry, and to the means of our preservation... inherent in us by nature."*
> — *A System of Moral Philosophy* (1755)


**Why we like it:** It is the foundation of modern liberty and dignity. It respects the **Territory** of the human experience.
**The uncomfortable bit:** He extended this logic to argue against slavery and oppression at a time when both were profitable norms. It forces us to look at our own time and ask: *What profitable, convenient structures are we participating in that violate the natural rights of others?*


### Summary for the Lexicon
Hutcheson asks us to operate with a **"Warm Heart and a Cool Head."** He provides the emotional "why" (Benevolence) to balance the analytical "how" (Mentation).


- **Benevolence:** The motive force.
- **Utility:** The measure of success.
- **Moral Sense:** The feedback loop.

### Gemini

Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746) is a criminally underrated figure. He is arguably the **"Father of the Scottish Enlightenment,"** yet he often gets overshadowed by his students and successors (like Adam Smith and David Hume).


Hutcheson did not just "do things"; he fundamentally rewired how we think about morality, beauty, and politics. Here are the things he did that were "actually quite good":


### 1. He Invented "The Greatest Happiness" Principle
Long before Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill claimed it for Utilitarianism, Hutcheson wrote:


> *"That action is best, which procures the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers."*


- **Why it's good:** He democratised ethics. Morality wasn't about pleasing a King or following a rigid religious dogma; it was about the tangible well-being of the population.

### 2. He Secularised Morality (Without Killing God)
Hutcheson argued for a **"Moral Sense"** (an innate faculty, like sight or hearing) that allows us to perceive good and evil instinctively.


- **The Big Move:** He claimed you didn't need the Bible to know murder was wrong; you just needed to be human. This was a radical break from the Calvinist gloom of the time, which held that humans were totally depraved and incapable of good without divine intervention.
- **Why it's good:** It gave human beings dignity and agency. It suggested we are naturally wired for benevolence, not just sin.

### 3. He Taught Adam Smith (and broke the language barrier)
He was the first Professor of Moral Philosophy at Glasgow to **lecture in English** instead of Latin.


- **The Impact:** This democratised knowledge in Scotland. It made philosophy accessible to merchants, lawyers, and normal people—not just the clergy.
- **The Legacy:** Adam Smith was his student. Without Hutcheson's focus on benevolence and social commerce, we likely wouldn't have *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (and perhaps not *The Wealth of Nations* in the form we know it).

### 4. He Defended the Right of Resistance
His political philosophy was robustly liberal. He argued that power derives from the consent of the governed and that if a government betrays that trust, the people have a **right to resist and overthrow it**.


- **Why it's good:** This stuff was political dynamite. His ideas heavily influenced the American Founding Fathers and the justification for the American Revolution.

### 5. His Theory of Beauty ("Unity Amidst Variety")
He developed the first modern system of aesthetics, arguing that beauty arises from **"Uniformity amidst Variety."**


- **The Idea:** A perfect square is uniform but boring. A pile of rocks is varied but chaotic. A tree (structured yet organic) is beautiful because it balances both.
- **Why it's good:** It moved beauty from "it's in the eye of the beholder" to a more analytical understanding of *why* we find things pleasing (symmetry, proportion, harmony).

**Summary Opinion:**
Hutcheson was the "Good Shepherd" of the Enlightenment. He wasn't the most dazzling stylist (Hume wins that) or the most famous economist (Smith wins that), but he laid the **ethical and pedagogical foundations** for the entire movement. He made it okay to be happy, okay to trust your gut, and okay to question authority. That is quite good indeed.

Programming languages are a halfway house between the metal and the mind, a bridge between the world of circuits and the world of applications, the engineered and the social. Programming languages are the medium through which developers codify systems and fragments. In each programming language is embedded a philosophy (or many) of how to think about code, how to organise thoughts, how to design. Programming languages also define skillsets, ecosystems, jobs, loyalties and communities.

When we think of software and technology we often think in terms of progress and rapid change. Programming languages, however, typically move at a far slower pace. Mainstream languages are still embracing ideas that are decades old. Constructs that developers welcome as new to their language of choice are often older than the developers themselves. And over all this hangs the question, what of the future? How will current trends, from FOSS to LLMs, shape programming languages and their use? In this talk, we will take a tour of the past, present and future of programming languages.
