The area, which had begun its modern life with industrial ventures like brickworks and potteries exploiting the local clay beds, was rapidly gaining a reputation as a desirable residential enclave and bathing resort for the burgeoning middle classes of Edinburgh.[3, 4] Streets were being laid out on a grid plan between the High Street and the sea, with elegant Georgian houses rising on newly feued plots.[5, 6] It was into this ambitious and developing landscape that the church at 15 Marlborough Street was born, a physical manifestation of a community's faith and its confidence in the future of their town.

## Part I: The Wellington Street Chapel (1835 – c. 1870)
### The Genesis of a Congregation
The story of 15 Marlborough Street begins not with bricks and mortar, but with a community of faith. On 23rd August 1835, the "Wellington Street Independent Chapel" was officially opened for worship under the auspices of the Congregational Union of Scotland.[7] This was a significant moment for the non-conformist movement in the area, providing a permanent home for a congregation that, just over a year later in September 1836, would see its chapel formally elevated to the status of a church.[7] Its identity as a Congregationalist church placed it outside the established Church of Scotland, representing a distinct and independent strand of Portobello's religious life.


### A New Street in a Growing Town
The choice of location was as deliberate as the act of building. The church was erected on Wellington Street, a thoroughfare that had been laid out around 1815-1816 by the builder Lewis Wallace, a figure involved in constructing parts of Edinburgh's prestigious New Town.[5] This street was part of the planned, grid-like expansion of Portobello, a speculative but optimistic development aimed at attracting well-off families to the seaside.[5, 6] By placing their church here, the congregation was embedding itself in the new, respectable heart of the town.


The construction of a permanent stone chapel in this developing landscape was a profound statement of confidence. In an era when Portobello's future as a significant burgh was still being forged, and for a minority denomination like the Congregationalists, such an undertaking was a major financial and social commitment. It signified a move away from temporary meeting rooms to a lasting, visible presence. In choosing to build a substantial church on the new Wellington Street, the congregation was asserting its permanence and importance within the emerging social fabric of Portobello, mirroring the town's own ambitions. The building became an anchor for both its congregation and its street.


### Architectural Analysis of the Original Structure
The original 1835 building, as detailed in its later heritage listing, was a work of Georgian simplicity and functional grace. It was a single-storey, rectangular-plan church constructed of stugged sandstone, with more utilitarian rubble used for the side and rear elevations.[7] A particularly notable feature of this initial design was its semicircular, or bowed, rear wall, an elegant architectural flourish that distinguished it from a simple box-like structure. The interior would have reflected the restrained ethos of both its Georgian era and its Congregationalist purpose: a clear, well-lit space for worship, unadorned by the high ritualism of other denominations.


## Part II: Victorian Grandeur: Expansion and Embellishment (c. 1870 – 1914)
### The Romanesque Remodelling (c. 1870)
As Portobello entered its Victorian golden age, the modest Georgian chapel on Wellington Street underwent a dramatic transformation. Around 1870, the building was significantly remodelled, most notably its principal street-facing elevation, which was updated in a grand Romanesque style.[7] This architectural facelift was a deliberate move to align the church with the prevailing tastes of the era. The project involved adding the projecting rectangular porch that remains a key feature today, along with its column-moulded, stop-chamfered doorpiece. Above, in the pediment, a round louvered opening was framed by a striking round-arched colonnade supported by four short, stout columns. This renovation was more than a simple repair; it was an ambitious aesthetic statement, transforming the building from a humble chapel into an imposing and fashionable Victorian church.


### Enlargement and Community Facilities (c. 1883-1885)
The physical growth of the church mirrored the growth of its congregation. Around 1885, the main building was enlarged to create more space for worship.[7] This expansion was complemented two years earlier, in 1883, by the construction of a separate brick hall at the rear of the property, designated 15A Marlborough Street.[7] Built at a cost of £240, this hall was intended to serve as a Sunday School, indicating a thriving community with a large number of children and a need for dedicated educational and social facilities.[7] This hall became a community asset in its own right, known locally for a time as Loftus Hall, and was used for various purposes, including Parish Church Bible Classes in the 1960s.[8]


The church's physical evolution during this period was a direct reflection of Portobello's own ascent. The decades from the 1860s to the turn of the century marked the peak of Portobello's popularity as "Scotland's premier seaside resort," a time of significant prosperity, population growth, and immense civic pride.[3] A flourishing town supported a flourishing congregation, whose growing numbers and financial health enabled these ambitious building projects. The decision to invest heavily in a fashionable Romanesque facade and then to physically expand the church and add a dedicated hall was not made in a vacuum. It was an act of civic participation. As Portobello invested in its own grand infrastructure, such as the new Promenade built in the 1860s, its key institutions, like the Congregational Church, did likewise.[11] The building's architecture, therefore, serves as a direct physical barometer of the wider socio-economic health and ambition of Portobello during its Victorian zenith.


### The Victorian Interior
The Historic Environment Scotland listing, compiled before the modern conversion, provides a valuable snapshot of the church's interior at the height of its life. The space was arranged with two aisles flanking wooden pews, each thoughtfully fitted with bracketed brass umbrella stands—a practical nod to the Scottish climate.[7] At the north-west end stood a raised platform, or presbytery, which was the focal point of the service. ## Part III: A Century of Worship and Community (1914 – c. 2010)
### A Community in Wartime
The church on Wellington Street was more than a place for Sunday service; it was the heart of a community that shared in times of celebration and sorrow. This is poignantly evidenced by the presence of a war memorial noted in the building's heritage description. The carved wood and gilt memorial commemorated the men from the congregation who went to fight in the First World War.[7] This object transformed the church into a site of public remembrance, a solemn record of the profound impact of global conflict on the families of Portobello.


### The Mid-Century: A Change of Name
A significant, if administrative, change occurred in the 1960s when Wellington Street was officially renamed Marlborough Street. This was done to resolve the issue of there being two streets with the same name within the expanded boundaries of the City of Edinburgh.[8] This seemingly minor event is a key historical marker. Portobello had been an independent burgh from 1833 until 1896, when it voted to amalgamate with its larger neighbour.[3] The street renaming, decades later, symbolized the final stage of this integration, cementing Portobello's identity as a suburb of the capital.


### The United Reformed Church
The building's final chapter as a place of worship saw it become the Portobello United Reformed Church.[7] This change reflected a major development in the history of British Protestantism. In 1972, the Presbyterian Church of England and the Congregational Church in England and Wales merged to form the United Reformed Church (URC).[9] The congregation at 15 Marlborough Street thus became part of this new, unified national body.


### Decline and Deconsecration
Like many churches across the country, the Portobello URC faced challenges in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. While the specific date of its closure is not documented in the available material, the timeline of events points to its deconsecration around 2010. ## Part V: Architectural Significance and Heritage Protection
### Understanding the Category B Listing
The former church at 15 Marlborough Street is formally protected as a building of special architectural and historic interest. It is included on the statutory list maintained by Historic Environment Scotland under the designation "15 MARLBOROUGH STREET, PORTOBELLO UNITED REFORMED CHURCH (FORMER CONGREGATIONAL CHURCH) (LB27308)".[7] It is designated as a Category B listed building.


This classification is significant. In Scotland, Category B is used for buildings of regional or more than local importance, or major examples of a particular period, style, or building type.[12, 13] This status recognizes the building's quality, its Romanesque facade, and its importance to the historical townscape of Portobello. This legal protection would have been the primary instrument governing the entire conversion process, ensuring that any alterations were sensitive to the building's historic character.


### An Architectural Glossary
The official listing description from Historic Environment Scotland uses several technical architectural terms that are key to understanding the building's design and construction.[7]


- **Stugged sandstone:** A traditional Scottish masonry finish where the surface of the stone is dressed with a specific type of chisel (a "stugging" iron), creating a pitted, textured appearance.
- **Mutuled cornice:** In classical architecture, a mutule is a projecting block in the cornice, typically above a triglyph in a Doric frieze. Its use here is a classical detail applied to the top of the building's walls.
- **Coped skews:** The sloping stones that form the edge of a gable wall are known as skews. "Coped" means they are topped with a coping stone to protect the joint from weather.
- **Stop-chamfered arrises:** An arris is a sharp edge or corner where two surfaces meet. A chamfer is a symmetrical sloped edge, and a "stop-chamfer" is one that ends before the end of the edge, often with a decorative curve or angle. This is a common decorative treatment in Victorian stonework.
- **Reredos:** An ornamental screen or decorated wall surface behind an altar. The church had a horizontal board reredos before its conversion.

### Contribution to the Portobello Conservation Area
Beyond its individual listing, the building is a key component of the Portobello Conservation Area.[6] Conservation areas are designated to protect and enhance places with special architectural or historic character. The building at 15 Marlborough Street contributes significantly to this character. Its unique Romanesque facade provides a point of visual and architectural interest on a street characterized by a mix of Georgian villas and Victorian tenements.[6, 11] As a former public and religious building, it adds a layer of social history to the predominantly residential streetscape, serving as a physical reminder of the institutions that shaped community life in 19th-century Portobello.
