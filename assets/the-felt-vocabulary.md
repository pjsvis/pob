### Beyond the four zones: the language of interoception

The four-zone mapping (good/ache/twinge/pain) covers the protective telemetry — the CNS's risk classification. But the body produces felt signals that are not protective, and the interoceptive vocabulary should name those too. The point is not to build a complete taxonomy of sensation; the point is to give the user words for the felt reports the book's framework predicts but the clinical vocabulary doesn't name.

**Activating** — the feeling that something is going on.

This is a distinct interoceptive signal, not part of the Green/Yellow/Orange/Red protective classification. It is not pain, not ache, not a twinge, and not the absence of signal (good). It is a positive report: something is happening in the tissue, and it feels a bit weird and slightly uncomfortable, but certainly not bad.

The qualities:
- **Duration:** lasts for a while — minutes to hours, not transient like a twinge, not persistent like pain. It has a beginning and an end, but it lingers.
- **Character:** weird, slightly uncomfortable, but not threatening. The CNS is not flagging it as danger (it's not Orange or Red). It's more like the CNS is flagging it as *news* — something is changing, and the system is paying attention, but it hasn't classified the change as threat.
- **Physical correlate:** likely local fascial realignment over an area. This is the felt signature of the eigenstructure being re-computed — the tissue is rearranging its fiber directions (eigenvectors) and resting tensions (eigenvalues), and the nervous system is reading the rearrangement. The "activating" feeling is the felt report of a spectral update in progress. It is the floor's re-computation made perceptible — not the breath-by-breath iteration (which is too small to feel), but the accumulated effect of many iterations, reaching the threshold of interoceptive awareness.
- **Appetite correlation:** promotes an appetite for carbs and a thirst. This is consistent with tissue remodeling — collagen synthesis is metabolically expensive (requires amino acids, requires energy), and the body is signalling that it needs fuel for the re-computation. The carb craving is the metabolic demand of fascial realignment; the thirst is the fluid demand (collagen synthesis requires water, and the fascial ground substance is a hydrophilic gel that needs hydration to reorganise). This is a prediction: if the "activating" feeling is the felt signature of fascial realignment, then it should correlate with metabolic demand (carb appetite, thirst) in a way that a simple ache or twinge does not. Falsifiable.

The spectral reading: "activating" is the felt report of the eigenstructure being re-computed. The eigenvectors are shifting (fiber realignment), the eigenvalues are updating (tension redistribution), and the nervous system is reading the update. It is not protective telemetry (Green/Yellow/Orange/Red) — it is *developmental telemetry*. The body is not saying "danger"; it is saying "under construction." The weirdness is the unfamiliarity of the new eigenstructure; the slight discomfort is the tissue being rearranged; the "not bad" is the CNS's classification that the re-computation is safe to proceed.

This maps onto the floor practice: after a session on the floor, the user may feel "activating" in the areas that were re-computed. The stiffness has gone (the old eigenstructure has been released), but something else is there — a weird, slightly uncomfortable, not-bad feeling that lasts for a while and makes them want to eat and drink. That's the fascia rebuilding. The book's vocabulary lets the user name it: "I feel activating" — and the naming tells them what to do (eat, drink, rest, let it finish) rather than what to fear (nothing — it's not pain, it's construction).

The staved-finger essay's steepling produces this: the fingers share a reference plane, and the user feels the connection. The vocabulary grows as the framework predicts more signals worth naming.

## Why the words carry more information than the numbers

The clinical 1-10 pain scale asks "how much?" — a magnitude with no direction. The word-mapping asks "what kind?" — a quality that implies a trajectory. The words carry temporal and qualitative information the scale cannot:

- **"Ache" is not a 3/10.** It's a *state* — the body saying "I'm here, I'm watching, be careful." It has a location (the frontier), a direction (the eigenvector being tested), and a duration (it stays while the direction is loaded and fades when it's released). A 3/10 has none of these.
- **"Twinge" is not a 5/10.** It's an *event* — a threshold brush that comes and goes. It has a timestamp (when it happened), a trigger (what movement provoked it), and a resolution (it passed). A 5/10 has none of these.
- **"Pain" is not a 7/10.** It's a *persistence* — the alarm that doesn't reset. It has a duration (it stays), a character (it doesn't fade when the movement stops), and a governance implication (the guard is not standing down). A 7/10 has none of these.
- **"Good" is not a 0/10.** It's the *absence of report* — the body has nothing to say because the eigenvector is within the safe envelope. A 0/10 is the bottom of a scale; "good" is the absence of the scale.

The words are categorical, not ordinal. "Ache" is not between "good" and "twinge" on a number line. It's a different *kind* of signal — a state, not a magnitude. The categories carry trajectory (is it arriving or departing?), duration (is it transient or persistent?), and governance (is the guard up or down?) — all information the 1-10 scale compresses into a single digit and loses.

## The literature on why the 1-10 scale fails

The 1-10 numeric rating scale (NRS) and the visual analogue scale (VAS) are known to suffer from systematic biases that make their output less informative than it appears:

**Number bias produces a normal distribution.** When people are asked to rate pain on a 1-10 scale, the results cluster around the middle (4-6) regardless of the actual pain severity. This is not because most pain is moderate; it's because people have an unconscious bias toward the centre of any bounded scale. The extremes feel like strong claims — "10" feels like "the worst pain imaginable," which most people won't claim even in severe pain, and "1" feels like "no pain at all," which most people won't claim if they're being asked. The result is a normal distribution centred on 5-6 that tells you more about the patient's number preferences than about their pain. The scale measures the bias, not the signal.

**The numbers change with mood and individual propensity for sensation.** The same physical state produces different numbers depending on the patient's affective state, their cultural background, their prior pain experiences, and their individual sensitivity to sensation. A 4 for one person is a 7 for another. The scale has no calibration — it pretends to be a measurement but is actually a comparison to an internal reference that varies across individuals and within individuals across time. The words are more robust because they report a *quality* (ache vs. twinge vs. pain) that is less sensitive to the patient's mood and more tied to the physical character of the signal.

**"When I do this I feel a ..." is more useful than a biased number.** The clinical utility of pain reporting is not the magnitude but the *relationship* — what movement provokes what sensation, and what kind of sensation it is. "When I reach overhead, I feel a twinge in the shoulder" tells the practitioner and the patient: the movement is the trigger, the shoulder is the location, the twinge is a transient threshold brush (not persistent pain), and the direction (overhead reach) is the eigenvector being tested. "When I reach overhead, it's a 4" tells you none of that. The word-mapping preserves the relationship and the quality; the number discards both.

## How the mapping connects to the spectral hinge

The word-mapping is the felt-vocabulary layer for the Green/Yellow/Orange/Red telemetry classification. The spectral language names the structure; the colours name the classification; the words name the felt report. Same thing three times, not dogmatically.

- **Green = good** is the eigenvector direction where the eigenvalue is well within the safe envelope. The body can load it. Nothing to report. The absence of signal is the signal.
- **Yellow = ache** is the eigenvector direction where the eigenvalue is approaching the boundary. The body feels it — a dull, present signal that says "this direction is being tested." The ache is the gradient signal: the felt sense of the eigenvalue rising. It's not pain; it's information. The approach-and-retreat protocol lives here: the ache is the gradient the nervous system is reading, and the reader's vocabulary for it is "I feel an ache when I do this."
- **Orange = twinge** is the eigenvector direction where the eigenvalue brushed the threshold. The GTO fires, the muscle clamps, the movement stops — and then the twinge passes. It's a transient threshold-crossing: the eigenvalue went to the line, the system cut, and the eigenvalue dropped back. The twinge is the GTO's felt signature — the felt event of a threshold check firing and resetting.
- **Red = pain** is the eigenvector direction where the eigenvalue is at or beyond the threshold and the system is clamped. The guard doesn't stand down. The pain is the persistent Red — the eigenvalue that won't drop, the eigenvector that stays clamped. This is where the approach-and-retreat protocol cannot go until the surrounding Yellow work has lowered the eigenvalues enough to let the Red direction be approached safely.

The mapping gives the spectral language a felt vocabulary. The eigenvalue is "how much" — but the reader doesn't feel eigenvalues. The reader feels good, ache, twinge, pain. The mapping connects the math to the feeling without a translation step: the ache *is* the felt eigenvalue at the Yellow boundary. The twinge *is* the felt GTO threshold brush. The pain *is* the felt clamped eigenvector. Same thing, said in the body's own words.

## The "when I do this I feel a ..." pattern

The word-mapping's practical power is in the sentence pattern it enables: **"When I do this, I feel a [word]."**

This pattern carries four pieces of information that a number cannot:

1. **The trigger** ("when I do this") — the movement that provoked the signal. This is the eigenvector direction being loaded.
2. **The quality** ("I feel a [word]") — the kind of signal: good, ache, twinge, pain. This is the zone classification.
3. **The location** (implied) — where in the body the signal lives. This is the eigenvector's physical location.
4. **The temporal signature** (implied by the word) — whether it's continuous (ache), transient (twinge), or persistent (pain). This is the eigenvalue's trajectory.

A number carries none of these. "When I do this, it's a 4" is a magnitude with no trigger, no quality, no location, and no trajectory. "When I reach overhead, I feel a twinge in the shoulder" is a complete diagnostic: the trigger is the overhead reach, the quality is a threshold brush, the location is the shoulder, and the temporal signature is transient. The practitioner (and the patient) know what to do with that: the twinge is Orange, it's a warning shot, don't push through it, but it's not Red — it passed. The approach-and-retreat can continue, more carefully.

This is why the word-mapping is not just a cosmetic improvement. It changes what the patient can report and what the practitioner can do with the report. The 1-10 scale produces a number; the word-mapping produces a diagnostic. The book's job is to give the reader the vocabulary and the pattern, and let them discover that "when I do this I feel a twinge" is more useful than any number they've ever been asked to produce.

## Epistemic register

Same as the spectral hinge: model, not measurement. The word-mapping is a proposed replacement for the 1-10 scale, grounded in the known biases of numeric rating and the known advantages of categorical, qualitative reporting. It is falsifiable: if the words turn out to carry less diagnostic information than the numbers (e.g., if clinical outcomes are better with numeric reporting than with word-based reporting), the mapping weakens. The prediction is the opposite: the words carry more information because they preserve quality, trajectory, and trigger-relationship that the numbers compress away.

## The empowerment: interoception as cognition, not complaint

The 1-10 scale disempowers the user. It takes a rich, multi-dimensional felt experience and compresses it into a digit the user can't do anything with. "It's a 4" is a complaint — it reports suffering and hands it to someone else (the doctor, the practitioner) to interpret. The user is a sensor producing a number; the meaning lives elsewhere. The user doesn't think about their pain; they report it and wait.

The interoceptive vocabulary does the opposite. "When I reach overhead, I feel a twinge in the shoulder" is not a complaint — it's a diagnostic. The user has named the trigger (overhead reach), the quality (twinge — transient, a threshold brush), and the location (shoulder). They have classified their own experience using a vocabulary that carries trajectory and governance information: a twinge is Orange, it's a warning shot, it passed, the approach-and-retreat can continue more carefully. The user is not reporting suffering to an authority; they are reading their own telemetry and making a decision about what to do next.

The empowerment is structural, not just rhetorical. The word-mapping gives the user a cognitive tool — a classification scheme that turns a felt experience into actionable information. "Ache" is not less information than "4 out of 10"; it's more information, and it's information the user can use. The ache says "you're at the frontier, this is the direction being tested, you can explore here." The 4 says nothing. The user who has the vocabulary can think about their interoception as data — data with structure, direction, and implications — rather than as suffering to be endured or reported.
