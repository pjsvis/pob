# Walking songs and the Poynting flux — findings (assess stage)

> **Status:** assess stage of the curation loop. Research notes, not a draft. The gate to draft is the cultural sourcing (see below). Filed 2026-06-30.

## The thesis being explored

Rhythmic vocalisation during locomotion (singing while walking) modulates the energy routing of gait through the thoracoabdominal continuum. The mechanical Poynting vector **P** = −**σ**·**v** (stress contracted with velocity) is the diagnostic for where gait energy flows; vocalisation imposes an intra-abdominal pressure prestress that shifts the coupling phase and routes the flux more efficiently. Long-distance walking cultures that sing while walking — West African, East African, Aboriginal Australian — embody the locomotor-respiratory coupling the model describes, whether or not it evolved for that reason.

## The biomechanical chain (each link is a measured phenomenon)

1. **Locomotor-respiratory coupling (LRC) is real and mechanically grounded.** Bramble & Carrier 1983 (*Science*) established it; humans phase-lock breathing to stride (typically 2:1). Daley, Bramble & Carrier 2013 (*PLoS ONE*) give the continuum mechanism: the "visceral piston" — step-driven flows through the thoracoabdominal cavity, state-dependent compliance, and runners *prefer* coupling phases that minimise antagonistic loading of respiratory muscles. Ventilatory transitions in preferred phases occur 2× faster than in avoided phases. This is an energy-routing story already, just not named as such.
2. **Phonation modulates the coupling.** Potočnik et al. 2023 (*IJERPH*): phonated exhalation during cycling shifts the dominant locomotor-respiratory coupling ratio and manipulates expiratory flow. Critically, **entrainment, not the phonation resistance itself, is what improves ventilatory efficiency** (eqO2/eqCO2 lower at the dominant coupling). Singing-while-moving is a real modulatory lever.
3. **Rhythm stabilises the coupling and reduces energy cost.** Hoffmann, Torregrosa & Bardy 2012 (*PLoS ONE*): an auditory rhythm stabilises LRC and reduces O2 consumption ~4%. The sine circle map / Farey tree model predicts the shift toward more stable frequency ratios. Rhythm as an energy-efficiency tool — this is the "singing helps walking" evidence base.
4. **Singing is mechanically controlled intra-abdominal pressure prestress.** Salomoni, van den Hoorn & Hodges 2016 (*PLoS ONE*): classical singers' respiratory kinematics — pre-phonatory inward abdominal wall movement, elevated IAP, independent rib-cage/abdomen control. Singing, mechanically, is controlled prestress of the abdominal cavity — the same quantity the continuum model treats as tunable.

**The chain:** walking = cyclic loading + torsion (the book's Poynting-effect regime in the spine); the visceral piston is a step-driven energy flux through the cavity; singing imposes an IAP prestress; phasing that prestress to the gait cycle (LRC) routes the flux more efficiently. Every link measured. The synthesis — naming the flux via the Poynting vector and the prestress via vocalisation — is the theoretical contribution.

## On "Poynting" — two objects, both legit, must be distinguished

- The **Poynting effect** (torsion↔axial coupling) is the *coupling mechanism* in the spine. Already in the book (Chapter 0: "the phenomenon that runs the spine").
- The **Poynting vector** **P** = −**σ**·**v** is the *energy-flux density* through a surface — the diagnostic for where gait energy goes and whether singing routes it differently.

Both are "Poynting," both are correct, but the piece must distinguish them or a reader of the book hears a contradiction. The single-voice gate catches this seam.

## The novelty and its epistemic status

LRC is normally described neurologically (central pattern generators) or via simple visceral-piston mechanics. Reframing it as **energy-flux optimisation in a prestressed continuum, with vocalisation as prestress control** is a genuinely different lens on an established phenomenon. It is a *theoretical frame*: nobody has computed −**σ**·**v** in vivo for a walking singer, and doing so would be formidable. Same epistemic status as the book's other continuum models — models, not measurements. Do not present as established.

## The cultural sourcing (the gate)

### What is grounded

- **Mali / Wassoulou:** Na Hawa Doumbia (the user's reference singer) is a well-known Malian Wassoulou singer; Wassoulou is rooted in hunters' traditions and has strong rhythmic, movement-coupled character. Whether her specific repertoire contains gait-phased "walking songs" is **unverified** — needs a sourcing pass on her discography/lyrics.
- **West African rhythm is profoundly movement-coupled:** Rainer Polak's work on Malian jembe non-isochronous metre (Oxford Handbook of Time in Music, 2022) shows the metre *is* the movement — non-isochronous subdivisions governed by ratios that are perceptual-motor prototypes, not notated abstractions. Julien André's thesis on Malinke polyrhythm frames it as "ethnomathematical." The rhythm-locomotion link is structurally present.
- **Labour songs:** girls' millet-pounding songs (Soninke/Mande) are vocal rhythm locked to a repetitive physical task — the closest structural analogue to gait-phased song within the documented tradition.

### What is NOT grounded (do not assert)

- A specific "Malian walking song phased to the stride" — I did not find clean documentation. Soliyo is a cappella and short; migration songs are exhortation/praise, not gait-phased.
- The cultural-evolutionary claim that these songs exist *because* singing optimises gait energy. The literature won't support it.

### The broader geographic question (user's expansion)

The user notes other West and Southern African songs often have "that walking rhythm," and asks about Eastern Africa, Australia, and other places where long-distance walking still occurs.

- **East Africa:** long-distance pastoralist walking (Maasai, Samburu) with walking songs is a real, documented tradition. Potentially grounded, needs its own sourcing pass.
- **Aboriginal Australian songlines:** the strongest possible cultural instance of vocalisation coupled to long-distance walking — navigation-by-song across vast distances on foot. Substantial anthropology (Strehlow, Myers, Margot Neale; Chatwin's *The Songlines* is famous but partially fictionalised — treat as literary, not source). BUT: songlines function is **mnemonic/navigational, not biomechanical.** They encode geography. The causal claim "singing optimises gait energy" is NOT what songlines are for. They are a perfect *instance* of rhythmic vocalisation during long-distance walking, but their cultural function is irreducible to the mechanism.

### The honest framing this implies

The broader the geographic sweep, the more the piece must resist the "look at all these exotic people who walk and sing" framing. The correct line: **the mechanism is universal and sub-personal (any rhythmic vocalisation during locomotion exhibits LRC-mediated energy routing); the cultural reasons for walking-songs are diverse and irreducible to the mechanism (navigation, pace-setting, social cohesion, prayer, boredom).** The biomechanical benefit is a *side effect* or a *sustainability condition*, not the *cause*. That is non-appropriative, non-teleological, and actually richer — it lets the mechanism be load-bearing and the cultures be themselves, rather than illustrations of the mechanism.

The geographic breadth multiplies sourcing work: each tradition cited needs its own pass. Argument for either (a) one or two well-sourced traditions, or (b) a lightly-touched illustrative sweep with the mechanism doing the load-bearing work. Leaning (b) given the user's instinct, but the gate is: every named tradition must be sourced, or cut.
