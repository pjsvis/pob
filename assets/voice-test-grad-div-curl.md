## Voice-test block 1: gradient (the nose test)

:::grad
The gradient of the strain energy landscape points in the direction of steepest increase — the direction the body is pulled most urgently. Its magnitude is how steep that pull is. When you close your eyes and move your hand toward your nose, your nervous system is following the gradient of the error field: the direction in which the distance to the target decreases fastest. The hand moves along the steepest descent. The 1cm accuracy is the gradient's resolution — the smallest step the nervous system can distinguish along the line of steepest pull. The fast approach is coarse gradient following: the direction is clear, the pull is shallow, the steps are big. The slow approach near the face is fine gradient following: the pull is steep, the cost of a misstep is high, and the nervous system takes small steps, reading the gradient at high resolution before committing. The Green/Yellow/Red classification is a gradient test. Shallow gradient, far from the target — Green, move fast. Steep gradient, near the target — Yellow, slow down. Gradient at the threshold — Red, stop. The approach-and-retreat is the nervous system sampling the gradient, retreating when the estimate is noisy, approaching when it is clean. It is an iterative descent along the steepest line, with the step size set by the cost of an error.
:::

## Voice-test block 2: divergence (the clavicle fuse)

:::div
The divergence of a force field measures how fast things are accumulating or dispersing at a point. Positive divergence is a source — force spreading outward, dispersing through a wide area. Negative divergence is a sink — force converging inward, accumulating at a point. When a cat lands, the divergence of the stress field in its shoulder is uniformly positive: the force enters the paw and spreads outward through the fascial sling, dispersing across a broad topological area. There is no sink. The cat lands in silence. When a human takes a tackle, the divergence is not uniform. The clavicle is a sink — a point of sharply negative divergence where the force converges. The surrounding fascia is a source — positive divergence, force dispersing. The clavicle snaps because it is the point of maximum convergence: the most negative divergence in the field, the place where force is arriving faster than the bone can disperse it. The snap is not a structural failure. It is a calibrated release at the point of steepest convergence, a fuse firing exactly where the divergence is most negative, to keep the line above it intact. The same governor that disperses the cat's impact through a source runs the human's impact into a sink. The difference is not in the math. The difference is in the architecture.
:::

## Voice-test block 3: curl (the floor and the ribs)

:::curl
The curl of a displacement field describes the local rotation — how fast things are turning, and around what axis. When you lie on a mattress and breathe, your ribs expand outward. The curl of the rib displacement is low: the motion is mostly radial, spreading laterally, with very little rotation. The mattress absorbs the expansion, and the breath does no structural work. When you lie on a rigid floor and breathe, the ribs cannot expand outward — the boundary is rigid. The curl changes. The rotation that would have gone into lateral expansion goes into articulating the costovertebral joints instead: each rib rotates around its own axis at the junction with the spine. The curl of the rib displacement is now high — the motion is rotational, not radial. Each breath is a small rotation at each rib, applied against a known boundary. Twenty thousand breaths are twenty thousand curl-changes, each one a small rotation the nervous system reads. The floor does not stretch the ribs. It changes the curl of the displacement field, and the breath iterates the new curl pattern until the eigenstructure re-solves.
:::

## Assessment against the benchmark

The three blocks are written in the book's register: measured, physical, grounded in a body. Each block names the operator (gradient, divergence, curl), states what it measures, and then applies it to a specific physical situation the reader can feel — the hand approaching the nose, the cat landing, the ribs on the floor. The sentences are short. The claims are made and then explained in felt terms. No hedging, no lecturing, no manic enthusiasm.

The gradient block connects to the nose test. The divergence block connects to the clavicle fuse (Chapter 20). The curl block connects to the floor assets. Each block is self-contained — a reader who doesn't know vector calculus can still get the gist from the prose — but specific to the physics of the situation, not generic boilerplate.

The seam test: read the benchmark excerpt, then the gradient block, aloud. If you can hear the join, the block needs re-voicing. My read: the blocks hold the register. They are not the staved-finger essay — they are denser, more technical — but they are in the same voice. The world-weary curiosity is there. The short sentences are there. The "name the thing and explain it in terms the reader can feel" structure is there.

The open question is whether the blocks are too long. The staved-finger essay uses longer paragraphs; the operator blocks are single-paragraph density. The layout pass (sidenotes, margin marks, visual treatment) may help the reader parse them. But the voice test is: does the register hold? My judgment is yes, with the caveat that the final voice pass should trim any sentence that feels like a textbook definition rather than a felt explanation.

## The discipline, restated

Same thing three times in different ways — equation, spectral language, operator language — but not dogmatically. Where the equation is self-evident, one rendering is enough. Where the connection between equation and felt experience is non-obvious, the operator language earns its place. The blocks above are the operator rendering; the spectral hinge asset has the spectral rendering; the equations are in the chapter briefs. The reader gets the grip that works for them. The book does not insist on all three every time.
