## The eigenvector survey

The CNS does not re-compute the entire eigenstructure at once. That would be the "jump in with both feet flying" approach, and the approach-and-retreat protocol exists precisely because the body doesn't do that. Instead, the CNS surveys the eigenstructure, identifies the most significant anomalies (the eigenvectors whose eigenvalues are farthest from the safe envelope), and prioritises those for attention. The rest are queued.

This is a priority queue, not a full solve. The CNS has limited attention — limited computational bandwidth, limited metabolic resources, limited capacity for simultaneous re-computation. It cannot re-compute every eigenvector at once. So it surveys, ranks by anomaly severity, and deals with the worst first. "Deals with" does not necessarily mean "fixes" — it could mean guarding (clamp the eigenvector), monitoring (flag it for the nightly re-computation), or no action (the anomaly is within tolerance, leave it). The priority is attention, not intervention.

### The spectral reading of the priority queue

- **Survey:** the CNS reads the eigenstructure — the eigenvectors (fiber directions) and their eigenvalues (prestress magnitudes). This is the proprioceptive read, the Class 2 measurement (see `the-inspection-boundary.md`). The survey produces a ranked list: which eigenvectors are most anomalous (eigenvalue farthest from the safe envelope).

- **Prioritise:** the most anomalous eigenvectors get attention first. This is the CNS's resource allocation — it has limited bandwidth, and it spends it on the biggest deviations. The "biggest" is the eigenvalue with the largest excursion from the safe zone — the steepest gradient, the loudest signal. This is the dominant-eigenvalue principle: when a process repeats, the biggest eigenvalue takes over. Here, the biggest anomaly takes the attention.

- **Act or defer:** for each prioritised eigenvector, the CNS decides: guard it (clamp the eigenvector, Red/Yellow), monitor it (flag for the nightly re-computation), or no action (within tolerance, leave it). The decision is the Green/Yellow/Orange/Red classification applied to the survey results.

- **Queue the rest:** the non-prioritised eigenvectors are not forgotten — they are queued. The CNS knows they are there (they are in the eigenstructure), but they are below the attention threshold. They will be surveyed again, and if they become more anomalous (the eigenvalue drifts further from safe), they will be promoted up the queue.

## How this explains the forgetting of old aches

Old aches disappear because the CNS stops surveying them, not because they are fixed. When a new, more anomalous eigenvector appears (a new injury, a new postural distortion, a new stress pattern), it jumps to the top of the priority queue. The CNS redirects its limited attention to the new anomaly. The old anomaly — the old ache — drops below the attention threshold. The CNS does not forget it because the tissue is healed; it forgets it because the attention budget is now spent on the louder signal.

The old ache is still in the eigenstructure. The eigenvector is still there, the eigenvalue is still off, the tissue is still distorted. But the CNS is no longer surveying it, because the survey is prioritised by anomaly severity, and the new anomaly is louder. The old ache drops out of awareness not because it is gone but because it has been demoted in the priority queue.

New aches replace old aches because attention is finite. The CNS has a bounded attention budget. When a new eigenvector becomes the most anomalous, the budget shifts to it. The old eigenvector's ache fades from awareness — not because the eigenvalue has returned to safe, but because the CNS is no longer reading it. The felt report (the ache) disappears because the telemetry channel is now pointed at the new anomaly, not because the old anomaly has been resolved.

This is why you can have a chronic old restriction that you have "forgotten about" — it is not gone, it is just not at the top of the queue. When the new, louder anomaly is resolved (the new ache is dealt with, the eigenvalue returns to safe), the CNS's attention budget frees up, and the survey goes back to the next item in the queue — which may be the old, forgotten restriction, now re-promoted to the top. The old ache "comes back" — not because it returned, but because the CNS is surveying it again.

## The somatic amnesia connection

The book's amnesia material (Chapter 17/18, "The Amnesia Paradox" / "The Two Faces of Amnesia") describes the chronic case: a region where the frontier stopped updating long ago, the map was never refreshed, and the CNS treats the boundary as a hard wall. The spectral priority queue adds the mechanism:

**Somatic amnesia is the permanent demotion of an eigenvector from the survey.** The old restriction's eigenvector has been below the attention threshold for so long that the CNS no longer includes it in the survey. It is not just demoted — it is dropped from the queue entirely. The CNS has stopped reading it, stopped guarding it, stopped flagging it for re-computation. The ache is gone because the telemetry channel is closed, not because the eigenvalue is safe.

This is why the old restriction does not show up as an ache anymore — the CNS has classified it as "background" and stopped surveying it. The tissue is still distorted (the eigenvector is still off, the eigenvalue is still anomalous), but the felt report is silent because the survey no longer includes it. The amnesia is the survey's silence, not the tissue's recovery.

**The floor re-computation re-includes the demoted eigenvectors.** When the body lies on the floor and the global threat level drops (the system goes to "quiet"), the CNS's attention budget frees up. The survey expands — it starts including eigenvectors that were previously below the attention threshold. The old, forgotten restriction is re-promoted: its eigenvector is surveyed again, its eigenvalue is read, and the felt report returns — the old ache "comes back." This is the "migration of stiffness" from the floor assets: the old restriction re-appears as the newer, louder restrictions are resolved and the survey reaches deeper into the queue.

The floor does not create new aches; it re-surveys old ones. The stiffness migrates because the priority queue is being drained — the CNS resolves the loudest anomaly, frees up attention, surveys the next one, and so on down the queue. The old aches that "come back" on the floor are the eigenvectors that were demoted when newer, louder anomalies took the budget. The floor gives the CNS the bandwidth to re-survey them, by removing the gravitational noise (the pencil test) and dropping the global threat level (the floor as known boundary).

## The Occam's Razor: energy budget analysis

This survey mechanism gives the book an additional analytical tool — an Occam's Razor for evaluating claims about the body's behaviour. The principle:

**The CNS and the human body are efficiency seekers. They do not spend energy without a return. If a behaviour has an energy cost, there is an energy reason.**

The maintenance burden equation (see `maintenance-burden-equation.md`) provides the frame: $M_B = E_{\text{metabolic}} + E_{\text{computational}}$. Every structural state has a maintenance cost, and the body minimises it. This is the Razor: when evaluating any claim about what the body is doing or why, ask **what is the energy budget, and is the behaviour the efficient option?**

Applications:

- **Why does the CNS forget old aches?** Not because the tissue is healed (it isn't). Because surveying every eigenvector simultaneously is computationally expensive ($E_{\text{computational}}$), and the CNS has a bounded budget. Demoting low-anomaly eigenvectors from the survey saves computation. The forgetting is the efficient option — it costs less to ignore the old ache than to keep monitoring it. The Razor says: the body is not being negligent; it is being efficient. The old ache is still there, but spending attention on it has a lower return than spending attention on the newer, louder anomaly.

- **Why does the body use muscle guarding instead of fixing the fascia?** Because fixing the fascia (re-computing the eigenstructure) requires a known boundary (the floor), time (20,000 breaths), and metabolic investment (collagen synthesis, the "activating" feeling). Muscle guarding is the cheap, immediate solution — it costs $E_{\text{metabolic}}$ but saves the re-computation cost. The Razor says: the body is not being stupid; it is being efficient. Guarding is the low-investment option; re-computation is the high-investment option. The body uses the cheap one until the expensive one becomes affordable (when the floor provides the boundary and the breath provides the time).

- **Why does the body let the clavicle snap?** Because the alternative (routing the full impact force through the spine) has a higher cost (spinal cord injury, death). The snap is the efficient option — a localised structural failure that costs a broken bone but saves the line above. The Razor says: the body is not failing; it is optimising. The fuse fires because the fuse is cheaper than the alternative. (This is Chapter 20's existing argument, now backed by the energy-budget Razor.)

- **Why does the body slow down near the face?** Because the cost of an error (a bash in the nose) is higher than the cost of slowing down (a few extra seconds). The slow approach is the efficient option — it costs time but saves the face. The Razor says: the nervous system is not being cautious; it is pricing the cost asymmetry. (This is the nose test's existing argument, now backed by the energy-budget Razor.)

- **Why does the floor work and the mattress doesn't?** Because the floor provides a known boundary (zero $E_{\text{computational}}$ for boundary uncertainty) and reflects the perturbation (the eigenstructure is readable). The mattress provides an unknown boundary (high $E_{\text{computational}}$ for boundary uncertainty) and absorbs the perturbation (the eigenstructure is not readable). The Razor says: the floor is the efficient re-computation environment; the mattress is the expensive one. The body re-computes on the floor because the floor is the cheaper option. (This is the floor's existing argument, now backed by the energy-budget Razor.)

### The Razor's discipline

The energy-budget Razor is not a proof — it is a heuristic for evaluating claims. It says: if a proposed explanation for the body's behaviour requires the body to spend energy without a return, the explanation is suspect. The body is an efficiency seeker; it does not waste energy. If the behaviour looks wasteful, either the behaviour is not what it appears (there is a hidden return), or the explanation is wrong (the body is not doing what the explanation says).

This is Occam's Razor in the original sense: the simpler explanation is the one with fewer assumptions, and "the body is being efficient" is a simpler assumption than "the body is being irrational, negligent, or broken." The energy-budget Razor adds: "efficient" means "minimising $M_B$," and $M_B$ is quantifiable (in principle, by indirect calorimetry and cognitive batteries). The Razor is falsifiable: if the body's behaviour does not minimise $M_B$ — if the expensive option is consistently chosen over the cheap one without a compensating return — the Razor is wrong, and the body is not the efficiency seeker the frame claims.

### The Razor and the Jaynes floor

The energy-budget Razor is held to the same epistemic standard as the rest of the spectral frame: model, not measurement. The claim "the body is an efficiency seeker" is a hypothesis, not a finding. It is falsifiable (show that the body systematically chooses high-$M_B$ options without return). It is interesting enough to be worth falsifying (it predicts that every apparently "irrational" body behaviour has an energy reason, which is a strong, testable claim). If it turns out that the body is not a consistent efficiency seeker — that it sometimes wastes energy for non-energy reasons (emotional, social, developmental) — the Razor weakens but does not die, because the claim was never that the body is *only* an efficiency seeker, just that energy minimisation is a good first-order explanation for its behaviour.

The worst case is Jaynes: a charming wrong hypothesis that named a useful question — "what is the energy budget, and is the behaviour the efficient option?" — and the answer turned out to be more nuanced than "always efficient." That is still worth having asked.

## The complete closed loop

The spectral hinge now has all its mechanisms:

1. **Storage:** the fascia stores the eigenstructure — eigenvectors as fiber directions, eigenvalues as prestress (see `the-spectral-hinge.md`).
2. **Survey:** the CNS surveys the eigenstructure by anomaly severity, prioritising the most anomalous eigenvectors (this asset).
3. **Cost:** every structural state has a maintenance burden — $M_B = E_{\text{metabolic}} + E_{\text{computational}}$ (see `maintenance-burden-equation.md`). The body minimises it (the energy-budget Razor).
4. **Re-computation:** the floor provides the known boundary that allows the eigenstructure to be re-solved, with the breath as the iterative solver (see `the-spectral-hinge.md`).
5. **Traversal:** the approach-and-retreat protocol traverses the eigenvector basis one direction at a time, in order of decreasing eigenvalue magnitude (see `the-spectral-hinge.md`).
6. **Report:** the felt vocabulary names what each stage feels like — good/ache/twinge/pain (protective), activating (developmental), unmasked (resolution) (see `the-felt-vocabulary.md`).
7. **Access:** the inspection boundary says you can only measure the eigenstructure from inside, using internal telemetry (see `the-inspection-boundary.md`).

Seven mechanisms, one frame. The fascia stores it; the CNS surveys it; the maintenance burden prices it; the floor re-computes it; the approach-and-retreat traverses it; the felt vocabulary reports it; the inspection boundary says you can only read it from inside. The energy-budget Razor is the analytical tool that evaluates claims about any of them: what is the energy budget, and is the behaviour the efficient option?

## Falsifiability

**Assertion: the CNS surveys the eigenstructure by anomaly severity and prioritises the most anomalous eigenvectors.**

Falsifier: if the order of tissue release during floor practice does not correlate with anomaly severity (i.e., if the CNS does not deal with the loudest signals first), the priority-queue claim weakens. Testable by tracking the sequence of felt reports (ache, twinge, releasing) during a floor session and comparing the order to the computed anomaly ranking. If the release order is random with respect to anomaly severity, the priority queue is not the mechanism.

**Assertion: old aches disappear because the CNS stops surveying them, not because the tissue is healed.**

Falsifier: if "forgotten" old restrictions show normal tissue properties on external inspection (palpation, ultrasound, imaging) — i.e., if the tissue is actually healed and the eigenvalue has returned to safe — then the old ache disappeared because it was resolved, not because it was demoted. The claim predicts that the tissue is still distorted even when the ache is forgotten. Testable by comparing tissue state in "forgotten" restriction sites to normal tissue. If the tissue is normal, the demotion claim is wrong. If the tissue is still distorted, the demotion claim holds.

**Assertion: the floor re-computation re-includes demoted eigenvectors, which is why old aches "come back."**

Falsifier: if old aches do not re-appear during floor practice (i.e., if the floor only produces new sensations, not the return of old ones), the re-survey claim weakens. The prediction is that the floor produces a characteristic pattern: new sensations first (the current top of the queue), then the return of old, forgotten sensations as the queue is drained. If the floor only produces new sensations and never old ones, the re-survey mechanism is not what is happening.

**Assertion: the body is an efficiency seeker that minimises $M_B$.**

Falsifier: if the body's behaviour systematically does not minimise $M_B$ — if the expensive option is consistently chosen over the cheap one without a compensating return — the energy-budget Razor is wrong. Testable in principle by comparing $M_B$ across behavioural options and checking whether the body tends to choose the lower one. In practice, the Razor is a heuristic for evaluating explanations, not a directly testable claim — but its predictions (guarding is cheaper than re-computation in the short term, the floor is cheaper than the mattress for re-computation, the fuse is cheaper than the alternative) are individually testable.
