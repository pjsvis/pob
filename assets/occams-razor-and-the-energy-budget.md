## The problem: everyone cites the wrong Razor

The most commonly cited formulation of Occam's Razor — "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity" (*Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*) — is not found anywhere in William of Ockham's extant works. It was coined by the Irish Franciscan philosopher John Punch in 1639, nearly 300 years after Ockham's death, in a commentary on Duns Scotus. Punch called it a "common axiom" (*axioma vulgare*) of the Scholastics, not Ockham's personal principle.

The term "Occam's Razor" itself did not appear until 1649, coined by Libert Froidmont in his *Philosophia Christiana de Anima*. Ockham never called it a razor, never used the "entities" formulation, and never stated the principle as a single aphorism. He used it as a methodological tool throughout his writings, but he stated it in several different ways, none of which is the one everyone quotes.

This matters because people who cite "Entia non sunt multiplicanda" as Ockham's words are making a false attribution — and false attributions are the kind of thing the book should not do. If the book references Occam's Razor, it should reference what Ockham actually said, or it should say "the principle commonly known as Occam's Razor" and acknowledge that the famous formulation is not his.

## What Ockham actually said

Three formulations appear in Ockham's extant works:

1. **"Numquam ponenda est pluralitas sine necessitate"** — "Plurality must never be posited without necessity." From *Quaestiones et decisiones in quattuor libros Sententiarum Petri Lombardi* (c. 1317-1319), i, dist. 27, qu. 2, K. This is the closest to the famous version, but it says "plurality" (*pluralitas*), not "entities" (*entia*). The distinction matters: plurality is about the number of explanations or causes posited, not about the number of things that exist. Ockham is saying: don't multiply *explanations* beyond what the phenomena require. He is not saying: don't multiply *things* beyond what exists. The Razor is a methodological principle about explanation, not an ontological principle about what exists.

2. **"Frustra fit per plura, quod potest fieri per pauciora"** — "It is pointless to do with more what can be done with fewer." From *Summa Totius Logicae*, i. 12. This is the most practical formulation — it is about efficiency, not ontology. If fewer explanations suffice, using more is futile. This is the formulation that connects most directly to the energy-budget Razor: if a behaviour can be explained by a lower maintenance burden, explaining it by a higher one is pointless.

3. **"Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate"** — "Plurality is not to be posited without necessity." A variant of (1), found in the Scholastic tradition and attributed to Ockham by later commentators. Again, *pluralitas* (plurality), not *entia* (entities).

The famous "Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem" — "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity" — was coined by John Punch in 1639. It is a paraphrase that shifts the principle from a methodological claim about explanations (don't posit plurality without necessity) to an ontological claim about entities (don't multiply entities beyond necessity). The shift is subtle but significant: Ockham was saying "don't over-explain," Punch was saying "don't over-populate your ontology." The two are related but not identical, and the conflation has caused centuries of confusion about what the Razor actually commits you to.

## The misattribution problem

The misattribution is not a pedantic quibble. It is an example of exactly the kind of error the book should avoid: citing a source for a claim the source did not make. The pattern is common — people cite "Occam's Razor" as "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity" and attribute it to Ockham, when Ockham never said it. The attribution feels authoritative because everyone does it, but it is wrong. The book's discipline is: if you cite a source, cite what the source actually said. If the source said something close but not identical, say "close but not identical" and name the difference.

The narrativised bibliography entry for Ockham (if the book cites him) should state what he actually said and note that the famous formulation is Punch's paraphrase, not Ockham's words. This is the anti-yap discipline applied to citations: the narrative sentence must be accurate, or the citation is cut.

## The energy-budget Razor

The book's energy-budget Razor is not Occam's Razor, and should not be presented as a version of it. It is a different principle, inspired by the same instinct (parsimony) but operating on a different quantity (energy, not entities).

Occam's Razor (what Ockham actually said): don't posit plurality without necessity. The test is: does the additional explanation do work that fewer explanations cannot? If not, drop it.

The energy-budget Razor: the body is an efficiency seeker that minimises $M_B = E_{\text{metabolic}} + E_{\text{computational}}$. The test is: is the behaviour the efficient option? If a behaviour has an energy cost, there is an energy reason.

The two Razors share the parsimony instinct — both prefer the cheaper explanation — but they differ in what they price. Occam prices explanations (fewer is better). The energy-budget Razor prices behaviours (lower $M_B$ is better). Occam is about theory choice; the energy-budget Razor is about the body's choice. They are complementary, not identical.

The connection is through Ockham's second formulation: "It is pointless to do with more what can be done with fewer" (*Frustra fit per plura, quod potest fieri per pauciora*). This is the efficiency formulation — if fewer suffices, more is futile. The energy-budget Razor is the same principle applied to the body: if a lower maintenance burden suffices, a higher one is futile. The body does not spend energy it does not need to spend. If it appears to, look for the hidden return or the wrong explanation.

## How the book should present this

If the book references Occam's Razor, it should:

1. **State what Ockham actually said**, not what Punch said. The formulation to cite is "Numquam ponenda est pluralitas sine necessitate" — "Plurality must never be posited without necessity" — or "Frustra fit per plura, quod potest fieri per pauciora" — "It is pointless to do with more what can be done with fewer." The second is the one that connects to the energy-budget Razor.

2. **Note the misattribution** if the book uses the famous "entities" formulation. The note should be brief — "the familiar 'entities should not be multiplied' is John Punch's 1639 paraphrase, not Ockham's own words" — and should not be pedantic. The point is accuracy, not pedantry.

3. **Present the energy-budget Razor as a distinct principle**, not as a version of Occam's Razor. They share the parsimony instinct but operate on different quantities. The energy-budget Razor is the body's Razor: it prices behaviours by maintenance burden, not explanations by entity count.

4. **Cite Ockham accurately in the bibliography.** The narrativised entry should state what Ockham said and what the book uses it for. If the book uses the "frustra fit per plura" formulation, cite the *Summa Totius Logicae*. If the book uses the "numquam ponenda est pluralitas" formulation, cite the *Sentences commentary*. Do not cite the *Entia non sunt multiplicanda* formulation as Ockham's — it is Punch's.

## The epistemic register

The energy-budget Razor, like the spectral hinge, is a model, not a measurement. It is held to the Jaynes floor: if the body turns out not to be a consistent efficiency seeker — if it systematically chooses high-$M_B$ options without return — the Razor is a charming wrong hypothesis that named a useful question. The question — "what is the energy budget, and is the behaviour the efficient option?" — is worth asking even if the answer turns out to be more nuanced than "always efficient."

The Razor's falsifiability is the same as the maintenance burden's: if resting metabolic rate and cognitive performance do not correlate with structural state in the predicted direction, the Razor weakens. The Razor is a heuristic for evaluating explanations, not a proof of the body's efficiency. It says: test the efficient explanation first. If it fails, test the inefficient one. But start with the one that assumes the body knows what it is doing, because the body has been solving these problems for a long time.

## The relationship to the book's other principles

The energy-budget Razor is the analytical tool that ties the spectral frame's mechanisms together. Each mechanism (storage, survey, re-computation, traversal, report, access) can be evaluated with the Razor: is this the efficient option? The answer, in each case, is yes — the body does it the cheap way because the cheap way works. The fascia stores the eigenstructure because storing it in tissue is cheaper than computing it in real time. The CNS surveys by anomaly severity because surveying everything is computationally unaffordable. The floor re-computes because the floor is the cheapest known-boundary environment. The approach-and-retreat traverses one eigenvector at a time because simultaneous traversal is unaffordable. The felt vocabulary reports by quality because quality carries more information per bit than magnitude. The inspection boundary says you can only measure from inside because the internal measurement is the cheap one (the body already has the sensors; the external observer would have to build them).

The Razor is the thread that makes the seven mechanisms feel like a coherent system rather than seven separate claims. They are all the efficient option for the problem they solve. The body is not doing seven different things; it is doing one thing — minimising $M_B$ — in seven different domains. The Razor names the common principle.
