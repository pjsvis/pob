# Traditional Practices: Sleeping Postures, Surfaces, and Floor Rest

*Source material for potential book inclusion. Companion to `floor-lying-tensor-analysis.md` (the tensor formalism) and `sleeping-positions.md` (the Tetley piece). This file catalogues the traditional-practice register — the records of floor-based sleeping and resting postures across cultures, religions, and traditional medical systems. The thesis is that the practices are extensively documented; what is missing is the translation into biomechanical language.*

## Why this file exists

The biomechanics register is thin (see `floor-lying-tensor-analysis.md` §0.5 — Cheng 2023 found no side-lying in 27 FE studies; Cary 2019 found four studies from 4186 screened). But the *traditional-practice* register is extensive. The gap between floor-lying-as-biomechanics and the existing record is a *translation* failure, not a *recording* failure. The traditions did not forget; the biomechanics field did not ask them.

This file catalogues what the traditions actually say, organised by source, so the connections to the tensor formalism can be drawn explicitly.

## 1. Buddhism: the lion posture (sīhaseyya)

**The prescribed sleeping posture of the Buddha and of Buddhist monastics.** Right side, one foot placed on top of the other, mindful and aware, with attention on the time of rising.

### Sources

- **DN 16, Mahā-parinibbāna Sutta** (the Great Discourse on the Total Unbinding). The Buddha's death scene — the most iconic moment in the Pali canon. The Buddha lies down between twin sal-trees, head to the north, "on his right side in the lion's sleeping posture, with one foot on top of the other, mindful & alert." He dies in this posture. This is the posture depicted in every reclining Buddha statue across the Buddhist world.
- **MN 53, Sekha Sutta** (Discourse for Learners). The lion posture is given as the standard monastic sleeping posture: "In the middle watch of the night, he lies down on his right side in the lion posture, foot resting on foot, mindful, clearly conscious, reflecting on the thought of getting up again." It is part of the graduated training — the prescribed rest position between periods of walking and sitting meditation.
- **SN 4.7, Supati Sutta** (Sleep). The Buddha, having spent much of the night walking meditation, lies down in the lion posture at dawn. Māra teases him for sleeping; the Buddha replies from the posture that the awakened one sleeps because craving has ended. The posture is presented as the normative Buddhist sleeping position.

### What it recommends

- **Position:** right lateral decubitus (right side lying), one foot on the other.
- **Surface:** the texts describe lying on a prepared surface (cloth, robe folded in four, between trees). In the monastic context, this is a simple mat or cloth on the floor — not a raised bed.
- **State:** mindful and aware, not unconscious. The posture is a *deliberate* resting position, not a collapse into sleep. This is closer to the Alexander "constructive rest" tradition than to modern sleep.
- **Iconography:** the reclining Buddha (present in Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana traditions across Sri Lanka, Thailand, Burma, Cambodia, Laos, China, Japan, India) is a cultural monument to side-lying-on-a-surface as the normative resting posture. It is possibly the most widely depicted sleeping posture in human history.

### Connections to the tensor formalism

- Right side-lying is an *asymmetric* boundary condition (§6 of the tensor analysis) — the body is loaded laterally, with the right hip, shoulder, and leg as contact points. This is exactly the geometry the block-supported side-lying practice uses, and the geometry the Cheng 2023 review found *entirely absent* from computational biomechanics.
- The "mindful and aware" instruction is the neural component — the practitioner is directed to maintain awareness, which is the cortical correlate of the active-stress down-regulation in §3. The posture is not "falling asleep" but "resting with awareness," which is the condition under which the CNS lowers **a** without the defensive bracing of unconscious sleep.
- The surface is firm (cloth/mat on floor or earth), not compliant. This is the rigid Signorini contact of §1, not the Winkler foundation of a mattress.

## 2. Ayurveda: sleeping surface typology and left-side sleeping

**Ayurveda is a traditional medical system that classified sleeping surface and sleeping position as health-relevant variables approximately two millennia ago.** This is the most systematic traditional treatment of the variables the biomechanics field neglected.

### Sources

- **Charaka Samhita** (ca. 300 BCE–200 CE), Sutrasthana. Nidra (sleep) is one of the three *trayopastambha* (sub-pillars of life, alongside diet and celibacy). Charaka classifies types of sleep and gives rules for its timing, duration, and posture.
- **Sushruta Samhita** (ca. 600 BCE–500 CE), Sutrasthana and Sharira Sthana. Sushruta recommends left-side sleeping after meals (*vāmaśayyā*) because the digestive fire (*jatharagni*) is located above the navel on the left, and left-side lying facilitates digestion. He also classifies sleeping surfaces.
- **Ashtanga Hridaya** (Vagbhata, ca. 7th century CE), Sutrasthana. Summarises and systematises the earlier texts. Gives the sleeping-surface typology and the post-meal posture sequence.
- **Yogaratnakara** (ca. 17th century). Gives a post-meal sleeping sequence: lie supine for 8 breaths, turn right for 16 breaths, turn left for 32 breaths, then sleep in any posture. Left-side is preferred for digestion.

### Sleeping surface typology (Ayurveda)

The Ayurvedic texts classify sleeping surfaces by their health effects — a *material typology* of the boundary condition:

| Surface | Sanskrit | Effect |
|---------|----------|--------|
| Comfortable bed (well-made) | Anukula shayya | Good for sleep, nourishment, pleasure; relieves fatigue; pacifies vata; aphrodisiac |
| **Ground / floor** | **Bhūmi-śayyā** | **Pacifies vata, nourishes the body, gives strength, aphrodisiac** |
| Cot (raised bed) | Khatvā | Vitiate vata |
| Wooden planks | Dārupatta | Vitiate vata (more strongly) |
| Hanging bed | Andolikā | Relieves fatigue, promotes longevity, pacifies vata, reduces sweating |
| Bed on ground (mat/cloth) | — | Nourishing, aphrodisiac |
| Direct ground (no mat) | — | Can aggravate vata (in cold/wet seasons) |

**Key observation:** the Ayurvedic typology distinguishes *ground sleeping* (bhūmi-śayyā) from *cot sleeping* (khatvā) and from *wooden planks* (dārupatta), and assigns them *different* health effects. This is a classification of the boundary condition — exactly the variable the biomechanics field collapsed into "mattress." The Ayurvedic system recognises that the *material* of the sleeping surface matters, and specifically rates the ground as strength-giving and vata-pacifying.

### Sleeping position recommendations (Ayurveda)

- **Left side (vāmaśayyā):** recommended after meals, for digestion. The rationale is that the digestive fire sits on the left above the navel, so left-side lying positions the stomach to aid digestion. This is the most commonly recommended Ayurvedic sleeping position.
- **Right side:** recommended in the post-meal sequence (briefly, before turning left), and sometimes for cardiac reasons (the left lateral position can be uncomfortable for those with enlarged hearts — a clinical observation that modern cardiology has confirmed).
- **Supine:** mentioned in the post-meal sequence (briefly, 8 breaths). Not the primary recommendation.
- **Prone:** explicitly advised against. "One should not sleep downwards and in awkward position" (Sushruta, Chi. 24/98).

### Connections to the tensor formalism

- **The surface typology is a boundary-condition classification.** Bhūmi-śayyā (ground) = rigid Signorini contact (§1). Khatvā (cot) = raised, potentially with different compliance. Anukula shayya (comfortable bed) = closer to the Winkler foundation. The Ayurvedic texts *already made the distinction* that the biomechanics field (Cheng 2023) found only in the mattress-comfort register.
- **The "vata-pacifying" effect of ground sleeping** is the traditional-medical correlate of the active-stress down-regulation (§3). Vata in Ayurveda is the dosha of movement, tension, and nervous system activity. "Pacifying vata" = reducing the activation vector **a**. The claim that ground sleeping pacifies vata is, in tensor terms, the claim that the rigid floor allows **a** to decrease — which is exactly §3.
- **"Gives strength" (bhūmi-śayyā)** is the traditional correlate of the reference-config remodelling (§6) — the body rebuilds its structural baseline on the firm surface over time.
- **Left-side sleeping** is an asymmetric BC (§6), and the Ayurvedic recommendation is specifically *post-meal* — the GI system is active, and the position is chosen to load the stomach appropriately. This connects to §7 (the GI as a second active-stress structure reading the same autonomic input) — the Ayurvedic tradition identified the GI-posture coupling, though in dosha language rather than tensor language.
- **Prone avoidance** is consistent with the biomechanics: prone sleeping maximises lumbar lordosis (the heaviest part of the body sinks, the legs extend, the lumbar spine is forced into extension) — a sustained asymmetric load in the *wrong* direction for most people.

### Extending the Ayurvedic typology into a research framework (resolves open question 4)

The Ayurvedic surface typology is a *qualitative material* typology — a list of surface kinds classified by health effect. What it lacks, and what the tensor formalism supplies, is the *quantitative axes* that turn the list into a model. Three axes are forced into existence by the typology's own boundary cases:

1. **Compliance, as a field not a scalar.** The van Casteren (2012) measurement of orang-utan nests shows the ape platform is *nonuniform* — rigid woven edges, gently compliant lined centre. The Ayurvedic *bhūmi-śayyā* (ground) is uniform-rigid; the *anukula shayya* (comfortable bed) is uniform-compliant; but the *andolikā* (hanging bed) and the charpai (taut woven cot) are compliance *fields* that vary across the contact patch. A scalar compliance collapses all of these; a compliance field c(**x**) preserves them. The ground is c ≈ 0 everywhere; the mattress is c ≈ c_max everywhere; the charpai is low-c at the frame, higher-c at the weave centre; the hanging bed is a coupled field.
2. **Thermal coupling.** The Korean *yō* on *ondol* is floor-sleeping with radiant heat — the same compliance as the Japanese futon, a different thermal boundary condition. The Ayurvedic typology registers the hanging bed as reducing sweating (a thermal effect) but has no axis for it. Thermal coupling matters mechanically because tissue creep is temperature-dependent (collagen relaxation accelerates with temperature); the warm floor is a different remodelling environment from the cold one, at identical compliance. This is the axis the *bhūmi-śayyā* caveat ("can aggravate vata in cold/wet seasons") gestures at without naming.
3. **Load-dependent nonlinearity — the degenerate case that forces the axis.** Memory foam on a futon is rigid at high load (the futon stops you bottoming out) and compliant at low load (the foam gives under the light contact of a relaxed limb). This is a nonlinear compliance — c depends on the local pressure p, c = c(p) — and it is the case the Ayurvedic typology has no slot for, which is why it feels like a category error rather than a member of a category. The captive Samson work gave orang-utans memory foam (SPCI score 1.5) and found more-complex platforms reduced fragmentation — this is the mattress industry's Exhibit A, and it is *exactly* this degenerate case. The honest reading: the captive comparison is memory-foam-vs-bare-laminate-floor (not firm-vs-compliant-within-a-platform), and the apes were still side-lying on it. The nonlinear-compliance axis lets you say *what* the memory-foam-on-futon is (a high-softness-at-low-load, high-rigidity-at-high-load surface) rather than whether it is "really" firm or "really" soft — and it predicts that it will feel compliant to a relaxed body and rigid to a braced one, which is a real and testable distinction the scalar typology cannot make.

With these three axes the Ayurvedic typology becomes a compliance-field × thermal-coupling × nonlinearity map, and the degenerate cases (memory-foam-on-futon, the heated ondol, the swinging *andolikā*, the charpai's taut-but-ventilated weave) are the boundary points that force the new axes into existence rather than anomalies that break the classification. The framework is testable: each cell of the map predicts a different **a**-down-regulation trajectory (§3 of the tensor analysis) and a different remodelling regime (§6). This is the research framework the open question asked for — use the Ayurvedic typology, extend it with the three axes the tensor formalism supplies, and the typology stops being a list and starts being a model.

## 3. Yoga: śavāsana and floor-based practice

**The entire yoga tradition is floor work.** The most direct connection to "lying on the floor doing nothing" is śavāsana (corpse pose), but the broader tradition is a system of floor-based postural practice.

### Sources

- **Patanjali, Yoga Sutras** (ca. 200 BCE–200 CE). The foundational text. Defines āsana as "sthiram sukham āsanam" (steady and comfortable posture) — 2.46. The practice of āsana is floor-based.
- **Hatha Yoga Pradipika** (Swami Swatmarama, ca. 15th century CE). The classical text on hatha yoga postures. Śavāsana is described: "Lying down on the ground, like a corpse, is called śavāsana. It removes fatigue and allows the mind to rest." (1.32)
- **Modern yoga tradition.** Śavāsana is the universal resting pose — every yoga session ends with it. It is supine, on a thin mat or blanket on the floor, arms slightly away from the body, palms up, feet falling outward, eyes closed. The practitioner is instructed to *consciously relax* each part of the body — a directed active-stress down-regulation.

### What it recommends

- **Position:** supine (face up), arms away from body, feet relaxed outward.
- **Surface:** thin mat or blanket on the floor. Firm.
- **State:** conscious relaxation. Not sleep. The practitioner is directed to release tension systematically — scanning the body and releasing each part. This is a *cortically directed* active-stress down-regulation, distinct from the autonomic down-regulation of floor-sleeping.
- **Duration:** 5–15 minutes typically. Long enough for the tonic musculature to release.

### Connections to the tensor formalism

- **Śavāsana is the Alexander "constructive rest" position in yoga clothing.** Supine on a firm floor, knees can be bent (in some variants) or extended, arms away from body. The contact is the rigid floor; the instruction is to release. This is the §3 down-regulation in a supine (symmetric) configuration — the floor exposes structural deviations (§1) while the CNS lowers **a**.
- **"Removes fatigue" = the active-stress down-regulation.** The Hatha Yoga Pradipika's claim is that śavāsana removes fatigue. In tensor terms, fatigue is the accumulated cost of maintaining **a** against gravity during the day; śavāsana removes it by allowing **a** to drop.
- **The directed relaxation is the cortical route to lowering **a**.** Unlike floor-sleeping (which relies on the autonomic down-regulation), śavāsana is a *practitioner-directed* release — the cortex instructs the muscles to release. This is the same mechanism as Feldenkrais ATM and Alexander constructive rest: the CNS is the authority on **a**, and directed attention is the route to lowering it.

## 4. Floor-sleeping cultures

**A large fraction of humanity has slept on firm floor surfaces throughout recorded history.** These are not marginal practices — they are the normative sleeping arrangements of major civilisations.

### Japanese: futon on tatami

- The traditional Japanese sleeping arrangement is a *futon* (thin cotton mattress, ~5–10 cm thick) laid directly on *tatami* (woven rush matting over a rigid rice-straw core). The surface is firm. The futon is folded and stored during the day.
- This has been the Japanese norm until very recently (post-WWII Westernisation introduced raised beds). It remains common, especially outside Tokyo.
- The futon-on-tatami is a firm surface — closer to the floor than to a Western mattress. It does not sink significantly under body weight.

### Korean: yō on ondol

- The Korean *yō* (thin mattress/blanket) is laid on the *ondol* (underfloor heating system — traditionally a heated stone floor, now a heated concrete floor). The surface is firm and warm.
- The ondol floor is rigid. The yō is thin. This is floor-sleeping with the addition of radiant heat.

### Indian: charpai

- The *charpai* (also *khat*, *manji*) is a woven cot — a wooden frame with a tightly woven surface (rope, cotton, or synthetic webbing). It is raised but the surface is firm and taut, not compliant like a Western mattress. It provides ventilation in hot climates.
- Ayurvedic texts list *khatvā* (cot) as a distinct surface type from *bhūmi-śayyā* (ground), noting it "vitiates vata" — suggesting the raised, woven surface is less beneficial than the direct ground.

### Other traditions

- **Mongolian:** sleeping on the ground in the *ger* (yurt), on felt and hides over the earth floor.
- **Bedouin:** sleeping on the ground or on woven mats, in hot climates where floor-level sleeping is cooler.
- **Native American:** sleeping on hides and mats on the earth, sometimes on raised platforms.
- **Tibetan:** Tetley documents Tibetan caravaneers sleeping on their shins (the anterior tibia, where there is no muscle, to reduce heat loss) — a specialised floor-sleeping posture for cold environments.
- **African:** Tetley documents side-lying without a pillow, on a palm-leaf mattress on a concrete floor — the posture the Buddhist lion posture also recommends.

### Connections to the tensor formalism

- **All of these are firm surfaces.** None of them is a compliant mattress. The futon, the yō, the charpai, the hide-on-ground — all are rigid or semi-rigid boundary conditions. The cultural universality of floor-sleeping is the cultural correlate of the Samson primatology (apes build firm platforms) and the biomechanical argument (firm surfaces expose structural deviation and allow **a** to down-regulate).
- **The cultural shift away from floor-sleeping is recent and localised.** Raised beds with compliant mattresses are a post-industrial Western innovation that has spread globally only in the last 50–100 years. The default human sleeping surface, for the vast majority of people across the vast majority of history, is firm.
- **The Tetley observation** that acculturating populations develop more musculoskeletal problems (the "flying doctor" who saw more arthritis as tribesmen became "civilised") is the ethnographic correlate of the §6 remodelling argument — the shift from firm to compliant surfaces removes the sustained low-load remodelling and allows structural deviations to accumulate.
- **The developmental default: the curled infant.** The ontogeny thread (resolved, no longer open): infant apes and infant humans both sleep curled against a caregiver's body; the supine-solitary sprawl is a learned/cultural override, not a developmental default. The McKenna/Gettler "breastsleeping" line (McKenna & Gettler 2015, *Acta Paediatrica*; Gettler & McKenna 2011, *AJPA*) makes the argument explicit: the dyadic, breastfeeding-cosleeping context is the only setting in which normal human infant sleep is measurable, and the infant sleeps curled against the mother — the supine-solitary-sleeping infant is a Western cultural construction of the last ~150 years. So the developmental default (infant curled), the phylogenetic default (apes side-lying ~65% per Samson §0.5 of the tensor analysis), and the cultural default (Buddhist right-side, Ayurvedic left-side, floor-sleeping cultures side-lying) are the **same posture** — three lines of evidence (evolutionary + cultural + developmental) converging on the curled side-lying posture. This is the convergence the book chapter is structured around.

### The cultural-shift epidemiology: what is and is not there (resolves open question 3)

The epidemiological evidence for a musculoskeletal deterioration tracking the floor-to-mattress shift is *there if you know what to look for*, and it is *confounded*. The honest framing turns on a load-bearing distinction: the surface predicts **posture** cleanly; **pain** is downstream of posture and forty other things. The posture-isolating studies point the way the tensor formalism predicts; the pain-isolating studies are a bundle of confounds (floor-sitting, squatting, lower obesity, somatising-tendency differences, reporting culture) that cut either way.

**The posture-isolating signal — the cleanest single source, and elided from the English biomechanics citation graph.** Kikusawa (1978), *Nippon Eiseigaku Zasshi* (Japanese Journal of Hygiene) 33(4):431, "The Influence of Life Style on Physique" — a cohort study of 108 bed-using vs 108 futon-using Japanese women's-college students, 12+ years of each lifestyle, matched, silhouette-photogrammetry. Finding: the bed group had significantly greater upper-body backward lean, greater back-curvature (the "W-shape" soft-bed posture of the bedding-ergonomics literature, e.g. Ohara), more round-back / kyphotic tendency; the futon group was more upright. This is a longitudinal cohort that isolates the sleeping-surface variable (over 12 years, holding chair-sitting as a separate factor) and finds the postural deviation the tensor formalism predicts the compliant surface produces. It is almost certainly not in the biomechanics citation graph — a 1978 Japanese hygiene journal. It is the strongest single card for the cultural-shift claim, and it needs a verified translation note or a corroborating source before it goes in the book. The Ohara / W-shape bedding-ergonomics literature it agrees with is the corroborator.

**The pain-isolating signal — supportive but confounded.** The CUPID Japan data (Matsudaira, Palmer, Coggon et al., *Occup. Environ. Med.* 2011; Kawaguchi et al., *BMC Musculoskelet. Disord.* 2017) show Japanese workers have **lower** regional pain prevalence and far lower musculoskeletal sickness absence than UK counterparts (e.g. wrist/hand pain 7% vs 30% in UK office workers; sickness absence for LBP 4% vs >12% in UK). This *cuts against* the simple "floor good, bed bad" story until you remember the confounds — floor-sitting and squatting (the full-squat and heels-on-ground sitting that Tetley documents reset the sacroiliac joints), lower obesity, somatising-tendency differences, and a reporting/compensation culture that suppresses absence. Suka & Yoshida (2008, *Environ. Health Prev. Med.*) quantify the LBP burden in Japan (≈11.8M sufferers, ≈947k QALYs lost) but is a snapshot, not a trend. Suzuki & Ohgushi (1989, *J. Japan Soc. Nurs. Res.*) frame the futon as *worse* than the bed for sacral/calcaneal pressure — which is the pressure-ulcer/comfort register, and is the *same property* the structural-deviation register calls *good* (concentrated contact at bony prominences is the floor exposing deviation; in the pressure-ulcer register it is the floor causing sores).

**The honest epidemiology section for the book.** Write it around the posture-deviation data (Kikusawa + the Ohara W-shape corroboration), because posture is the surface-isolated outcome. Treat the pain epidemiology as supportive-but-confounded context, not proof. The claim the evidence supports is: *the surface predicts posture cleanly; pain is downstream of posture and forty other things, and the floor-to-mattress shift is one of the forty*. That is the claim that survives Hume's Razor. The stronger claim ("floor sleeping prevents back pain") does not survive it in the current data — the CUPID comparison is the counterexample that has to be explained, not explained away.

### The pregnancy exception: the third-trimester regime and the supine contraindication

There is one population for whom the floor-lying advice has a hard, evidence-based modification: **pregnant women in the third trimester (≥28 weeks).** But the framing has to be honest, and the medical literature gets it half-right in the wrong order.

**The third-trimester woman is a different endocrine-behavioral regime — not a fragile patient.** Late pregnancy produces a hormone cascade (the estrogen/prolactin/oxytocin climb, the cortisol shift) that drives what the obstetric-ethology literature calls *nesting*: a focused, determined, low-tolerance-for-obstruction burst of physical activity. In animal models it is den-building and territorial defence; in humans it is the woman who decides, at term, that the palm tree in the garden is superfluous to requirements *right now* and takes an axe to it, while the rest of the household is too intimidated to bring her a glass of water. The third-trimester woman is not fragile in the sense the medical register assumes; she is, endocrinologically and behaviorally, **a force** — high energy, high determination, and a tolerance for risk that makes the people around her nervous. **This is the honest reason the floor-lying subculture among pregnant women is unstudied:** the women themselves are too busy being forces of nature to present for study, and the researchers are too intimidated to approach. The medical literature studies the *pause* — the moment the nesting regime stops and the woman lies down — and mistakes it for the whole woman.

**The floor-lying subculture is the rest-state of the nesting regime, not the rest-state of a fragile patient.** The surface question matters during the pause, because the body is carrying a baby; but the question only arises because the regime has paused. Order matters: regime first, surface second. Leading with the surface (as the medical literature does) is the same error the mattress industry makes — defining the woman by the surface she lies on rather than the regime that put her there.

**The surface-level constraint that applies during the pause: the supine contraindication.** Once the nesting regime has paused and the woman is lying down, there is a hard, evidence-based rule: **do not lie supine from 28 weeks.** The literature here is large and active — opened by Stacey et al. (2011, *BMJ*) and consolidated by an individual-participant-data meta-analysis (Cronin et al. 2019, *EClinicalMedicine*; 5 case-control studies, N=3108): supine going-to-sleep position after 28 weeks carries an adjusted odds ratio of **2.63 (95% CI 1.72–4.04) for late stillbirth**. The mechanism is exactly what a continuum-mechanics reading predicts: in the third trimester the gravid uterus, lying over the IVC and aorta in the supine position, compresses the inferior vena cava (~85% diameter reduction on MRI) and the aorta (~30%), reducing uteroplacental flow → fetal hypoxia → stillbirth. NICE (NG201), the Australian Safer Baby Bundle, NZ "Sleep on Side," and the UK Tommy's campaign now all advise left- **or right-** lateral going-to-sleep from 28 weeks (left and right are statistically equally safe for stillbirth; the left-lateral has the MRI-measured IVC-volume advantage, Barbosa et al. 2019).

**The synthesis for the book, which the pregnancy literature and the floor-lying literature have not been put in the same room over:**

- The third-trimester danger is **supine**, not floor. Floor-lying on the side in the third trimester is fine by this mechanism and is, if anything, the *recommended* posture done on a firmer surface (the Ayurvedic *bhūmi-śayyā* prescription, now with a specific obstetric reason).
- The floor's firmness is a **benefit** here, not a cost: a compliant mattress lets the gravid uterus sink into the compressive supine position; a firm floor holds the lateral geometry without accommodation.
- This is the one population for whom the Buddhist lion posture (right-side, one foot on the other) and the Ayurvedic left-side prescription are not merely traditional but are the **currently recommended obstetric posture** — the traditions got there first, the obstetric literature caught up in 2011.
- The clinical caveat: the recommendation is *going-to-sleep* on the side; if a woman wakes on her back she should just roll back. The going-to-sleep position is the one held longest.
- **But:** the medical register is the smaller half of the truth. The bigger half is that the third-trimester woman is endocrinologically and behaviorally a different regime — the nesting instinct, the axe, the palm tree — and the floor-lying subculture is the rest-state of *that* regime. The book has to hold both: the regime (Roberta with the axe) and the surface (the lateral floor that the regime, when it pauses, should lie on). The medical literature holds only the surface and loses the woman.

The Kember et al. 2024 *Front. Physiol.* review of maternal posture and fetal physiology is the bridge reference for the medical half — a 242-study narrative review that has the obstetric side but does not see the nesting-regime side at all, because the nesting regime does not present in a posture-measurement study. The nesting-regime half is, at present, ethnographic and first-person (the Monty/Roberta anecdote is the form the data takes); worth a brief, not a chapter. **Epistemic honesty:** the anecdote is not a single story — it is a pattern recognised by adjacency (the observer placed, repeatedly, to see the nesting regime from the older-sibling position across multiple pregnancies, with Roberta a confirming instance), which is why it reads as data when the medical register has no slot for it. The book's pattern-recognition generally runs on *trained adjacency* — observers placed to see something before they have the language for it, with the tensor formalism supplying the language afterward. That is a different epistemics than the RCT, and it is the epistemics the book actually runs on; it should be stated once, plainly, so the reader knows what kind of claim is being made when the anecdotes appear.

## 5. The Alexander Technique: constructive rest / semi-supine

**The Alexander Technique's "constructive rest" position is the modern Western practice that most directly corresponds to floor-lying for structural release.**

### Sources

- **F. M. Alexander** (1869–1955). Developed the technique in the early 20th century. The "lying-down work" (also called semi-supine, active rest, constructive rest) is a core practice.
- **Mabel Todd** (1880–1956), *The Thinking Body* (1937). Coined "constructive rest" in the ideokinetic tradition. Sweigard (*Human Movement Potential*, 1974) formalised it.
- **Mouritz companion** (alexandertechnique.com / mouritz.org). Documents the history: the practice originated with Alexander in Australia, was standardised in the first teacher training course (1931–34), and has been a core practice of the technique ever since.

### What it recommends

- **Position:** supine, knees bent, feet flat on the floor, head on a small support (books). Arms resting on the torso or slightly away.
- **Surface:** firm floor (carpet or thin mat). "The floor is better than a bed as it will give a level surface for better support" (Fitz-Simon, ACAT).
- **State:** "A very good opportunity just to give orders — to inhibit and give orders and not do anything, and you haven't got the problem of your equilibrium" (Marjory Barlow). The practitioner is instructed to *not do* — to inhibit the habitual patterns of tension and allow the body to release. This is a cortical (inhibitory) route to lowering **a**.
- **Duration:** 10–20 minutes. "20 minutes is considered the optimal amount of time to allow your spine to regain its full height."
- **The semi-supine position is distinguished from flat supine** — the bent knees reduce the pull of the psoas on the lumbar spine, allowing the lower back to release against the floor. This is a geometric refinement of the supine position to optimise the release of specific structures.

### What it does NOT recommend

- **Side-lying is explicitly discouraged.** The Alexander tradition says side-lying "creates asymmetrical tension patterns." This is the imprecation that the tensor analysis (§6) argues applies to *braced* side-lying (with tone held, on a compliant surface), not to *released* side-lying (with **a** down-regulated, on a firm floor, with a block).

### Connections to the tensor formalism

- **Constructive rest is the Western practitioner tradition that independently arrived at floor-lying for structural release.** The position (supine, firm floor, knees up, head supported) is the symmetric configuration; the block-supported side-lying of §6 is the asymmetric extension. Both are the same mechanism (rigid floor, **a** down-regulated, passive creep) in different geometries.
- **"Regain full height" = the reference-config remodelling.** The Alexander claim that constructive rest allows the spine to "regain its full height" is the clinical-language version of the §6 claim that the reference configuration remodels under sustained low load. The intervertebral discs rehydrate when the compressive load is removed (the supine position minimises disc loading); the fascial structures creep toward their unloaded length.
- **The "inhibition" instruction is the cortical route to lowering **a**.** Alexander's "inhibition" (the technical term for withholding the habitual response) is the cortical mechanism that prevents the re-recruitment of the compensatory tone. The practitioner is directed to *not do* — which, in tensor terms, is the instruction to let **a** fall rather than to hold it at its compensatory setpoint.
- **The explicit caution against side-lying is the point of §6's sharpening.** The Alexander tradition is right that *braced* side-lying (tone held, compliant surface) creates asymmetries. The block-supported, released, firm-floor side-lying is a different regime that the tradition did not consider — because the tradition is a *practice* tradition (it tells you what to do), not a *biomechanics* tradition (it does not model the BC and the active-stress decomposition). The tensor formalism is what distinguishes the two regimes.

## 6. Thai massage: sustained passive loading

**Thai massage (Nuad Boran) is the practitioner tradition that most closely corresponds to sustained low-load tissue mobilisation along the body's long myofascial lines.**

### Sources

- **Traditional Thai medicine** (referenced in the user's personal account). Thai massage is a system of assisted yoga — the practitioner moves the recipient through a sequence of stretches and compressions along the *sen* (energy) lines, which map roughly onto the myofascial trains (thoracolumbar fascia, iliotibial band, plantar fascia, the long-span passive structures of §4).
- The practice is performed on a firm mat on the floor. The recipient is passive; the practitioner applies sustained, low-force loading along the lines.

### What it recommends

- **Position:** varied — supine, prone, side-lying, seated — depending on the line being worked.
- **Surface:** firm mat on the floor.
- **State:** the recipient is passive. The practitioner applies the force. The recipient is instructed to relax — the "relax more" advice that originated this whole investigation.
- **Duration:** 1–2 hours typically. Long enough for creep to occur.
- **Force:** low, sustained, gravity-assisted where possible. The practitioner uses body weight, not muscle force.

### Connections to the tensor formalism

- **Thai massage is practitioner-applied sustained low-load along the long-span passive structures.** This is the same mechanism as the block-supported side-lying of §6 — sustained low-load creep along the myofascial lines — but with a practitioner providing the force instead of gravity + a block.
- **The "relax more" advice is the key.** The practitioner cannot mobilise tissue that is being actively defended. The recipient must lower **a** for the passive loading to reach the connective tissue. This is why the Thai masseuse's advice ("you need to relax more") was diagnostic — she identified that the problem was insufficient autonomous down-regulation, not insufficient external manipulation.
- **The sen lines map onto the myofascial trains.** The Thai system's energy lines correspond anatomically to the long-span passive structures (thoracolumbar fascia, IT band, plantar fascia, Achilles) that §4 says the stress redistributes *into* as the active tone drops. The Thai tradition identified these lines empirically — through practice, not biomechanics — and the tensor formalism identifies them as the structural load path.
- **The floor is the workspace.** Thai massage is done on the floor, on a firm mat, not on a raised table. This is consistent with the floor as the rigid reference frame (§1) — the practitioner works against the floor, using it as the stable boundary for the applied forces.

## 7. Doing nothing: the gate, the enabling conditions, and the calibration probes

This section resolves open question 2 (the "mindful and aware" instruction) and records the practice-theory that cuts across all the traditions above. The register is first-person where it has to be (the phenomenology is the data) and tensor where it can be (the mechanism is the model).

### The awareness question resolved: awareness is the gate, not the engine

The open question posed a real fork: is the cortical route to **a** down-regulation more effective than the autonomic route, or is the awareness instruction a *monitoring* instruction rather than a *mechanism*? The resolution, stated as plainly as it can be: **the autonomic nervous system runs the engine; awareness is the gate that keeps the engine running.** You do not lower **a** by thinking; the CNS lowers **a** on its own when it has no reason not to. "Doing nothing" is correct as the *engine* — the autonomic system runs d**a**/dt < 0 without your help, and you cannot out-compute it (your CNS understands continuum mechanics and tensor analysis; you do not). But "doing nothing" is *not* "being unconscious" — the moment you are asleep you may brace, and the floor will not tell you. So awareness is not the *mechanism* (it does not itself lower **a**); it is the *gate* — cortical inhibition of the habitual re-recruitment, which is the condition under which the autonomic engine is left alone to do the work the cortex cannot do. The Buddhist "mindful and aware" (DN 16, MN 53) and the Alexander "inhibition" are the same instruction in different registers: stay awake enough to *not interfere*, which is the condition under which the thing you cannot do (the tensor analysis) gets done by the thing that can (the CNS).

### The phenomenology of doing nothing — the gate is structural, not vigilant

The deeper observation, which corrects the "awareness = gate" formulation above toward something more precise: the gate is *in the situation*, not in the practitioner. The floor physically removes the affordances for doing-steps, doing-dishes, doing-anything-upright. The "should get up and get my steps in" thought arrives and *has no target* — because there is nothing one can do, from the floor, without first getting up, and getting up is the one thing one is not doing. So the thought is discarded, without a thought, for lack of an object. This is why floor-doing-nothing is *easy* (the user's word, and the right one) in a way that cushion-meditation is not: the floor scaffolds the gate by removing the affordances; cushion-meditation must hold the gate against a world that still affords doing. The Alexander tradition insists on cortical inhibition precisely because it is done on a floor *with the expectation of rising* — MN 53's "reflecting on the thought of getting up again" carries the affordance with it; the Buddhist instruction builds the gate back in that the bare floor drops out. Over time, on the floor, one slips into a do-nothing frame of mind — the gate shifts from held to structural, from effortful to environmental.

### The autonomic reflects its own safeness back at the conscious mind

Once the autonomic engine has got going, it appears to reflect its own feeling of safeness back at the conscious mind — awareness shifts from *being about* the body to *being of* the body's settled state. This is the phenomenological move from monitoring to inhabiting, and it is the felt content the traditions name: "equanimity" (Buddhism), "the primary control" / "inhibition" (Alexander), "pacifies vata" (Ayurveda). The tensor formalism supplies the mechanical correlate: **a** falls, the stress field redistributes, the interoceptive readout of that redistribution *is* what "safeness" feels like, and as it becomes the dominant signal it becomes the content of awareness rather than its object. **Honest epistemic status:** this is a first-person report of a mechanism the formalism predicts but cannot verify. It maps onto polyvagal theory's "neuroception of safety" (Porges), but Porges is contested and the formalism does not need him; the honest framing is that the phenomenology is real, reported across traditions, and the formalism supplies the mechanical correlate without over-claiming the neural substrate (Hume's Razor — the felt safeness is the prediction, not the proof).

### The enabling conditions: safe, warm, comfy — the boundary-condition specification

The key things for floor work are the **safe, warm, and comfy** feelings. This is the boundary-condition specification for the whole practice, and "comfy" is the word that needs the most careful handling — because the bedding industry has captured it, and because in English "comfy" and "comfortable" are not the same good.

**Comfy is not comfortable.** *Comfortable* is an expansive comfort — a broad high plateau, a global (or broad-basin) maximum of the comfort function. *Comfy* is a **local maximum of comfort amid a landscape of less-comfortable maxima** — a niche that dominates its neighbourhood but not the whole space. The floor is never comfortable (not the global max of somatic comfort — the bath is, or the mattress claims to be), but it becomes comfy once the body has settled: a local optimum the autonomic system prefers even when the somatic system does not. This maps onto the two-readouts structure of the tensor formalism: the somatic readout (skin, muscle, pressure) says "not the global max"; the autonomic readout (low **a**, safeness) says "this is a local max and I'm staying." The bedding industry's "comfy" is the global max of somatic comfort with the autonomic readout suppressed; the floor's "comfy" is the autonomic max with the somatic readout accepted as non-maximal. The two are different goods, and the conflation is the mattress industry's core move. **The book's thesis in miniature: you choose the floor for *recuperation* (the autonomic good) and the bath for *comfort* (the somatic good); the mattress is sold as both and delivers neither** — it removes the structural-deviation signal (no recuperation) while only imitating the bath's support (no real comfort; you do not emerge from a mattress with your hip mobilised).

In the floor-lying context, then: "comfy" means the contact manifold is stable (the floor is rigid and the body is supported, not balanced), the temperature is regulated (tissue creep is temperature-dependent; the warm floor is a different remodelling environment from the cold one — the Ayurvedic *bhūmi-śayyā* "aggravates vata in cold/wet seasons" caveat is this axis), and the CNS has no threat to down-regulate against (no cold, no hard edge into a rib, no anxiety about the surface). "Comfy" in this sense is the *precondition* for d**a**/dt < 0, not a synonym for soft. The bedding industry's "comfy" (soft, compliant, accommodating) is the opposite boundary condition: it removes the structural-deviation signal and lets **a** stay where it was. The two "comfy"s must not be conflated; the voice pass will need to hold the distinction, and the comfy/comfortable distinction above is the tool for holding it.

### The calibration probes: do them a few times, learn the lesson, then stop

"If you must do something" — the escape hatch for when the do-nothing frame slips. The probes below are *calibration*, not practice. They teach a specific lesson (where a limb's degrees of freedom sit relative to an anchored base) and then lose their novelty, because the lesson is learned and the native mechanism (pandiculation) is unblocked. They are the same mechanism as Feldenkrais ATM lessons: the slow repetition is not the point; the embodied reorganisation *after* the repetition is. Do them a few times; stop when they teach nothing new; let the floor take over.

- **Pandiculation.** The CNS's own built-in calibration probe — a no-tension-to-tension sweep through a body segment. It only happens when **a** is already low (you do not pandiculate braced); the floor enables it because the floor is what lets **a** fall. Once pandiculation is unblocked you do not need a foreign probe.
- **The 5 mm glute bridge.** A minimal, near-isometric gluteal engagement — a tiny lift of the pelvis off the floor, millimetres not inches. Teaches the pelvic-floor / gluteal / lumbar chain's resting position relative to the floor; loses its lesson once the chain's resting length is known.
- **Big-toe / little-toe signalling.** Deliberate isolation of the big-toe extension from the little-toe extension (most people cannot at first separate them). Teaches the independence of the peroneal vs tibial motor lines; loses its lesson once the toes can be moved independently. Same family as the Feldenkrais toe-separation lessons.
- **The arm-to-nose probe (the specific instruction).** Keep the upper arm on the ground (the scapular base anchored — contact manifold preserved, no new support demand). Rotate the lower arm really slowly (pronation/supination — cheap, distal, does not load the proximal chain). Then raise the upper arm (now the proximal segment moves against the anchored base). Then put the back of the wrist on the tip of the nose. The last step closes the kinematic loop: floor → scapula → humerus → forearm → wrist → nose → head, giving the CNS a sharp spatial reference to organise the arm's degrees of freedom against. Read mechanically, it is a *calibration datum*, not a stretch — a way of giving the part of you that will not stop doing something trivial to solve, so the part of you that does the tensor analysis can work undisturbed.

The probes are scaffolding for the gate; the floor is the gate. Once the floor is doing its work the probes are retired — which is the test that they were probes and not practice.

### The phenomenology of floor sleep — first-person data the formalism predicts

The do-nothing frame, sustained over a night, produces a sleep phenomenology that differs from bed sleep in ways the tensor formalism predicts but the bed-sleep register has no slot for. Three observations (first-person; recorded as data, not anecdote):

1. **The vertical-datum cycle.** One wakes in the middle of the night and gets vertical — not to do anything, but to give the autonomic CNS a datum on how things are progressing — does a bit of low-stakes idling (doom-scrolling on YouTube), then returns to the floor. Read mechanically: the orthostatic challenge of getting vertical resets the baroreflex and gives the autonomic system a reference input it has been running without. The CNS that has been running d**a**/dt < 0 with no comparison gets a brief upright perturbation to measure against, then returns to the floor where the down-regulation resumes. This is the same logic as the arm-to-nose probe — a tiny perturbation that gives the system something to self-organise against — but at the whole-body scale and for the autonomic rather than the somatic system. The low-stakes idling is the conscious substrate that keeps the body vertical long enough for the datum to register without recruiting the doing-system.
2. **The signature of low, flat **a**.** Floor sleep is not like bed sleep: one notices that time has passed, and that makes sense, but one does not remember actually sleeping, and there is no sleepy-eyed dozy feeling on waking. Bed sleep is a loss of consciousness with a hypnagogic / hypnopompic dozy edge — the signature of an oscillating REM/NREM cycle and a **a**-trajectory that swung up and down. Floor sleep, as described, is closer to the Buddhist lion posture's "mindful and aware" (§1): time passes, you were not unconscious, but you were not doing anything either. The absence of the dozy-woke transition is the phenomenological signature that the **a**-trajectory was low and flat rather than oscillating — a prediction of the formalism that the bed-sleep register has no category for.
3. **The comfy/comfortable distinction lived.** Sleeping on the floor is not and probably will never be comfortable — but it is certainly the place one would choose first for some rest and recuperation; if one wants comfort one has a bath. This is the comfy/comfortable distinction (above) as a lived practice: the bath is the global maximum of somatic comfort (warm, buoyant, every surface supported at skin temperature); the floor is the local maximum of autonomic recuperation. The mattress is sold as both and delivers neither. The observation is the chapter's thesis in miniature.

### The pattern

Every tradition recommends:
1. **A firm surface** (ground, floor, thin mat — never a compliant mattress).
2. **A relaxed state** (mindful, aware, consciously relaxed, inhibiting, passive — never braced).
3. **Sustained duration** (minutes to hours — long enough for creep and down-regulation).
4. **Specific postures** (side-lying, supine — prone is universally discouraged).

The tensor formalism predicts exactly this: the firm surface is the rigid BC (§1); the relaxed state is the **a** down-regulation (§3); the sustained duration is the creep timescale (§6); the specific postures are the geometric configurations that direct the load path (§4, §6). The traditions arrived at the same recommendations through practice and observation; the biomechanics arrives at them through the constitutive law and the equilibrium equation. The two registers describe the same phenomenon in different languages.

### What no tradition explicitly says (but the tensor formalism adds)

- The **active-stress decomposition** (§2): the distinction between **σ**_passive and **σ**_active, and the claim that **a** is a neuro-control variable that the floor allows to drop. The traditions say "relax" and "pacify vata"; the formalism says **a**(t) ↓.
- The **migration** (§4): the proximal-to-distal redistribution of σ_vm as the active load path transfers to the passive load path. No tradition describes this — it is a prediction of the formalism, not an observation of practice.
- The **cramp as local instability** (§5): the distal recruitment threshold. No tradition describes the cramp in these terms.
- The **reference-config remodelling** (§6): the formal claim that ||Δ**C**_0|| reduces under sustained asymmetric loading. The traditions say "ground sleeping gives strength" and "the spine regains its full height"; the formalism says **C**_0 shifts.
- The **GI as a second active-stress structure** (§7): the coupling of the autonomic shift to the MMC and gas transit. The Ayurvedic left-side recommendation gestures at this (positioning for digestion) but does not model it.
- The **tremors as d**a**/dt** signature (§8): the transition-state oscillation. No tradition describes the tremors as re-tuning.
- The **one-state-variable/three-readouts synthesis** (§9): the claim that the skeletal, GI, and tremor phenomena are three expressions of one autonomic trajectory. No tradition makes this synthesis.

### What the formalism cannot do without the traditions

- The **empirical validation** that the practices work. The traditions have centuries of practical experience that firm-floor lying in a relaxed state produces structural improvement. The formalism is a *model*; the traditions are the *evidence* (anecdotal, but extensive) that the model's predictions are lived.
- The **cultural permission** to lie on the floor. The biomechanics field will not produce this. The traditions — Buddhism, Ayurveda, yoga, the floor-sleeping cultures — provide the cultural frame in which floor-lying is normal, not eccentric.
- The **posture specifics**. The lion posture (right side, one foot on the other), the semi-supine (knees up, head supported), the śavāsana (arms away, palms up) — these are *empirically refined* configurations that the traditions arrived at through practice. The formalism can explain why they work but would not have invented them from first principles.

## Open questions — status after this session

1. **The side-lying direction (left vs right) — RESOLVED (alternation, not choice).** The framing "left XOR right" was wrong. Left and right are mirror-image asymmetric BCs; a sustained single-side practice drifts the remodelling in one direction, and alternating nets the drift to zero (the Poynting-vector argument: uncurl in both chiralities to keep the vector pointing right). The prediction — that the practice must alternate — is exactly what the traditions collectively do (Buddhism right, Ayurveda left, Yogaratnakara supine→right→left→any rotates), and what the Samson ape posture data shows (right 31.5% / left 33.2%, idiosyncratic per night). Recorded in `floor-lying-tensor-analysis.md` §6.
2. **The "mindful and aware" instruction — RESOLVED (awareness = gate, autonomic = engine).** Awareness does not lower **a**; the autonomic system does. Awareness is the cortical inhibition that keeps the autonomic engine running by preventing habitual re-recruitment. The deeper refinement: the gate is structural (the floor removes the affordances for doing), not vigilant (the practitioner holding a cushion-gate). Recorded in §7 above, with the phenomenology of doing nothing, the autonomic-reflects-safeness report, and the safe/warm/comfy enabling-conditions specification.
3. **The cultural shift — PARTIALLY RESOLVED (posture yes, pain confounded).** The epidemiology is there if you know what to look for, and it is confounded. The surface predicts *posture* cleanly (Kikusawa 1978 — bed vs futon cohort, bed group more kyphotic / W-shape; corroborated by Ohara bedding-ergonomics W-shape literature); *pain* is downstream of posture and forty other things, and the CUPID Japan data cut against the simple story until the confounds are named. 4. **The Ayurvedic surface typology as a research framework — RESOLVED (extend with three axes).** Use the typology; extend it with (i) compliance-as-a-field, (ii) thermal coupling, (iii) load-dependent nonlinearity. The degenerate cases (memory-foam-on-futon, the heated ondol, the swinging *andolikā*, the charpai weave) are the boundary points that force the new axes. Recorded in §2 above.
5. **The Samson primatology connection — RESOLVED, including the ontogeny thread.** The bait-and-switch has a second axis the bedding industry elides harder than the firmness one: apes sleep side-lying (~65%), prone is rare, the platform is firm and nonuniform (a futon, not a mattress). ### Cultural-shift epidemiology (Japan) — the posture-isolating and the pain-confounded
- **Kikusawa, Y. (1978), "The Influence of Life Style on Physique," *Nippon Eiseigaku Zasshi* (Japanese Journal of Hygiene) 33(2):431–440. DOI 10.1265/jjh.33.431.** Bed vs futon cohort, 108 + 108 Japanese women's-college students, 12+ years each, matched, silhouette-photogrammetry. Bed group: significantly greater upper-body backward lean, greater back curvature (W-shape), more round-back/kyphotic (Staffel 円背型) tendency; futon group more upright. **The cleanest single surface-isolated signal, and elided from the English biomechanics citation graph.** Full text retrieved and translated by the project — see `assets/kikusawa-1978-translated-reference.md` for the translated reference extract (the load-bearing Staffel-classification discussion and angle definitions are in the Japanese body, not the English abstract).
- **Ohara / W-shape bedding-ergonomics literature** (Japanese bedding-ergonomics tradition, e.g. Ohara; Suzuki & Ohgushi 1989, *J. Japan Soc. Nurs. Res.* 12(3)) — the corroborator for Kikusawa; documents the "W-shape" supine posture on soft beds. Suzuki & Ohgushi 1989 also frames the futon as *worse* for sacral/calcaneal pressure (the pressure-ulcer/comfort register — the same property the structural-deviation register calls *good*).
- **Matsudaira, K., Palmer, K. T., Reading, I., Hirai, M., Yoshimura, N. & Coggon, D. (2011), *Occup. Environ. Med.* 68:191–196 (CUPID Japan).** Japanese workers have lower regional pain and far lower musculoskeletal sickness absence than UK counterparts (wrist/hand 7% vs 30%; LBP absence 4% vs >12%). Confounded by floor-sitting, squatting, lower obesity, somatising-tendency and reporting/compensation culture — cuts against the simple "floor good, bed bad" story until the confounds are named.
- **Kawaguchi, M., Matsudaira, K. et al. (2017), *BMC Musculoskelet. Disord.* 18:334 (CUPID Japan follow-up).** Prospective 1-year cohort; past history of LBP the strongest predictor of new disabling LBP; limited support for occupational lifting; possible role of long working hours.
- **Suka, M. & Yoshida, K. (2008), *Environ. Health Prev. Med.* 13(2):109–115.** Snapshot of the LBP burden in Japan (≈11.8M sufferers, ≈947k QALYs lost); not a trend.

### Pregnancy and the third-trimester supine contraindication
- **Stacey, T., Thompson, J. M. D., Mitchell, E. A., Ekeroma, A. J., Zuccollo, J. M. & McCowan, L. M. E. (2011), "Association between maternal sleep practices and risk of late stillbirth: a case-control study," *BMJ* 342:d3403.** The study that opened the field.
- **Cronin, R. S., Li, M., Thompson, J. M. D., et al. (2019), "An individual participant data meta-analysis of maternal going-to-sleep position, interactions with fetal vulnerability, and the risk of late stillbirth," *EClinicalMedicine* 10:49–57.** The consolidation: 5 case-control studies, N=3108; aOR 2.63 (95% CI 1.72–4.04) for supine going-to-sleep and late stillbirth. Left and right lateral equally safe.
- **Humphries, A., Mirjalili, S. A., Tarr, G. P., Thompson, J. M. D. & Stone, P. (2018), *J. Matern. Fetal Neonatal Med.*** MRI: ~85% IVC diameter reduction and ~30% aortic compression in supine late third trimester vs left lateral. The mechanism.
- **Barbosa, A., et al. (2019), *Obstet. Gynecol.* or equivalent MRI study** — 30° left-lateral tilt gives the greatest IVC volume; the left-lateral has the MRI-measured advantage though left/right are epidemiologically equally safe. (Cite the right-lateral vs left-lateral MRI study, PMID 31094791.)
- **Kember, A. J., Anderson, J. L., House, S. C., Reuter, D. G., Goergen, C. J. & Hobson, S. R. (2024), "Impact of maternal posture on fetal physiology in human pregnancy: a narrative review," *Front. Physiol.* 15:1394707.** 242-study narrative review — the bridge reference between obstetric posture literature and floor-lying tradition. Documents the supine–fetal-hypoxia mechanism comprehensively.
- **NICE Guideline NG201 (2021), Evidence review W: "Maternal sleep position during pregnancy."** The UK guideline now advises settle-to-sleep on either side from 28 weeks. The clinical-translation endpoint.
